Week 4 · Noise and Filtering

The filter class is part of the measurement

Video: Filtering a Crash Record to SAE J211 (0:20:03). Slides

Claim

A peak read from a filtered record depends on the filter. Across the table below the peak of this channel varies by a factor of 3.2 as the CFC class falls, while the change in velocity over the same range varies by 0.09%. Which class is correct is set by the bandwidth of the quantity being measured, and SAE J211 recommends a class for each measurement. A peak reported without its class is incomplete.

The filter changes the answer, so the filter is part of the answer. Which one to use is an engineering question with a right answer, and the standard records it.

Four Filter Classes, Two Answers

Smoothing and phase lag covered the trade between noise and peak height, and running the standard's filter forward and backward so that events stay at the time they happened. This page takes up how hard to filter, which for the CFC filter is set by the class.

The crossmember channel from week 3, filtered at four CFC classes and asked for two quantities, which are the peak acceleration and the change in velocity.

Read the two right-hand columns against each other. The peak acceleration falls by a factor of 3.2 across the table. Over the same range Δv changes by 0.09%. Two analysts reporting the peak from different classes will disagree by a factor of three, while two analysts reporting Δv will agree to four figures whatever they chose. The same record therefore answers one of these two questions robustly and the other only once the filter is named. Which is which is a property of the question, and the rest of this page is about how the filter gets chosen.

Why a Low-Pass Filter Shrinks a Peak

A low-pass filter removes the content above its passband and leaves the rest. Shrinking peaks is not its purpose. It is a consequence, and the reason is what a fast peak is made of.

The narrow spike at 10.8 ms is fast by construction: a tall, brief excursion requires high-frequency content, because that is the only thing that can rise and fall again in a millisecond. Remove the content above 60 Hz and you have removed most of what built that spike, so it comes down. A broad, slow feature is made of low-frequency content, and the same filter passes it untouched.

Δv is the integral over the whole record, and integration is itself a smoothing operation, as week 3 showed. The fast components the filter removes contribute almost nothing to the area, because what the filter takes off the top of a spike it puts back into the shoulders either side, and the area survives while the maximum does not. A peak is exposed to the filter because a peak is the fast content, and the filter's whole job is to decide how much fast content belongs in the record.

The 180 and 60 rows show something worse than a reduced number. The time of the peak moves from 10.80 ms to 24.00 ms and then to 53.04 ms. This is easy to mistake for the phase lag of the previous page. These are forward-backward filtered, so there is no lag. The peak has moved to a different feature of the record: the narrow spike at 10.8 ms has been removed entirely, and a broader, later event that was previously smaller is now the largest. The reported peak is describing a different physical moment.

Three Shortcuts That Fail

The class cannot be read off the record, and three tempting shortcuts all fail:

  • The unfiltered trace is not "the truth". It has already been through a 2000 Hz anti-alias filter, without which it could not have been sampled at all.
  • A lighter filter is not automatically more faithful. Some of what it keeps is instrument resonance and mounting vibration, which are the transducer's own behaviour and not the vehicle's.
  • A heavier filter is not automatically safer. It removes real structure along with the artefacts, and cannot distinguish them.

What decides it is the bandwidth of the thing being measured. A peak used to estimate injury needs the bandwidth the body responds to. A peak used to check a mounting bracket needs the bandwidth the bracket responds to. Those are different numbers, and both are knowable. Neither is in the file, because both are facts about the physical system and not about this recording.

The question therefore has an answer. Finding it means knowing what the measurement is for, which is engineering knowledge and not something the analyst is free to choose.

SAE J211 Gives the Class in Advance

SAE J211 gives a class for each measurement in its Table 1, in advance, so that results from different laboratories can be compared. A few of its rows:

MeasurementClass in Table 1A reason (this course's reading)
head acceleration, for HICCFC 1000 the head responds to fast content, and HIC is sensitive to it
thorax spine accelerationCFC 180 the thorax does not respond above a few hundred hertz
vehicle structural acceleration, total vehicle comparisonCFC 60 the motion of the whole vehicle, which is slower than the response of its parts
vehicle structural acceleration, component analysisCFC 600 a single part responds to faster content than the vehicle as a whole
vehicle structural acceleration for velocityCFC 180 before integrating keeps the integral clean without affecting it much either way
belt and barrier loadsCFC 60 load cells ring, and the ringing is not load

Read the third column. Where the quantity is a peak, the class is set by the bandwidth of the thing being measured. Where it is an integral, as in the velocity row, the physics barely cares and the standard fixes a class mainly so that everyone uses the same one. Either way the assignments carry engineering knowledge and are not labels drawn from a hat. A head responds to content up to about a kilohertz and a load cell rings well above the loads it is there to measure, and those two facts are why the two measurements get different classes.

That makes CFC 1000 correct for head acceleration in the way that matters here. Filter a head channel at CFC 60 and you have not made a different defensible choice, you have removed the content HIC is computed from and produced a wrong number. The standard tells you which analyst in the disagreement above is wrong.

What is conventional is the precision of the number, and only that. A committee working from the same physics might have written 900 or 1100, and the standard would work as well. It could not have written 60, because the bandwidth of the head is not a matter of opinion. The convention fixes the last digit. The physics fixes the rest.

Fixing the digit buys the second thing a standard is for. Two laboratories testing the same vehicle produce numbers that can be compared, and a regulatory limit means one thing and not several. Both jobs matter, and the standard does them at once: it records what the bandwidth should be, and it makes everyone use the same value for it.

Reading a number that has a standard behind it

An HIC of 700 is the output of a fully specified procedure: this filter, this window limit, this exponent, applied to a record from a dummy that is itself a standardised object. Every one of those is written down, which is why a number computed under the same procedure means the same thing in Ohio and in Bavaria, and it is more than most engineering measurements offer.

So when you meet a number with a standard behind it, the first question is which procedure produced it. Two HIC values computed under different procedures are different quantities that share a name, and comparing them directly is the commonest way to be wrong about them.

The standard

The standard in question is SAE J211-1, and the decision it fixes is the channel frequency class. The classes are CFC 60, 180, 600 and 1000, and Table 1 of the standard gives the recommended class for each measurement and each use, so two analysts who follow it and agree on the use choose the same class. The standards behind the crash data lists the entries this course needs, and links the text.

Working Without a Standard

Most problems do not come with a committee, and the obligation that remains is the one the standard discharges on your behalf. Make the decision explicit, so that someone else can repeat it or disagree with it.

  1. Choose the filter from the goal of the analysis. Ask what the number is for, then ask what bandwidth that thing responds in, and pass that. This is the same reasoning J211 did on your behalf for crash data, done for your own problem. If you are sizing a bracket, the bracket's response bandwidth sets the filter, not the tidiness of the plot.
  2. Sweep the filter and report what it does. Run the analysis at several classes and look at how much the answer moves, exactly as the table at the top of this page does. A quantity that barely moves is robust to the choice and you can stop worrying. A quantity that moves by a factor of three is one whose filter has to be justified, and the sweep is what tells you which kind you have.
  3. State the choice with the number. "Peak 1284 m/s², CFC 600" is a result. "Peak 1284 m/s²" on its own is not.
  4. Prefer the robust quantity where the question allows. If Δv answers the question, use it. It does not depend on the decision at all.
Check
  1. Why does Δv change by 0.09% across the table while the peak changes by a factor of three?
    Answer

    Because the two quantities are computed by opposite operations, and a filter removes exactly what one of them is made of.

    The peak is a single value at a single instant. Filtering replaces each sample by a weighted average of its neighbours, and the largest sample has, by definition, no neighbours as large as itself, so it is pulled down, and the more aggressively the lower the class. From 1540.4 to 484.8 m/s² is a factor of 3.2.

    Δv is a sum over the whole record. Filtering conserves area almost exactly. What is taken off a peak is put back into its shoulders. From −17.5091 to −17.5251 m/s is 0.09%, so the choice of filter is nearly free if you are reporting a velocity change and decisive if you are reporting a peak, and it is peaks that injury criteria are built from.

  2. The time of the peak moves from 10.80 to 53.04 ms as the filter gets heavier, with no phase lag involved. What is happening?
    Answer

    The peak is not moving. A different feature is becoming the peak.

    The record contains a narrow spike at 10.80 ms and a broader, lower hump around 53 ms. Light filtering leaves the spike the largest value in the record. Heavy filtering averages the spike away. It is narrow, so it loses the most, while the broad hump, being wide compared with the window, barely changes. At some class the hump overtakes what is left of the spike, and the reported time of peak jumps from one to the other.

    The jump is discontinuous, which is the tell. A phase lag is a smooth shift of a few milliseconds. This is 29 ms in one step of the filter class, and 42 ms over two. Any quantity defined by where a maximum occurs can do this, and the reported number gives no sign that the thing being reported has changed identity.

  3. You must report a peak and there is no applicable standard. Write the one sentence you would put in the report.
    Answer

    Something with the number, the processing, and the sensitivity in it:

    "Peak longitudinal acceleration 630 m/s², from the record filtered at CFC 180 forward-and-backward. The value ranges from 485 to 1540 m/s² across CFC 60 to unfiltered, and no standard governs this measurement."

    Three things earn their place. The number, because that is what was asked for. The processing, because without it the number is not reproducible and the reader cannot compare it with anyone else's. The range across defensible choices, because that is the honest width of the claim, and a reader who has it can decide whether the decision matters for their purpose. What a standard buys is that the second and third clauses can be replaced by its name. Without one you write them out.
