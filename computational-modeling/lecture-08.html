Signal, offset and noise

We assume every sample is signal plus offset plus noise.

$$a_i = s_i + b + \varepsilon_i$$

The integral splits into three sums.

$$\Delta v \;=\; \Delta t\!\sum s_i \;+\; \Delta t\!\sum b \;+\; \Delta t\!\sum \varepsilon_i$$

Computational Physical Modeling

How the offset and noise sums grow

The offset sum grows in proportion to $n$.

$$\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} b \;=\; n\,b\,\Delta t \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{b\,T}$$

If the noise is independent, its sum grows as $\sigma\sqrt{n}$.

Dashed: ±1 standard deviation. Our noise is not independent (neighbouring samples correlate at about 0.78), so over short stretches it spreads wider.

$$\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} \varepsilon_i \;\sim\; \sigma\sqrt{n}\,\Delta t \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\sigma\sqrt{T\,\Delta t}}$$

Doubling $n$ doubles the offset error and multiplies the noise error by $\sqrt{2}$.

Measuring offset and noise in a crash record

Before impact we assume the acceleration is zero.

The pre-impact window gives $b$ and $\sigma$.

The window holds the $m$ samples recorded before impact, where the signal is zero.

in the window$a_i = 0 + b + \varepsilon_i$ On the tow line the car is not accelerating, so each sample is the offset plus that sample's noise.
the offset$\hat b = \bar a = \dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{t_i<0} a_i$ The mean keeps $b$, and the noise mostly averages away. What is left of it is about $\sigma/\sqrt{m}$.
the noise$\hat\sigma = \sqrt{\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{t_i<0}\left(a_i - \bar a\right)^2}$ The scatter of the samples about their mean.

The X5 window gives b and σ.

$$m = 625 \text{ samples } (50\text{ ms at } 12\,500\text{ per s}) \qquad \bar a = -0.2135 \qquad \hat\sigma = 2.377$$ $$\frac{\hat\sigma}{|\bar a|} = \frac{2.377}{0.2135} \approx 11 \qquad\qquad \frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{m}} = \frac{2.377}{25} \approx 0.095\ \text{m/s}^2$$

An offset one eleventh the size of the noise overtakes it after 124 samples.

Computational Physical Modeling

Integrating twice for displacement

Each integration adds a power of $T$ to the offset error.

$$\text{velocity: } bT \qquad\qquad \text{displacement: } \textcolor{#8A6508}{\tfrac12 b T^2}$$

Subtracting the mean shrinks $bT$ to the error in $b$.
