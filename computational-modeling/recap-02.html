Reports as Professional Work 3:40

Each assignment this quarter is a report with plots and some text around them, and its shape is up to you (which is why every assignment comes with three example reports). Chris asked you to treat each one as a report you would hand a boss in your first week on the job. That boss will ask for something in a loosely specified way and will not hand you a spec. Nobody gets fired from a class, but it is worth working as if you were confident enough in the report to put your name on it.

That does not mean knowing everything. A junior engineer is not expected to know everything about crash tests (Chris does not either). The senior engineers give structure and guidelines, and they expect you to work within them.

The AI Maxims 5:59

The seven maxims on the AI Use page are framed around AI, but each one applies to working with other people and to organizing your own work too. Chris covered them out of order.

  • Augmented intelligence, not artificial replacement. A tool that completes the homework for you harms your learning in the same way copying from a classmate does. At work, if a colleague does some of the work for you, you still read it before it goes up with your name on it. The same goes for anything that comes from a tool or a search.
  • Prompt as craft. This one is about communication with any person or agent. Engineers communicate technical findings to each other, which means giving a number in the right units with its uncertainty, so the team knows how far to trust it. The reports are practice at technical writing, and the first test of technical writing is usually whether it is correct. With an AI, the more precisely you say what you want, the more likely you are to get it.
  • Iterative refinement and critical evaluation. You read what comes back, ask whether it is what you wanted, and go around again (or edit it yourself). With practice you usually need fewer rounds, because you communicate more precisely the first time and find errors faster.
  • Continuous improvement. "Practice" can mean the rehearsal before a recital or a game. It can also mean a doctor's practice, where the practice is the whole career. Engineering is a practice in that second sense, and your problem solving can keep improving across a career.
  • Attribution and recognition. In engineering the thing you most often cite is a number. Most numbers in your reports this quarter should come from your program (or from the header, read by your program). If you do not know where a number came from, take it out or flag it.

Some labs flagged channels in the original data with a comment that something looked wrong (a large spike, for instance). They left the data in and flagged it, so whoever uses it can decide what to do. That is the same kind of flag you can put in a report.

Student question

Q: Is flagging something also a way to protect yourself? 17:39

A: It is more about honesty. A flag tells whoever reads the report how far you trust that number. With experience you find that almost every number you report is "close to here", and stating your uncertainty becomes a habit.

Voice and Ownership 18:51

Your writing has a voice, in the words you choose, just as your speech does. When something written by someone else is about to carry your name and you think "that's not how I would say it", rewrite it in your own words.

An idea does not have to come from scratch to be yours (making an apple pie from scratch would start with creating the universe). Chris counts an idea as yours when you can justify it and explain it to a classmate, wherever it came from.

What Showed Up in the Assignment 1 Reports 21:59

Chris had looked through some reports (this was not about grading). The slides list the examples with no names.

  • Read your PDF before you submit it. Some reports still had notes from an AI saying to check something before submitting, or the phrase from the example report. Chris reads a paper for publication many times, until he stops making changes. He recommends at least one thorough read.
  • Check your units. A number in the wrong units is still wrong (even when your own program produced it).
  • Look at the strangest feature of each plot. Chris softened the slide's advice here. Ask whether you understand the feature or believe it is normal. If it is a big spike, or the trace drops to zero, and you cannot explain it, flag it in the report.
  • Make the check yourself. If your report says a value should be double checked, do the check and report what you found.
  • Count in and out. Nothing in Assignment 1 said to plot every channel, but a channel that is never mentioned is an omission. If you leave one out, say so.
  • Watch for the points where an AI (or a colleague) stopped and handed the task back to you. You can ask the AI how to check that point, and sometimes it is a hand calculation.
  • Keep claims to what you have evidence for. Nobody asked whether a crash would have been fatal, so leave that out. "Maybe this dummy is loose, and we should check" states your uncertainty. "This dummy is loose" does not.
Student question

Q: Is it enough to say a number came from my program, or from a figure? 29:13

A: Usually. It is still worth practicing how to say it more precisely. There is a point where you have given all the details cleanly, and you will probably develop a feel for when you have reached it.

Good Uses of the Chatbot 31:31

Chris is not reading through everyone's chats, though he has looked at a few as part of his research on the course chatbot. Three things he saw were worth passing on.

  • Asking it to rewrite code with a comment on every line, or to explain the code to a beginner who only knows MATLAB. Code you can explain line by line counts as yours, by the same rule as ideas.
  • Asking a question, and then asking about each piece of the answer you did not understand until you understood the whole thing. Chris once spent six weeks working through a 45-minute video this way, and an AI can make the same process much faster.
  • Answering some of the questions the chatbot asks you. It asks in a Socratic way, hoping you learn something by answering, and your answer also steers its next step.

Pólya's Four Steps 35:12

The course chatbot was built around the framework in George Pólya's How to Solve It. Pólya was a mathematician, and he argued that the same strategy carries over to problems in science and engineering.

  1. Understand the problem. Assignment 1 gave you a zip file and a loosely stated problem, so the first job was turning it into a problem you could work on (opening the folder and looking around is a fine start).
  2. Devise a plan. Chris will not hand you a plan, because there are usually several paths to a solution and you should use the tools that work for you.
  3. Carry out the plan. With a precise enough plan (this algorithm, on this data, printing this output and plotting it this way), an AI can largely write the code for you.
  4. Look back. Did you solve the problem? If not, update the plan and go around again.

Chris wants the quarter's focus on the first two steps, and class time demonstrates them. The chatbot thinks this way too, and it may ask which step you are on. Explaining your understanding of the problem is a good answer. If you already have a plan, say so and give it the plan.

The code does tell you the seat, as the slide for this part says. Its second digit is the position, where 0 is the vehicle, 1 the driver and 3 the front passenger. The descriptions number the dummies instead, so the passenger is P2 with a code starting 13.

Coming This Quarter 44:01

  • Before impact the car is on the tow line, so the acceleration should be flat at zero. Up close it is not, because no instrument is perfect. What you see is mostly noise and an offset, and Week 3 (Bias and noise) covers dealing with them.
  • Whether a big spike is bad depends on what you are measuring. Count its samples. A spike of one sample may be an error of some kind. A spike across three samples is more likely a real feature, though it is still not certain.
  • 56.56 km/h and 15.71 m/s are the same speed in different units.

Why the Head Peaks Later 46:37

A peak is a maximum or a minimum, and finding peaks is one of the main things you do with data. Another common one is looking at wiggles, or oscillations. On the X5 test from the slides, the crossmember (a sensor on the car's structure, under the rear seat) peaks at about −1540 m/s² around 11 ms, and then rings. Ringing is the frame vibrating after the hit, like a struck bell, though much less tuneful. The pelvis peaks at about −510 m/s² at 57 ms and the head at about −470 m/s² at 79 ms.

Most of the difference is probably the seatbelt and airbag working as intended. Without a seatbelt and airbag the body would keep moving forward and hit the dashboard at something like the structure's deceleration. With them, the body slows later, less sharply and with a smaller jerk (the rate of change of acceleration). Evaluating that is one of the main purposes of a crash test.

Assignment 2, the Request 56:15

Alice forwards a thread. Purchasing wants to standardize the whole lab on a cheaper logger that records at 1 kHz, and Alice has to sign off on it, so she wants to know what the lab gives up. That is a loosely stated request, and the class worked through understanding it (Pólya's first step).

What is a logger? The accelerometer is the measuring instrument, like a ruler. The logger is the equipment that records its readings over time into storage. It is fine to think of them as one tool (they are built together). Depending on your pack, your test was recorded at 10, 12.5 or 20 kHz.

Purchasing has its own reasons. The logger is cheaper, and one logger across all the labs means one set of software and licenses. They are asking the crash lab whether the change would hurt its work. A request like that, from another part of the business, is a common situation for an engineer.

On the X5 crossmember, keeping every 12th sample of a 12.5 kHz record gives about 1 kHz, and the deepest dip, the peak of about −1540 m/s², is not in the decimated record at all. That is a bad sign, though whether it matters depends on whether the analysis cares about peaks. Alice's request asks for:

  • A rate. Saying 1 kHz is not good is acceptable, and giving the rate you think is safe is better.
  • What is given up at that rate, as a number (a peak that reads 90% of its true height, for instance).
  • The evidence. A memo to purchasing needs the numbers behind a no.
  • Where your evidence runs out. Each of you has one car, which is a big limitation. Alice could combine the answers from the whole class.

Decimation 64:34

Keeping one sample out of every so many is called decimation. "Filtering" is a different technique, coming in Week 4. The word comes from the Roman army, which punished a unit by killing one soldier in every ten. Here you keep one sample in every twelve and drop the rest.

The class voted on which channel would keep more of its peak at about 1 kHz, the driver's head or the crossmember. The head's peak is long and wide, so it survives. The crossmember's peak is very narrow, with only a few samples on it, so it is likely to be lost.

The Live Demo 67:42

Chris called this slide the whole homework on one slide. The "keep one sample in" slider sets the decimation. Keeping one in three, the head keeps 99.7% of its peak and the crossmember 86.5%. Keeping one in twelve, the head keeps 99.2% and the crossmember 66.4%.

You need a tolerance, which is how much loss you will accept. Chris uses 5% in the slides because scientists have long used it (a convention, so you still need your own reason). You may choose another (10%, say) and justify it in your report. At either tolerance the head passes and the crossmember fails.

The Worst Case 71:49

Decimation has a second setting, the sample you start from. Keeping every second sample, you can keep the even ones or the odd ones, and if the peak falls on one of them you get it and if not you miss it. Slide the starting sample and you can always find a lucky start that keeps the whole peak. Engineers usually assume they will be unlucky, because the logger cannot choose where its samples fall. So you report the worst start. On the X5 crossmember at one in twelve, the worst of the twelve starts keeps 50.6% of the peak.

Chris sketched the program. Set the decimation (12, or a range of values in a loop), loop over every starting sample, and take the minimum.

Student question

Q: The demo only shows two channels. Is that all we need? 75:55

A: No, run it on all your channels. The two were chosen because one passes and one fails, and you will probably find both kinds in your data. Your crossmember may ring less than the X5's. Alice, with everyone's results, would apply the worst case again and take the worst car.

Samples Across a Peak 77:16

Whether decimation keeps a peak depends on how many samples fall across it. Across the head's peak (above half its height) there are 459 samples, so almost any sample you keep is on the peak. Across the crossmember's there are 7, and keeping one in twelve leaves fewer than one. Divide the count by the decimation, and if the result is less than about two, you are probably decimating too much.

Peaks and oscillations are features of a signal, and features can be fast or slow. A slow logger usually keeps slow features (like the head's peak) and loses fast ones (like the crossmember's).

Lecture correction

The axes of the figure that plots every channel were described the other way around in class. The vertical axis is the worst share of the peak kept at about 1 kHz (one Elantra channel keeps less than 40%). The horizontal axis is the number of samples across the peak at that rate. The vertical number is the one your program computes. The figure shows the correlation and you do not need to reproduce it.

Your program first needs each channel's peak. That is probably a minimum, since the accelerations are mostly negative. With the peak in hand you can compute the share kept after decimating.

Ringing 84:26

The crossmember's signal is mostly the metal frame vibrating after the impact, which is part of what the sensor is there to measure. Three things can ring.

  • The structure, which is the ringing you want.
  • The mount that holds the accelerometer on the crossmember. It is made very stiff for this reason.
  • The accelerometer itself, roughly a small weight between two springs. It is designed to ring far faster than anything it measures (around 40 kHz, Chris said, as a figure he found and would not vouch for), so it does not show up in this data.

As a result the record holds the structure's ringing and the mount's together, and without the material properties it is hard to say which oscillation comes from which.

The class voted on what the ringing would look like at about 1 kHz (the same shape with fewer dots, smoother, or a different and slower wiggle). Chris thinks the answer is a mix of the last two. The fast wiggles at the top are gone, and three or four of them become one slow wiggle. If the ringing is what you care about, 1 kHz loses it. To see a cycle at all you want to sample at least twice as fast as it, and that is a second way you could judge the logger.

Why a Slower Logger 91:35

The cost of a slower logger to the crash lab is the engineering cost. The other side is why anyone would want one. A crash test is short (about 0.36 s), so even 190 channels at 12.5 kHz come to only about 3 MB, and your whole data pack was a few megabytes at most. An eight-hour fatigue test recording at 10 kHz produces about 57 GB, and almost certainly does not need it, so at 1 kHz it would be about 6 GB (and probably slower would still be enough). Every logger has a top rate and every lab has limited storage, so choosing a sample rate is usually a trade between detail and file size.

There is also a risk on the other side. If the lab installs a logger that records too little and finds out after the test, the cost is a car. So it is usually safer to record more than you need and decimate later.

The tolerance slide shows that the 5% line is arbitrary 95:20. Move it and different channels pass, and a different car gives different outcomes. State your tolerance in your report, and why you picked it.

The Same Choice Elsewhere 96:38

Sampling is central to signal processing, and audio is a familiar case. Most people hear up to about 20 kHz, and you want to sample at least twice as fast as what you need to capture, so CDs sample at 44.1 kHz. Sample at half that rate and an 18 kHz tone is recorded wrongly, as a slower one. That is aliasing (in this week's notes and videos), where fast signals look slow because of how they were sampled.

The second example, from the notes, is a design sweep 98:45. Picture a pump bolted to a frame and run at different speeds. Like a child on a swing pushed at just the right moment, the frame's motion is amplified when the pump runs near the frame's natural frequency, and too much amplification could tear it loose. A sweep in steps of 2 Hz misses the true peak and says the design is fine. When two points straddle a possible peak, test more points between them.

The spacing between evaluations comes from one of two places 102:04. In a measurement the instrument sets it, and in a sweep you choose it and can always halve it.

Where Your Evidence Runs Out 102:49

Much of the class was about justifying a number and stating your uncertainty about it. For Assignment 2, your evidence runs out in at least two places that belong in your report.

  • You have one car.
  • You are decimating data that was already sampled, so your record may already have missed part of a peak (perhaps the test should have been recorded at 20 kHz).

A real logger usually filters its signal before it records it. That means your data has already been filtered in ways you cannot undo, and a new logger would filter differently. You do not need this in your report, since filtering is Week 4.

If your test was recorded at 10 or 20 kHz, 1 kHz is every 10th or 20th sample. At 12.5 kHz there is no 12.5th sample, so pick every 12th or every 13th (or try both) and say which 104:32. Alternating 12 and 13 is possible, though Chris does not recommend it as a normal engineering practice.
