Week 6 · Least Squares Fitting

Solving the normal equations

Claim

Least squares on a model linear in its parameters reduces to a square system of linear equations, solved by elimination in a fixed number of operations with no iteration and no convergence to check. How many digits of the answer survive is governed by the conditioning of that system and not by the quality of the data, and forming the normal equations squares the condition number of the problem they came from. The fitted values are far more robust to this than the coefficients, which are what carry the physical meaning. The solve reports nothing when it fails.

The closed form is a matrix solve. It always returns something and it usually does not warn you, so the curve it draws may be right long after the numbers it is built from have stopped meaning anything.

Deriving the Normal Equations

Take the straight line $y = c_0 + c_1x$ and $n$ measurements. The sum of squared residuals is

$$S(c_0, c_1) \;=\; \sum_i \left(y_i - c_0 - c_1 x_i\right)^2$$

At the minimum both partial derivatives vanish. Differentiating and dividing by $-2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_i (y_i - c_0 - c_1 x_i) &= 0\\ \sum_i x_i(y_i - c_0 - c_1 x_i) &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

which rearranges into two equations in two unknowns:

$$\begin{bmatrix} n & \sum x_i \\[2pt] \sum x_i & \sum x_i^2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c_0 \\[2pt] c_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sum y_i \\[2pt] \sum x_i y_i \end{bmatrix}$$

These are the normal equations, and every entry in them is a sum over the data, computed once. The unknowns appear linearly, which is the property the previous page was about, and it is what makes this a linear system and not a search.

For $p$ parameters the matrix is $p \times p$ and the pattern is the same, with entry $(i,j)$ equal to $\sum_k f_i(x_k)f_j(x_k)$, where the $f$ are whatever functions of $x$ the model multiplies its parameters by. A parabola gives $1, x, x^2$, and a Fourier model gives sines and cosines. That is, the model form changes the entries of the matrix and nothing else about the procedure.

Elimination with Pivoting

Optional, open to read

The steps below are elimination in the version that works on a computer, which differs from the one usually taught by hand.

  1. Choose a pivot. In column $k$, find the row at or below $k$ with the largest magnitude in that column, and swap it up. This is partial pivoting.
  2. Eliminate. Subtract multiples of the pivot row from the others so column $k$ becomes zero everywhere else.
  3. Read off. After the last column the matrix is diagonal and each unknown is one division.

The cost is about $\tfrac{2}{3}p^3$ operations, independent of $n$, because the data enter only when the sums are formed. Twelve parameters is a thousand operations, which is why this is done once at the end and not repeatedly inside a search (and why it is usually not worth optimising).

Pivoting

Without pivoting the elimination divides by whatever happens to be on the diagonal. If that entry is zero the method stops. If it is merely small, the multipliers are large, and the subtraction that follows cancels the leading digits of every row it touches. Pivoting costs a comparison per column and removes this class of failure, which is why a library routine does it by default.

Limits of the Closed Form

Optional, open to read

Page 15 lists what a linear-in-parameters model buys: no starting guess, no iteration, no convergence to check, and the global minimum. All of that is true, though all of it is about the search. The solve that follows the search has a failure mode of its own. A matrix is ill-conditioned when small changes in its entries produce large changes in the solution. The condition number $\kappa$ measures it, and a rule of thumb follows from it.

You lose about $\log_{10}\kappa$ of your sixteen significant digits. Polynomial fitting produces ill-conditioned normal equations quickly, because $1, x, x^2, x^3$ look increasingly alike over a fixed interval, so the columns of the matrix become nearly parallel and $\kappa$ becomes large.

Twenty-one points evenly spaced over $[0, 1]$. By degree nine only about three of the sixteen digits are left, and by degree five half of them have already gone.

Conditioning and the Units of $x$

Forming the normal equations squares the conditioning. The least-squares problem is stated by a tall matrix $A$ whose rows are the model functions evaluated at each point, and multiplying by $A^{\!\top}$ to make it square costs $\kappa(A^{\!\top}\!A) = \kappa(A)^2$, exactly. At degree six the tall problem has $\kappa(A) = 1.8\times10^4$ and the normal equations have $3.4\times10^8$, which is four digits thrown away by a step taken for convenience. Methods that avoid forming $A^{\!\top}\!A$, such as the QR factorisation, keep $\kappa(A)$ and lose half as many digits, which is why a library's least-squares routine uses one of them.

The units of $x$ change the answer. The same degree-5 fit on twenty-one points, differing only in how $x$ is expressed:

$x$ ascondition numberdigits lost
0 to 100, as measured2.0×10²⁰all 16
0 to 11.1×10⁷7.0
−1 to 1, centred1.5×10³3.2

Nothing about the physics or the data has changed. Shifting $x$ to sit around zero and scaling it to order one turns an unsolvable system into a comfortable one, and it costs two lines. It is a good idea to make it the first step of any polynomial fit, though it is rarely mentioned.

Errors in the Curve and in the Coefficients

Optional, open to read

A condition number of 10¹² sounds like it should ruin everything, and the demo below shows the fitted curve surviving it comfortably. Both facts are true, because they are about different things. These are the same normal equations solved twice, once in ordinary double precision, once in exact rational arithmetic, which has no rounding error at all. The difference between them is the damage done by the arithmetic alone.

degreeworst error in a coefficient worst error in the fitted curve
32×10⁻¹³3×10⁻¹⁴ V
64×10⁻⁹3×10⁻¹² V
91×10⁻³1×10⁻⁷ V

At degree nine the coefficients are wrong in the fourth significant figure while the curve they produce is right to a ten-millionth of a volt. The errors are large in the directions the data barely constrains and those directions almost cancel when the polynomial is evaluated back inside the range the data covers. Which of those matters depends on what the fit was for.

  • Reading values off the curve, inside the data. Robust. Interpolating a calibration at 34 °C survives conditioning that has destroyed the coefficients.
  • Reporting or interpreting a coefficient. Fragile, and this is usually the point, because page 13 says the parameters are what the model means. A fitted gravity, a decay constant and a stiffness are all coefficients, so they are the first to lose digits.
  • Evaluating outside the data. Worst of all, because extrapolation is where the near-cancelling directions stop cancelling, and page 32 shows what distance does to an error in a coefficient.

Both Solves on One Record

Optional, open to read

The demo runs the fit twice: once on temperature in degrees as recorded, once with $x$ shifted and scaled. Same data, same normal equations, same elimination.

Raise the degree and watch the two answers separate. They agree to better than a microvolt at degree nine, differ by 7 mV at degree twelve and 23 mV at degree fourteen, and at degree twenty (twenty-one parameters for twenty-one points) the two curves differ by 6.98 V somewhere in the range, on a signal that spans about two volts.

Degree twenty is the sharpest case. With as many parameters as points the fit is exactly determined and must pass through every point, so the residual is required to be zero. The scaled solve reports 0.95 mV and the raw solve 1.76 mV. Both are small though neither is zero, and the difference between them comes from the arithmetic alone.

While this happens there is no warning, no error and no growing residual. The elimination performs the same operations it always performs and returns a vector of coefficients, and every check available inside the calculation passes.

Comparison with root finding

A root finder returns a value satisfying its own stopping test, which does not make it the root you wanted. A linear solve returns the vector its elimination produced, which does not make it the solution of your system. In both cases the routine is correct, the internal check passes, and the thing that would catch the error has to come from outside, a domain, a plausibility, a second method.

Reportable Digits

  1. Centre and scale $x$. Two lines, and worth several digits.
  2. Use the library's least-squares routine, not your own normal equations. It will factorise and not square, and it will usually report a condition number or a rank if you ask.
  3. Keep the degree low. The conditioning problem is often a symptom of asking for more parameters than the data supports, which page 34 shows is also a modelling mistake.
  4. Check by substitution. Put the coefficients back and compute $A^{\!\top}\!Ac - A^{\!\top}\!y$. It is cheap, and a large result is proof of trouble, though a small one is not proof of its absence, for the reason page 18 gives.
Checks on conditioning, optional
Check
  1. A fit's normal equations have a condition number of 10⁹. How many significant digits of the coefficients do you trust, and what would you do before reporting them?
    Answer

    About seven. A double carries roughly sixteen significant digits and log₁₀(10⁹) = 9 of them are gone, leaving seven (an optimistic reading, since the rule is only approximate). Before reporting anything, three things, in order of effort:

    1. Centre and scale x. Two lines. On the example above it moved the condition number by seventeen orders of magnitude, and it may remove the problem.
    2. Solve it again by a method that does not form A⊤A, the library's least-squares routine, which factorises instead. If the coefficients agree to the digits you intend to print, print them.
    3. Ask whether you need that many parameters. A condition number of 10⁹ on a polynomial fit usually means the degree is higher than the data supports, which page 34 shows is a modelling problem before it is an arithmetic one.

    Report the digits you have and not the digits the computer printed (here, seven of the sixteen returned).

  2. Why does centring $x$ about its mean help, when shifting the data cannot change the underlying relationship?
    Answer

    Centring leaves the relationship alone and changes the basis it is expressed in, and conditioning is a property of the basis.

    Over 0 to 100, the functions 1, x, x², x³ are all large, all positive, and all increasing together. As vectors sampled at the data points they are nearly parallel, and their entries already differ by a factor of a million at x³. At degree five the entries of the normal equations span twenty orders of magnitude, which is the 10²⁰ in the table. Centred on zero and scaled to ±1, the even powers stay positive while the odd ones change sign halfway, the columns become far closer to orthogonal, and every entry is of order one. The fitted curve is identical either way, the same polynomial, written differently. What changes is how much cancellation the elimination has to do to find it, and so how many digits survive. In general, a well-conditioned problem can be posed in an ill-conditioned way, and the fix is usually to change how it is written and not how it is solved. Fitting in an orthogonal polynomial basis instead of powers is the same idea taken further.

  3. You solve the same least-squares problem with your own normal equations and with a library routine, and the coefficients differ in the fourth digit while both fits have the same residual. Which do you trust, and why can the residual not settle it?
    Answer

    The library routine. It almost certainly factorises the tall matrix and does not form A⊤A, so it works at κ(A) where your normal equations work at κ(A)², twice as many digits, from the same data.

    The residual cannot settle it because the residual is nearly blind to this error. Ill-conditioning puts the error into the directions the data constrains least, and those are the directions that barely move the fitted curve over the range the points cover. The table above measures it. At degree nine the coefficients are wrong in the fourth figure while the curve is right to 10−7 V. So two coefficient sets can differ in the fourth digit and produce residuals that agree to every digit printed. Both fits pass through the data equally well. They disagree about what the data means, and the residual does not measure that. If it matters, a smaller residual will not settle it. The check is an independent solve, meaning a different method, or the same one after centring, and agreement between them to the digits you intend to use.
