Residual and error

Claim

The residual is the gap between a model and the data it was fitted to. The error is the gap between the model and the truth. They are computed from different things. The residual comes from data you have, and the error from a truth you usually do not, and a small residual does not imply a small error.

How close the curve came to your points is not how close it came to being right. Fitting minimises the first of those, and nothing has minimised the second.

The residual is small because an algorithm was told to make it small. A small residual is easy to read as evidence about the truth, though what it reports is that the algorithm did its job.

Residual and Error on 140 Shots

140 real three-point attempts. Each one gets a parabola fitted to its first few frames after release, extrapolated to the plane of the hoop at 3.048 m, and compared against the answer. Every arc has been screened for free flight, so what follows is unlikely to be a tracking artefact. One point per shot: how well the fit matched its own frames, against how far it landed.

The correlation is essentially zero. At ten frames it is −0.003 across 140 shots, so knowing how closely a fit passed through the points it was given tells you almost nothing about how far its prediction will land from the answer.

The demo marks the two extremes at ten frames. The tightest fit in the whole set, residual 0.53 cm, a sixth of the tracker's own noise, lands 5 cm from the answer. The loosest fit, residual 4.88 cm, nine times worse by the only measure available at the time, lands 7 cm from the answer. Their errors are two centimetres apart, on fits that look like different species. Elsewhere in the same cloud, a fit of 0.81 cm, the fourth tightest of the 140, lands 186 cm out.

The median residual rises from 1.27 to 2.06 cm as more frames are fitted, while the median error falls from 113 to 13 cm. More data makes the fit look worse and the prediction better, because a short window lets the parabola bend to the noise. The number that improves is the error, which on real data nobody can see.

Sources of the Gap

Three mechanisms, and they compound:

The fit is judged where the data are. Least squares minimises the residual at the ten frames and says nothing about $t = 1.07$ s, which in the first three rows is beyond all of them. A curve constrained at one end is free at the other, and the residual is computed entirely at the constrained end, which is why it can be small while the free end wanders.

Small parameter changes become large predictions far away. The fitted curvature is uncertain by a little. Multiplied by the square of a distance about three times the observed span, a little becomes a lot. That amplification is the subject of week 9.

The residual has been made small on purpose. Adding parameters drives it toward zero regardless of whether the model is right, and page 13's demo shows the residual reaching 0.00005 at degree 9 while the error against the truth climbs back to 0.442, twenty-five times its value at degree 2.

The Uses of the Residual

The residual answers a narrower question, and answers it well. What it can answer:

  • Is the model form wrong? Structure in the residuals, a run of positives then a run of negatives, usually means the form cannot follow the data, whatever its size. That is week 10.
  • Is a point bad? One residual far larger than the rest points at the data and not at the model.
  • How noisy is the measurement? With a correct form, the residual scatter estimates the noise. That is a measurement of the instrument, not of the prediction error.

Residual Versus Prediction Error

The residual is one number about the fit, and what you take off the fit can behave very differently. Here are polynomial fits to a measured crash pulse, each asked for three quantities. Polynomial fits over the 150 ms pulse, measured against the record on ten tests:

A fit whose residual is one seventh of the peak (a curve that visibly misses the data, obvious on the plot) reproduces the integral to four decimal places and gets the peak wrong by a factor of five.

The residual barely moved down that row, and what changed the accuracy was what you asked the fit for.

The ordering follows how much integration the quantity involves. A least-squares residual is near zero-mean over the fitted window, so integrating cancels it, which is the argument page 8 makes about noise, arriving here about fit error instead. The peak is a single point, nothing cancels, and the fit's inability to turn sharply is most of the error. HIC sits between, being an integral over a short window, and improves with degree because that window is short enough for the cancellation to be only partial. Thus the question to ask of a fit is whether the quantity you want is an averaging one, and the size of the residual does not answer it. Integrals of a fit tend to be robust. Peaks, slopes, extrema and endpoints are not, and neither is anything evaluated outside the fitted range, page 32.

Estimating Prediction Error

To estimate prediction error you need something the fitted data cannot supply:

  1. Held-out data. Fit on part, predict the rest, measure. The only direct method.
  2. Resampling. Perturb the inputs within their known noise, refit, and see how far the prediction moves. Week 9.
  3. A known case. Run the whole pipeline where the answer is known, and count how often the interval contains it. Also week 9, and the one that checks the other two.

All three share a property the residual lacks, which is that each one confronts the model with something it was not fitted to.

Two Models Compared on Held-Out Frames

Holding data back is also how two models are compared. Consider the parabola for a shot's height, fitted two ways, with $g$ as a third parameter or with $g$ held at 9.81 m/s² by moving $\tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ to the data side, as page 13 does for the velocities. Each shot's free flight, found by the rules on page 13, is split in half. Both models are fitted to the first half of each shot, and each is scored by the RMS error of its predictions on the second half, which neither fit has seen. The example pack has 24 shots. Shot 325 is left out, so the comparison runs on 23. From its apex at frame 25 its height falls by a fixed amount at each of the next 19 frames (0.016 m ten times, then 0.146 m nine times), two straight runs that are likely the tracker filling a gap.

The fitted model matches the frames it was given at least as closely on every shot, as it must, since the held model is the same parabola with one coefficient fixed. On the frames it was not given it does worse on 14 of the 23 shots. Fourteen of 23 is a majority but not a large one, and the clearer difference is in the bad shots. The worst second-half error is 35.7 cm with gravity fitted and 11.8 cm with it held, likely because a fitted gravity can absorb the noise in half a flight and then carry the wrong curvature forward. On this pack, then, the fitted model matched the first half more closely on every shot, and the held model predicted the second half better on 14 of 23.

In code the comparison is a loop over the shots. Here free_flight returns t and z from the release frame up to, but not including, the first frame within 1 m of the hoop. fit_free fits $a + bt + ct^2$ to $z$, and fit_held fits $a + bt$ to $z + \tfrac{1}{2}(9.81)t^2$ and returns $c = -9.81/2$ with it, both by least squares. rms evaluates the parabola at the given times and returns the RMS of its difference from the heights, in centimetres.

out_free, out_held = [], []
for s in train['shots']:
    if s['id'] == 325:
        continue
    t, z = free_flight(s)
    half = len(t) // 2
    c_free = fit_free(t[:half], z[:half])
    c_held = fit_held(t[:half], z[:half])
    out_free.append(rms(c_free, t[half:], z[half:]))
    out_held.append(rms(c_held, t[half:], z[half:]))
Check
  1. A colleague reports "the fit is good, residuals under 1%". What have they established, and what have they not?
    Answer

    They have established that the model reproduces the data it was fitted to, to within 1%. That is a statement about the fitting, and it is worth having only as a check that the fit converged and that nothing is badly broken. They have not established anything about accuracy, meaning the error at any point outside the data, whether the model form is right, or whether the parameters mean what their names suggest. The demo on this page measures the relationship across 140 real shots and finds a correlation of −0.003, so on this evidence the residual carries no usable information about the error.

    The misleading word is "good". It attaches a judgement of quality to a number that only describes the fit's relationship with its own inputs. A useful reply is to ask what the model is for, and then what the error is at that point, which almost always requires holding data back.

  2. Adding a parameter reduces your residual by 30%. Name two different situations that would produce that, and say how you would tell them apart.
    Answer

    The parameter captures real structure the model was missing, drag in a trajectory, a second time constant in a decay. The residual falls because the model is closer to the truth, and prediction improves with it.

    The parameter is fitting noise. An extra degree of freedom cannot increase the residual on the data it was fitted to. That is guaranteed, and it happens whether or not the parameter corresponds to anything. Prediction usually gets worse.

    Tell them apart by holding data back. Fit both versions on part of the data and measure the error on the part neither saw. The real parameter improves the held-out error. The spurious one degrades it. That is the whole of page 34, and it is the most reliable test that separates these two cases.

    A cheaper hint beforehand is to look at where the residual fell. Structure removed from one region is a sign of the first case. A uniform shrink everywhere is a sign of the second.

  3. Under what condition is the residual scatter a fair estimate of the noise, and why is that still not the prediction error?
    Answer

    It is fair when the model form is correct, so that what is left over is noise and not unmodelled signal, and when there are many more points than parameters. With n points and p parameters the fit absorbs some of the noise into the parameters, and the residual scatter underestimates σ by a factor of about √((n − p)/n). At ten points and three parameters that is 16% low, and at four points and three parameters it is nearly useless.

    Even when it is a fair estimate of σ, it is not the prediction error, because prediction error has two more parts. The parameters are uncertain, and that uncertainty is carried to the prediction point and amplified by the distance extrapolated, page 32. In addition, any model-form error is added on top, and a residual computed under the same wrong form may not show it. That is, σ describes the instrument, while prediction error describes your whole pipeline at a place it has never been.
