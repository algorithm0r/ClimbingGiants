Example reports

Example reports · Assignment 2

These are three reports for Assignment 2, Sample Rate and Peak Loss, written in three different styles. All three use the example pack (crash test 9793, which no phrase assigns), so nothing here answers anybody's assignment. Each student builds the cheaper logger's record from the one they have and reads the peaks again. They agree on what the record shows, and they recommend different rates because each chose a different standard for good enough. Please see the Example reports page for what every report needs and who it is written for. A fourth report, written by Claude Opus 5.5 and edited in class, is at the end of this page.

Each report is followed by a short note on what it does well. The purpose of the notes is to show that a choice the assignment leaves open is part of the answer. Stating the choice is what makes the answer defensible. Here the choices move the recommended rate from 5 kHz to 20 kHz (all three reject the proposed 1 kHz).

Things to watch for

Test 9793 is sampled at 20 kHz, and most tests in the course are sampled at 10 kHz (some at 12.5 kHz). If yours is one of those there are fewer slower rates below it to try, and the rate a report recommends here may not apply to your test. It is a good idea to start from your own sample rate and work down from it.

To: Alice

Re: The 1 kHz logger from purchasing

Data phrase: example pack (test 9793)

I recommend 5 kHz (5,000 samples a second) as the lowest rate we accept. On our test, the proposed 1 kHz logger could read the dummy's peaks up to 7.4% low (the driver's chest, 54.6 g read as 50.5 g), and the peak on the car's structure up to 64.6% low (195.7 g read as 69.4 g). Our present logger records at 20 kHz, one sample every 0.05 ms, which is the rate stored in the test file.

The evidence is our own test (crash test 9793, a 2017 Hyundai Elantra driven into a rigid barrier at 56.15 km/h). A 1 kHz logger takes one sample for every 20 of ours, so I made its record by keeping every 20th sample of ours and reading the peak again. Its first sample could fall on any of our first 20, so I tried all 20 starts and kept the lowest peak (the worst case). I did the same at 10, 5, 2 and 0.5 kHz. The figure shows the worst case for four dummy measurements and for the left rear seat crossmember, a sensor on the car's structure. Peaks are in g, where one g is 9.81 m/s².

I counted a rate as acceptable if no dummy peak reads more than 5% low at the worst start. 5 kHz is the lowest rate on the list that passes, with the driver's chest keeping 95.8% of its peak. 2 kHz misses narrowly (the chest keeps 94.4%).

Three things need your attention before you sign.

  • The structure loses far more than the dummy. The crossmember keeps 83.5% of its peak at 10 kHz and 69.2% at 5 kHz. If the lab reports structure peaks, no rate below our present 20 kHz passes on this evidence.
  • Where the samples fall matters as much as the rate. At 1 kHz the crossmember reads anything from 69.4 to 195.7 g, depending only on which sample the logger starts on. One test at 1 kHz would not tell you which of those you had.
  • Where my evidence runs out. This is one test of one car. A real 1 kHz logger probably filters its signal before sampling, and my thinned copy does not, so the real logger may read differently (I have not checked by how much). Our own record is also a sampled record, so its peaks may already be a little below the true ones.

Assumptions. I set the standard on the dummy, since it stands in for the people in the car. I used the four dummy channels along the direction of travel (X). The driver's head also has sideways and vertical channels. They peak at only 7.3 and 22.3 g, against 43.2 g in X, so I left them out of the standard, but they lose more: at 5 kHz they keep 68.4% and 87.3%. If those channels matter to the lab, 5 kHz is not enough for them. The 5% standard and the list of rates (the usual 1, 2, 5 steps) are my choices. The chest displacement is left out, since it is not an acceleration.

Program. a2_logger_check.py (attached) builds the slower records, prints every worst-case peak and draws the figure.

A Technical Report

Example 2 · Technical report

Data phrase: example pack (test 9793)

Summary

The lab should keep its present rate of 20 kHz (20,000 samples a second). On test 9793, no slower rate keeps 95% of every channel's peak wherever the slower logger's samples happen to fall. At the proposed 1 kHz, the crossmember sensor on the car's structure could read as little as 35.4% of its peak (69.37 of 195.70 g), and the driver's chest as little as 92.6%. The first rate to fail is 10 kHz, on the crossmember and on the sideways channel of the driver's head.

Method

A Python program, a2_peak_loss.py (listed in the appendix and submitted with this report), makes each slower record from the 20 kHz one. A logger running at 1/k of our rate takes one sample for every k of ours, so the program keeps every k-th sample (this is called decimation). It does this for every whole number k from 1 to 100 that divides 20,000 exactly, which gives 14 rates from 20 kHz down to 200 Hz.

The slower logger's first sample could line up with any of our first k samples, and nothing in the record says which. The program therefore tries all k starts and keeps both the lowest peak (the worst start) and the highest (the best start). A rate passes if every channel keeps at least 95% of its 20 kHz peak at the worst start. The worst start is used because the lab cannot choose where its samples fall. Every channel is included because the lab records all of them, and this report cannot say which ones its readers will use.

Figure 2 shows why the crossmember is so sensitive. Its peak is a single sharp swing, much shorter than the 1 ms between two samples of the proposed logger. Errors and Anomalies

Chest displacement units. The header gives meters, but the values are micrometers (found in assignment 1). The program shows the channel in millimeters. Its percentages do not depend on the units.

A slower rate can read higher than a faster one. At the worst start, the head's Y channel keeps 76.2% at 4000 Hz and 68.4% at 5000 Hz, and the chest keeps 92.6% at 1000 Hz and 90.2% at 1250 Hz. This is expected and is not a fault in the program. The samples kept at one rate are only a subset of those kept at a faster rate when one rate divides the other, so the curves in Figure 1 do not have to fall steadily.

The head's Y channel. It peaks at only 7.33 g, at 29.95 ms, well before the other dummy channels (65.90 to 73.75 ms). A small, brief peak is easy to miss at a lower rate, which is probably why it fails at 10 kHz along with the crossmember. I have not checked whether that early peak is real motion of the head or noise.

The channel named vehicle_x. Its header describes a sensor on the left rear seat crossmember, so this report uses that description. It is one point on the structure and not the whole car.

Where the Evidence Runs Out

  • This is one test of one car. A test with a sharper or a broader peak would lose a different amount at the same rate.
  • Keeping every k-th sample is a model of the cheaper logger, and no record from it exists. A real logger probably has its own analog filter ahead of sampling, set for its slower rate. That filter would change these numbers, probably by lowering the sharpest peaks further, although I have not checked this.
  • The 20 kHz record is itself sampled and filtered (prefilter 4000 Hz). Its peaks are lower bounds on the true peaks, so every percentage here is measured against a reference that may already be low.

Assumptions

  • Apart from its own filter, the slower logger would record the same values as ours at the samples it keeps.
  • Every starting sample is equally possible, so the worst start is a case the lab could actually get.
  • 95% of the peak is my threshold, applied to every channel equally, including small ones like the head's Y channel.
  • Impact is at time zero, as the file declares, and the chest displacement is in micrometers.

Appendix. Program

"""Assignment 2: peak loss at lower sample rates, every channel of test 9793.

Run this from the unzipped pack folder. A record at a lower rate is made from
the 20 kHz one by keeping every k-th sample. Which sample the slower logger
starts on is not known, so every start from 0 to k-1 is tried and the lowest
peak (the worst start) and highest peak (the best start) are both kept.

Prints the 20 kHz peak of each channel, the worst-start peak at each rate, and
the lowest rate at which every channel keeps 95% of its peak. Saves
fraction_vs_rate.svg (Figure 1) and crossmember_1khz.svg (Figure 2).
"""
import json

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = 9.81                 # m/s^2 in one g
KEEP = 0.95              # a rate passes if every channel keeps 95% of its peak
PROPOSED = 1000          # Hz, the logger purchasing wants

with open('crash/09793.json') as f:
    test = json.load(f)
channels = test['channels']

rate_now = round(1 / channels['head_x']['dt'])
# every whole-number step down from 20 kHz, to 200 Hz
factors = [k for k in range(1, 101) if rate_now % k == 0]
rates = [rate_now // k for k in factors]


def values(key):
    """The channel in g, or in mm for the chest displacement.
    Its header says m, but the values are micrometers (see Errors)."""
    ch = channels[key]
    if key == 'chest_d':
        return [v / 1000 for v in ch['y']], 'mm'
    return [v / G for v in ch['y']], 'g'


def peak(y):
    return max(abs(v) for v in y)


def peaks_by_start(y, k):
    """Peak of the record that keeps every k-th sample, for each start 0..k-1."""
    return [peak(y[start::k]) for start in range(k)]


v, info = test['vehicle'], test['test']
print(f"Test {test['testNo']}: {v['year']} {v['make']} {v['model']}, "
      f"{info['config']} at {info['speed_kph']} km/h")
print("samples, spacing (s) and prefilter (Hz) of each channel:",
      {(ch['n'], ch['dt'], ch['prefilter_hz']) for ch in channels.values()})
print(f"recorded rate {rate_now} Hz, {len(rates)} rates tried, {rates[0]} to {rates[-1]} Hz")
print()

# the 20 kHz peak of every channel, and when it happens
print(f"{'key':12} {'description':34} {'peak':>8} {'unit':4} {'at (ms)':>8}")
for key, ch in channels.items():
    y, unit = values(key)
    i = max(range(len(y)), key=lambda j: abs(y[j]))
    t_ms = 1000 * (ch['t0'] + i * ch['dt'])
    print(f"{key:12} {ch['comment']:34} {peak(y):8.2f} {unit:4} {t_ms:8.2f}")

# worst-start peak at each rate, as a fraction of the 20 kHz peak
worst = {}
for key in channels:
    y, unit = values(key)
    full = peak(y)
    worst[key] = [min(peaks_by_start(y, k)) / full for k in factors]

print(f"\nworst-start peak as % of the 20 kHz peak")
print(f"{'rate (Hz)':>9} " + ' '.join(f"{key:>11}" for key in channels))
for i, r in enumerate(rates):
    print(f"{r:9d} " + ' '.join(f"{100 * worst[key][i]:11.1f}" for key in channels))

# the lowest rate that every channel passes, with every faster rate passing too
lowest = rate_now
for i, r in enumerate(rates):
    if all(worst[key][i] >= KEEP for key in channels):
        lowest = r
    else:
        failing = [key for key in channels if worst[key][i] < KEEP]
        print(f"\n{r} Hz is the first rate to fail: {', '.join(failing)}")
        break
print(f"Lowest rate keeping {100 * KEEP:.0f}% of every peak: {lowest} Hz")

# the proposed rate, worst and best start, in the channel's own units
k = rate_now // PROPOSED
print(f"\nAt {PROPOSED} Hz (every {k}th sample), range of peaks over the {k} starts:")
for key in channels:
    y, unit = values(key)
    p = peaks_by_start(y, k)
    print(f"  {key:12} 20 kHz {peak(y):7.2f}   worst {min(p):7.2f} ({100 * min(p) / peak(y):5.1f}%)"
          f"   best {max(p):7.2f}   start 0 {p[0]:7.2f} {unit}")

# Figure 1: worst-start fraction against rate, one line per channel
plt.figure(figsize=(7.5, 4.5))
for key, ch in channels.items():
    plt.semilogx(rates, [100 * w for w in worst[key]], '.-', label=ch['comment'].title())
plt.axhline(100 * KEEP, color='black', linestyle=':', linewidth=0.8, label='95% of the peak')
plt.axvline(PROPOSED, color='red', linewidth=0.8, label='proposed logger, 1000 Hz')
plt.xticks([200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000, 20000],
           ['200', '500', '1000', '2000', '5000', '10000', '20000'])
plt.xlabel('sample rate (Hz, log scale)')
plt.ylabel('worst-start peak (% of the 20 kHz peak)')
plt.title('Peak kept at each rate, worst start, test 9793', fontsize=10)
plt.legend(fontsize=7, loc='lower right')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('fraction_vs_rate.svg')
plt.close()

# Figure 2: the crossmember around its peak, and what a 1 kHz logger keeps of it
ch = channels['vehicle_x']
y, unit = values('vehicle_x')
t_ms = [1000 * (ch['t0'] + i * ch['dt']) for i in range(ch['n'])]
p = peaks_by_start(y, k)
best_start, worst_start = p.index(max(p)), p.index(min(p))
plt.figure(figsize=(7.5, 4))
win = [i for i in range(ch['n']) if 40 <= t_ms[i] <= 70]
plt.plot([t_ms[i] for i in win], [y[i] for i in win], color='gray', linewidth=0.8,
         label='recorded at 20000 Hz')
for start, mark, name in ((best_start, 'o', 'best'), (worst_start, 's', 'worst')):
    kept = [i for i in win if (i - start) % k == 0]
    plt.plot([t_ms[i] for i in kept], [y[i] for i in kept], mark + '-',
             label=f'1000 Hz, {name} start (sample {start})')
plt.xlabel('time from impact (ms)')
plt.ylabel('acceleration (g)')
plt.title(ch['comment'].title() + ' [' + ch['code'] + '], 40 to 70 ms',
          fontsize=10)
plt.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('crossmember_1khz.svg')

Flags. A slower rate can read a higher peak than a faster one when the samples start at 0 (4000 against 5000 Hz above), so one run per rate is not enough. The crossmember and head_y set my answer, and I have not checked whether the head_y peak is real motion or noise. The chest displacement units are wrong in the file, which did not matter here. Assumptions. Keeping every k-th sample stands in for the cheaper logger, although a real one probably filters its signal before sampling and would read differently (I have not checked how). Every starting sample is equally likely. The 10% tolerance is my choice, and a tighter one such as 5% would push the rate to 20 kHz, because head_y keeps 90.3% at 10 kHz. This is one test of one car, and our own record is also sampled, so its peaks may already be a little low.

What to notice

The notebook keeps the step that surprised its author (a slower rate reading higher than a faster one) and shows how it was resolved. It is a good idea to keep a surprise like this in a notebook, because it is the evidence for the method that follows. The answer is stated at the top, and the flags and assumptions are gathered again at the end.
