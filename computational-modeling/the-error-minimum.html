Week 4 · Noise and Filtering

The error minimum in step size

Claim

The error in a finite-difference derivative is the sum of two terms that move in opposite directions as the step $h$ shrinks. Truncation error falls with $h$. Rounding error grows as $1/h$ because a fixed subtraction error is divided by a shrinking number. Their sum has a minimum at a finite $h$, below which making the step smaller makes the answer worse.

There is a best step size, and it is not the smallest one your computer can represent.

Truncation Error and Rounding Error

Take the central difference as the case to follow:

$$f'(x) \;\approx\; \frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}$$

Truncation error is the error the formula would still have on a machine that made no arithmetic mistakes at all. It is there because the formula is an abbreviation. Finite differences showed where it comes from: the central difference equals the derivative plus an infinite tail of terms, and the formula keeps the first and discards the rest.

$$\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} \;=\; \underbrace{f'(x)}_{\text{kept}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{h^2}{6}f'''(x) + \dots}_{\text{truncated}}$$

"Truncated" is literal. The error is the part of the sum that was cut off, and it is worth seeing that this is a prediction you can check and not a statement of principle. Take $f = \sin x$ at $x = 1$ with $h = 0.1$, where $f'''(x) = -\cos x$:

central difference, $h = 0.1$0.539402252170
true value, $\cos 1$0.540302305868
measured error−9.001×10−4
first discarded term, $\frac{h^2}{6}f'''(1) = -\frac{0.01}{6}\cos 1$ −9.005×10−4

The first term of the tail accounts for the error to three figures, which is 0.05%. That is what "proportional to $h^2$" means in practice, and it is why halving $h$ quarters this term. On its own it would say: make $h$ as small as you can.

Rounding error is what the machine adds. A double holds about 16 significant decimal digits and no more, so every stored value carries an absolute error of roughly $\varepsilon |f|$, where $\varepsilon$ is machine epsilon, about $2.2\times10^{-16}$. That sounds harmless, and the subtraction is what makes it not.

Read the bottom row. The two values agree in their first eleven significant digits, so of the sixteen the machine is holding, only about five survive the subtraction. Those five are then divided by $2h = 2\times10^{-12}$, which multiplies whatever error they carry by $5\times10^{11}$. Nothing went wrong in the arithmetic. The subtraction itself is exact, and every operation was correctly rounded. The information was lost earlier, when each sine was rounded to sixteen digits to be stored, and the subtraction removed the eleven shared digits that had been hiding that rounding.

This is why the third row is worse than the second despite using a smaller step. The absolute error in the numerator does not shrink as $h$ does, because it is set by the size of $f$ and not by the size of the difference. Dividing a fixed error by $2h$ therefore grows it as $1/h$. Writing the two terms together:

$$E(h) \;\approx\; \underbrace{C h^2}_{\text{truncation}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon |f|}{h}}_{\text{rounding}}$$

One term falls as $h$ falls and the other rises, so their sum has a minimum somewhere. Finding it is ordinary calculus. Differentiate $E$ with respect to $h$:

$$E(h) = C h^2 + \frac{\varepsilon |f|}{h} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad E'(h) = 2Ch - \frac{\varepsilon |f|}{h^2}$$

The second term picked up its minus sign and its $h^{2}$ in the denominator from differentiating $h^{-1}$. Set $E'(h) = 0$ and the two terms balance:

$$2Ch \;=\; \frac{\varepsilon |f|}{h^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h^3 \;=\; \frac{\varepsilon |f|}{2C} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad h_{\text{best}} \;=\; \left(\frac{\varepsilon |f|}{2C}\right)^{1/3}$$

The cube root is the whole result. $\varepsilon$ is the only small quantity in there, so $h_{\text{best}}$ scales as $\varepsilon^{1/3}$, and $\varepsilon^{1/3} \approx 6\times10^{-6}$. The constants move it a little without changing the story: for $\sin x$ at $x = 1$, where $C = |f'''|/6 = \cos(1)/6$ and $|f| = \sin 1$, the formula gives $h_{\text{best}} = 1.0\times10^{-5}$.

Two things are worth taking from the shape of that answer. The first is that the best step is nowhere near the smallest step, and it is not close: $10^{-5}$ against the $10^{-16}$ the machine can represent. The second is that $h_{\text{best}}$ depends on $\varepsilon$ and on the function, and on nothing about your data, so it can be computed before you have any, though it does assume a size for $f$ and its derivatives that your problem need not have.

The same argument for the forward difference runs identically, except that its truncation error is proportional to $h$ and not to $h^2$, so $E = C_1 h + \varepsilon|f|/h$ and setting $E' = C_1 - \varepsilon|f|/h^2$ to zero gives a square root instead of a cube root, $h_{\text{best}} \sim \varepsilon^{1/2} \approx 1.5\times10^{-8}$. A worse rule wants a smaller step, because its truncation error falls more slowly, so $h$ has to go much further down before rounding takes over.

The Error Against Step Size

Differentiating $\sin x$ at $x = 1$, where the true answer is $\cos 1 = 0.5403023\ldots$ and the error can therefore be computed and not estimated. Both axes are logarithmic, because the quantities span sixteen orders of magnitude (this is one of the few places in the course where a log axis is not a stylistic choice).

Both predictions land within a factor of two of the measured minimum, which is what an order-of-magnitude argument is entitled to. The minima are quoted at decade steps deliberately: between decades on the left-hand branch the error swings by orders of magnitude, because it depends on the exact bits of two nearly equal numbers. Searching a fine grid there finds a lucky cancellation, and nothing reproducible. The prediction is that the best step is near $\varepsilon^{1/3}$. It is that it is near it, and specifically that it is nowhere near zero.

Reading the Exponents off the Slopes

On the logarithmic plot each error term is a straight line, and the slope of each line is the exponent in its power law. Read the plot left to right, in the direction of increasing $h$.

  • To the right of the minimum, truncation dominates and the error grows with $h$. The central-difference branch has slope 2, a factor of 10 in $h$ is a factor of 100 in error, and the forward branch has slope 1.
  • To the left of the minimum, rounding dominates and the error is proportional to $h^{-1}$, which is slope −1, falling as you read rightwards, which is to say growing without limit as $h$ is made smaller. Both rules share this branch, because both divide the same fixed subtraction error by the same $h$.

The left-hand branch is the same for both rules, which is the giveaway that it has nothing to do with the formula and everything to do with the arithmetic. A better formula moves the right-hand branch down and the minimum right, and it cannot help you on the left at all.

The subtraction is where it happens

At $h = 10^{-12}$, $\sin(1 + h)$ and $\sin(1 - h)$ agree in their first eleven significant figures. Their difference is therefore determined by the last five figures each of them has, and those are exactly the figures the floating-point representation is least sure about. This is cancellation, and it is not specific to differentiation. Any subtraction of two nearly equal computed numbers loses significant figures, and the loss is a silent one, because the result has the same number of digits as before and most of them are now meaningless. That is what makes it worth watching for deliberately.

Finding an Event

A common reason to differentiate a record is to locate a moment, meaning the instant something starts, stops, or changes character. The derivative itself is often discarded once the moment is found. A ball leaves a hand and its vertical acceleration becomes $-g$ and stays there. A vehicle contacts a barrier and its acceleration leaves zero. Neither moment is written down anywhere. Both are visible in a derivative and nowhere in the values themselves.

This is where the minimum above stops being a curiosity. Locating an event needs two things at once, and the stencil width trades the one against the other.

  • A narrow stencil is noisy. The scatter can be larger than the change being looked for, and the event is somewhere inside it.
  • A wide stencil is late and blunt. It averages across the event, so the transition is smeared over the width of the stencil and its apparent position moves.

The second failure is the dangerous one, because a smoothed signal looks better. A wide stencil returns a clean curve with an obvious corner in it, and the corner is in the wrong place. Page 13 is about that shift in general. Here it lands directly on the number being reported, because the number is a time. For 25 Hz tracking with 3.07 cm of vertical noise, a two-frame second difference carries a scatter of roughly 19 m/s², about twice gravity, against a signal of one gravity, so nothing can be located from a single one. Widening the stencil brings the scatter down as the averaging demands, and pushes the apparent event later.

An event found in a differentiated signal has an uncertainty in time, and it is set by the same choice that sets the uncertainty in value.

Two things follow for practice. State the criterion, because "where the acceleration settles to $-g$" is not one until "settles" has a tolerance and a number of consecutive samples attached to it, and check the answer against something the derivative did not produce. The values themselves usually show whether the moment you found is plausible.

Three Consequences for a Computed Function

The situation this page describes is the one where $f$ can be evaluated wherever you like, which means a computed function and not a record. Three consequences:

  1. Do not reach for the smallest representable step. The instinct that smaller is more accurate is wrong below the minimum, and wrong by a lot. At $h = 10^{-16}$ the central difference is off by $1.5 \times 10^{-2}$, which is 2.7% of the answer and sixteen times worse than at $h = 0.1$.
  2. Prefer the higher-order rule. The central difference at its best is 270 times more accurate than the forward difference at its best, for one extra function evaluation.
  3. Find the minimum by measuring. Halve $h$ repeatedly and watch the answer. While it keeps changing in the same direction you are on the truncation branch. When it starts moving erratically you have crossed into the rounding branch and gone too far.

For a measured record none of this applies, because $h$ is the sample interval and cannot be changed. The error that matters there is noise, and that is the next page.

Check
  1. You halve h and the central-difference answer changes by 1%. You halve it again and it changes by 0.25%. Which branch are you on, and what should you do next?
    Answer

    Changes falling by a factor of 4 for each halving is error ∝ h², so you are on the truncation branch, meaning the right-hand side of the curve, where making h smaller makes the answer better. What to do next: keep halving, and watch the ratio and not the answer. While it stays near 4 you are still winning. When it falls towards 2, then 1, then starts rising, you have passed the minimum and rounding error is taking over, and the best answer available was one or two steps back. Doing this is the only way to find the minimum for a particular function on a particular machine. The textbook estimate of the optimal h assumes a size for f and its derivatives that your problem need not have.

  2. A colleague reports a derivative computed with h = 10⁻¹⁴ "to be safe". How would you show them, in one run, that it is not?
    Answer

    Compute the same derivative at a sweep of h, from 10−1 down to 10−16, and plot the error on log-log. The U appears, and their h is a long way up the left-hand wall of it. One run, one figure, no argument required.

    If they want the reason as well as the picture, then at h = 10−14, f(x+h) and f(x−h) agree to about twelve digits, and a double carries sixteen. Their difference has about four significant digits left. The rest was cancelled away. Dividing that by 2×10−14 does not recover the lost digits, it scales what remains. "To be safe" is the instinct that smaller steps are safer, which is true of the truncation term alone and false of the sum.

  3. Why does the left-hand branch of the plot have the same slope for both rules, when the right-hand branches do not?
    Answer

    Because the left branch is not about the rule.

    There, the error is dominated by rounding. The numerator is a subtraction of two nearly equal numbers, carrying an absolute error of about ε|f|, and the result is divided by h. That gives ε|f|/h, a slope of −1 on log-log, and nothing in it depends on which difference formula produced the numerator. Both rules subtract two values of similar size and divide by a small number, so both rise identically. The right branch is truncation, which is where the rule's order lives: slope 1 for the one-sided rules, 2 for the central one. The order of a method describes only half of the picture, and it is the half that stops mattering first.
