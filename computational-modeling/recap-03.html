Critical Judgment on Claude's Report 4:37

Last week looked back at your Assignment 2 work through one lens, and this week used another. Chris gave Claude Assignment 2 and asked it to do the homework. The class then read its report with critical judgment, one of the AI maxims. The aim is to build the same judgment for your own work and for work from colleagues.

Critical here means reading as a critic. That is, assume every line has something wrong with it and ask four questions 5:51.

  • Do we understand it? This question tests the writing. You might be able to work out what Claude meant, but the writer's job is to be clear enough that anyone can follow without extra work.
  • Where does it come from? A number or a claim needs a source.
  • Could we explain it to Alice? If your boss comes around the corner and asks about something in your report, you want to say what it means and where you got it.
  • What new question does it raise? Questions like "what units is this in?" usually show up when the first two answers are weak. Jot them down even if you cannot answer them.

The site has Claude's original report, an annotated version and the edited version. The original holds more than it should, including two tables that say almost the same thing and a figure that adds little. Some of the annotations came from asking Claude to flag things the course has not covered, things Alice did not ask for, and things a working engineer would not use. The rest are Chris's own cuts and rewordings, since the point is for you to practice the judgment yourself. The edited version answers what Alice asked without the extra noise.

The 1 kHz Paragraph 10:46

Chris read one of the report's worst paragraphs aloud and said what he was thinking as he went. It opens with "Rates that do not divide 12,500.", which is not a sentence (it has no verb), and reads like a heading. Partway through it reaches "25 different possible starts at 1 kHz" with no word on where the 25 came from, and the rest of the paragraph builds on that point. Chris's first rewrite of the opening was a sentence that says what the paragraph is about. Since 1,000 does not divide 12,500 exactly, that rate cannot be tested with a simple decimation.

12,500 divided by 1,000 is 12.5, and decimation can keep every 12th sample or every 13th but not every 12.5th 16:26. Chris drew the first 25 samples. Every 12th lands on 12 and 24, every 13th on 13 and 26, and the sample a 1 kHz logger would want after two steps is the 25th, which does exist. Claude's method alternates gaps of 12 and 13 so it lands on 25. It may give a better answer, and as a computer scientist Chris finds it clever. However, it is not a standard method, the course has not covered it, and Alice did not ask for it. If Alice asks why you used it, you would need a reason she can check.

Claude then checked its method against a second model, a straight line between two neighboring samples 20:53. Checking a method you are unsure of is usually a good idea. Drawing a line between points to estimate what happens between them is interpolation (extrapolation is outside the points), a standard engineering technique that the course covers in Week 6 (Interpolation and regression). The line plots from Week 1 already did this, connecting samples with straight lines. Claude's check goes beyond what was asked, and anything beyond the request needs its own justification in your report.

Student question

Q: Should the fix be telling Claude to use a different technique? 23:04

A: Yes. The wording is fine, so the fix is in the method Claude used.

The edited version keeps every 12th sample (a 1,042 Hz logger) and every 13th (a 962 Hz logger), which bracket 1 kHz, and reports the worse of the two 23:41. Taking the worse case is a conservative rule (a requirement is usually checked against the worst outcome), and next week's standards work the same way.

Student question

Q: How do you decide which of the two is worse? 25:09

A: By the measure you are evaluating, here how much of the peak survives. You might expect the slower 962 Hz rate to always be worse. However, you are decimating your data and not running real loggers, so either one might happen to keep the better samples, and you evaluate both and take the worse.

On the crossmember, every 12th sample kept 50.6% of the peak and every 13th kept 54.9%, so the faster rate was the worse one there. The simple method reached the same decisions as Claude's more complicated one, so it is the one to report.

The 6,250 Hz Paragraph and Figure 3 27:48

At 6,250 Hz (every second sample) the worst a logger can do is skip the single peak sample and read the one next to it. Claude probably meant that the neighbors are close to the peak, but it did not say so. It then gives −1,503 m/s² as "the 2.4% loss" and −1,333 m/s² as 86.5%, with no word on where those numbers come from. Chris worked out that they come from the dots in a figure. Slope Stability Lab 38:09

The lab uses Chris's discrete element simulator of a slope in an earthquake (about 10 to 20 hours over the quarter). Carol is worried about a county road below a rock slope when the next earthquake comes, and your task is to find the most shaking the slope can take without damage.

First you decide what damage means, and the helper pages describe how civil engineers judge it (Chris highlighted two signals).

  • Survey stations. These sit on a slope to measure its slow everyday settling, and they keep reporting during an earthquake. If one stops reporting (buried or carried downhill), engineers usually take that as a sign of damage.
  • Rockfall. Boulders that reach the bottom are counted (a catch fence or a camera does this on a real slope), and the simulator counts them for you. You decide whether one rock counts as damage, or ten.

The week 4 proposal asks what damage means, how you will find where damage begins, how you will know you are sure, and how you plan to spend your budget 41:37. The simulations run on an old GPU in Chris's apartment (each run warms it up), so you get 40 runs. If you need more, request them with the form and say what they are for, as you would ask whoever set an engineering budget. Chris expects 40 to be enough and will approve every request.

The damage tool lets you combine conditions, such as the slope moving more than 5 cm (a screen used in California, which also uses 15 cm) or at least two rockfalls. Then put your definition in your own words.

For the method, each proposal gets a bond strength (how strong the slope is) and a range of peak ground acceleration from 0 to 5 g to test 44:26. Every bond strength from 1 to 8 was tested to hold under its own weight with no shaking. At 0 g you know the slope is safe, and you assume 5 g is not. Test halfway (2.5 g), keep the half where the change from safe to damaged must lie, and repeat until you find the line. The example class on the help page shows this pattern, and you can load and watch other students' runs. Each run also returns its seismograph, survey station and surface data, though your damage definition already decides the result.

Labor Journal Essay 51:32

This one is popular with Chris's CS students, since the effort is what earns the credit. The essay is about your own quarter, so you already know the content. You get feedback on your writing and on your reflection, and students who take it seriously often improve both.

Common topics are learning groups, schedules and deadline crunches, and work or family priorities. A reflection usually works best when it is tied to a real moment ("On Tuesday I got home from Assignment 3 and I was really stressed") and says how you felt. The guide points you to Gibbs' reflective cycle. Describe an event, say how you felt, evaluate it (good, bad or both), analyze what could have gone differently, draw a conclusion, and end with an action plan for next time.

This week you need a theme, a few sub-themes and a point-form outline. The big lift is the draft in Week 7, and in Week 10 you revise it from Chris's feedback. Please see Labor Journal Essay.

Assignment 3, the Request 74:01

Alice writes that the accelerometers ought to tell her how fast the car was going when it hit the barrier, and the lab measured that separately with its own equipment, so she wants to know whether those two numbers agree. She also asks how far the passenger compartment traveled after the car hit the wall. The first question is a double check, since if integrating the data recovers the lab's speed you can be more confident in your methods. The second asks how much of the car was crushed. The front is designed to crush so that the middle, where the people are, does not.

The class worked on Pólya's first step, understanding the problem, by listing questions to ask 76:35. Where is the lab's number? How do you get a velocity from an acceleration (integrate), and how do you integrate samples instead of a function f(x)? How do you get from velocity to the crush, which is a displacement (integrate again)?

Integrating acceleration gives the change in velocity, Δv, and not the velocity itself 80:08. To turn Δv into v you add a known velocity, such as the initial one. You need to make that correction before integrating again, or the second integral is not the displacement you want.

Chris's list of questions, which the rest of class answered in order, was where to find the lab speed, which channel is on the car, how to turn acceleration into a change in speed, how to turn Δv into v, whether anything in the method might add error, how to find the crush, and whether it all makes sense (Pólya's look-back step) 81:21. The class worked the problem on the Civic (test 9808), which is held back from the phrase assignment.

The Lab Speed and the Channel 83:11

The lab speed is in the header file, in km/h. Most of the work is in m/s, so convert it (56.17 km/h is 15.60 m/s on the Civic). Most packs likely have a similar speed, though you should check your own file.

Most of the sensors are on the dummies, and you want the car's motion and not a dummy's head or chest. On Chris's test the only channel attached to the car is the crossmember under the rear seat, the channel with the fast features and ringing from last week. If your pack has more than one channel on the car, you can pick another.

Before time zero the car is on the tow line and the acceleration should be zero, which it nearly is apart from some noise 84:54. Time zero is the moment the car first touches the wall, as the lab defines it. The trace stays near zero for a sliver after that and then looks like a car hitting a wall.

Rectangles and Trapezoids 85:58

Integration is the area between the curve and the axis. The acceleration is mostly negative, so the sum will be too. With samples, you build the area from shapes whose area is easy to compute. A rectangle has the step size as its width (the spacing in the header, 0.1 ms on the Civic) and a sample's value as its height. Some rectangles come out too small and others too large. Adding the triangle on top of each makes a trapezoid, which is just as easy to compute and has much less error. When you have the choice use trapezoids (rectangles come first mostly because they are easier to understand). Please see Area under a curve.

For rectangles, whether a piece is too small or too large depends on whether the curve is going up or down. For trapezoids it depends on how the curve bends 88:49. Where it bends down the trapezoid is too small, and where it bends up the trapezoid is too large. In computer science you often want a strict lower or upper bound, while in engineering you want to be close to the true value. On a curve that bends both ways the errors partly cancel.

The Change in Velocity 90:38

The running sum of the trapezoids (the first one, then the first two, then the first three, and so on) gives the whole Δv curve. Please see Cumulative integration. On the Civic, Δv starts at zero and falls. It reaches −15.60 m/s (the tow speed) at 77 ms, which is when the car should be stopped. It keeps going, reaches its lowest point (−18.18 m/s) at 119 ms, and then comes back up.

One story that may explain this is that the car stops at 77 ms and bounces off the wall, so the stretch to 119 ms is the car accelerating backward, and after that friction slows it. With the full record it should come to rest back at the tow-speed line, and the record ends before it does. Whether that story is real, or an error in the data that has not been accounted for yet, is the next thing to check.

Choosing the Constant 93:59

The class compared three constants to add to Δv (the tow speed from the header, the peak of Δv, and its final value). The tow speed is the right one, since it is an independent measurement of the starting velocity. Please see The constant of integration. With it added, the velocity starts at 15.60 m/s, crosses zero at 77 ms, goes negative as the car bounces back, and has not returned to zero when the record ends. Without an independent measurement of the bounce this is still a story, though checks like the offset below can make the uncertainty small.

The Offset 96:06

A recorded signal is the truth plus a small constant offset (every reading a little too high, say) plus random noise. You cannot remove the noise, but you can remove the offset, since it is the same in every reading. On the tow line the acceleration should be zero, so every wobble there is instrument error (an assumption, since the car might move a little). The average of that quiet period estimates the offset, which you subtract from every sample in the channel. What remains is noise, usually described by its standard deviation. Please see Bias and noise.

On the Civic the offset was small compared to the noise, so the corrected trace looks the same as the uncorrected one. The total Δv moved from −16.75 to −16.74 m/s, the stop stayed at 77 ms, and every value checked moved by at most 0.02 m/s. That is well within tolerance (under 1%), so the offset does not explain the bounce, and the story holds up a little better.

The Crush 100:30

The crush starts when the car touches the wall, at time zero. The stop is the guess at 77 ms, where the velocity crosses zero. Integrating the velocity gives the displacement. Before contact it rises in a straight line with a slope of 15.60 m/s (constant velocity), at contact it starts to curve over, and at its peak the car is as far forward as it goes before moving back. That peak is also at 77 ms, so two signals agree on the stop (a double confirmation, and in engineering you usually want two).

The cumulative sum already holds the area from the start up to every point 103:38. Chris drew it on the board, with A the area up to the start of the crush and B the area up to its end, so the area between them is C = B − A (674 mm on the Civic).

Leave out the tow speed and the displacement curve comes out the wrong shape 105:44. With either wrong constant the crush is too long and the peak may be in the wrong place. With one of them the car keeps moving forward after it stops, as if drilling into the wall, which is not physically possible and so points back to the wrong constant.

Chris annotated the figures to close the look-back step 107:18. Contact is at zero, the stop is at 77 ms (confirmed two ways), the crush is the area between them, and the offset did not change the answer. The summary for your homework 108:15 is as follows.

  1. The lab speed. It is in the header (probably about 56 km/h, or 15.6 m/s), and you should check your own.
  2. The channel. One attached to the car and not a dummy.
  3. The rule. Trapezoids, for less error.
  4. The constant. The tow speed from the header, an independent measurement of the starting velocity.
  5. The offset. Average the quiet period before contact and subtract it. It did not change the answer on the Civic and probably will not on yours, but Chris has not seen everyone's data, so check.
  6. The crush. From contact at zero to the end of contact (where the velocity crosses zero, or the peak of the displacement), the area between them.
  7. The look back. Make sure it all makes sense to you, so you can stand behind your report.

Please see the example reports.

Where Your Evidence Runs Out 110:33

Your report should say where your evidence runs out, and for this problem that includes the following.

  • One sensor, on one crossmember, stands for the whole compartment. A real crash test has many more sensors on the car (Chris left most of them out of your data), and a real analysis would probably integrate several and average them or pick the most accurate.
  • Nothing in the file measures the car's motion after it leaves the barrier. The bounce is part of the story but has no independent confirmation (a distance at the end of the trial in the header would help).
  • The channel is unfiltered, though crash test standards ask you to filter a channel before you integrate it. Week 4 covers that filter, why it is applied and how, so this may change.
  • The lab's speed has an uncertainty of its own, and the file does not say how it was measured or how far off it might be.
  • The samples may not be exactly evenly spaced. Engineers usually treat that timing error as error in the measured values instead.
Technically

Class described the standard as filtering your data down to a rate that makes sense, and linked it to last week's decimation. Those are different operations, since decimation keeps every so many samples and so lowers the sample rate. The filter SAE J211 assigns a channel removes content faster than that channel's class and leaves the sample rate as it is. Week 4 covers the filter properly.

The Same Integration Elsewhere 113:33

Integrating gives a change, and you need a constant from outside the record to turn it into a value. A flow meter on a pipe integrates to the change in volume, for instance, and not the volume itself.

Air Canada Flight 143 in 1983 tracked its fuel this way 114:00. The fuel computer subtracted the measured burn from the 22,300 kg it was given. After a pounds-for-kilograms mistake about 10,100 kg was aboard, and the tank gauges that would have caught it were not working. The constant of integration was wrong, so the running total was too high the whole flight, and the fuel ran out. This is one reason engineers state what they know and how uncertain it is.

Lecture correction

Class said the plane crashed. Both engines stopped, though the crew glided it to a landing at Gimli, Manitoba, and everyone on board survived.

The second example is a phone in Seattle's SR 99 tunnel 115:04. The phone has a GPS fix at the entrance. Inside, it can only estimate its position from its own sensors, and with nothing to correct it the error grows. At the exit a new GPS fix resets it. That fix is an independent measurement, so it checks the estimate and supplies the new constant.
