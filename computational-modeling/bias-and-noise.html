Week 3 · Numerical Integration

Bias and noise accumulate at different rates

Video: Bias and Noise Accumulate at Different Rates During Integration (0:14:43). Slides

Claim

Summing n samples, a constant offset b contributes an error proportional to n, while random error of standard deviation σ that is equally likely to be positive or negative contributes an error proportional to √n. Integrating a record of duration T, the offset produces a velocity error bT. Integrating a second time produces a displacement error bT²/2.

Random error partly cancels as it is added up. A constant offset does not cancel at all.

The Three Terms in a Sample

Sample $i$ returned by an accelerometer is the sum of three separate quantities:

$$a_i \;=\; s_i \;+\; b \;+\; \varepsilon_i$$
$s_i$
the acceleration of the object the sensor is attached to, which is the quantity the measurement is for.
$b$
a constant offset, the same in every sample, present whether or not the object is accelerating. It arises from the sensor and its mounting and drifts slowly with temperature.
$\varepsilon_i$
random noise, differing from sample to sample, equally likely to be positive or negative, with standard deviation $\sigma$.

The instrument reports $a_i$. The three terms on the right are not separately available. Only their sum is recorded.

Integration as a Sum

The velocity after a time $T$ is the integral of the acceleration:

$$v(T) \;=\; \int_0^T a \,\mathrm{d}t$$

That integral cannot be evaluated, because $a$ arrives as samples and not as a function. What you have is $n$ samples spaced $\Delta t$ apart, and the integral is evaluated by adding them up:

$$v(T) \;\approx\; \Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i$$

Substituting the decomposition and splitting the sum into three sums, one per term:

$$\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \;=\; \underbrace{\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i}_{\text{signal}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} b}_{\text{offset}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} \varepsilon_i}_{\text{noise}}$$

The three sums have the same number of terms and are formed by the same operation. What separates them is what happens when their terms are added.

The Offset Sum

Every term is the same number, so the sum is that number $n$ times:

$$\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} b \;=\; n\,b\,\Delta t \;=\; bT$$

Adding a constant involves no cancellation, so the sum is $n$ times that constant and doubling the number of samples doubles the result.

The Noise Sum

Here the terms have different signs. Adding $n$ independent values that are equally likely to be positive or negative does not give $n$ times a typical value, because positive and negative terms cancel. The sum of $n$ such values with standard deviation $\sigma$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{n}$, so

$$\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} \varepsilon_i \quad\text{has standard deviation}\quad \sigma\,\Delta t\,\sqrt{n} \;=\; \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t\,T}$$

using $n = T/\Delta t$. Doubling the number of samples multiplies this by $\sqrt{2}$, not by 2.

That result needs the $\varepsilon_i$ to be uncorrelated with one another, which is to say that knowing one sample's noise tells you nothing about the next one's. Being equally likely to be positive or negative is not enough on its own. Noise that has been smoothed or filtered fails this, because a filter that removes fast content makes neighbouring samples resemble each other, and its sum grows faster than $\sqrt{n}$. Week 4 measures a record where exactly that happens.

This is a different kind of statement from the one about the offset. $bT$ is a number. Given $b$ and $T$ it can be computed, and it is the error present in the record. The noise sum cannot be computed, because the individual $\varepsilon_i$ are unknown. Its distribution is known. It has expected value zero. Over many records made under the same conditions it contributes nothing on average, and standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t\,T}$. A single record produces one draw from that distribution. The formula does not say what the draw will be. It says how large such draws typically are.

That difference decides what can be done about each. The offset is a number, so it can be estimated and subtracted. The best estimate of the noise sum is its expected value, zero, so subtracting the best estimate changes nothing. Noise is reduced by averaging or by filtering. It is not removed by correction.

The Two Errors Together

Writing $E(T)$ for the noise sum, the measured velocity is

$$v(T) \;=\; v_{\text{true}}(T) \;+\; \underbrace{bT}_{\text{grows as } n} \;+\; \underbrace{E(T)}_{\text{grows as } \sqrt{n}}$$

Integrating a second time gives displacement. The velocity now carries the term $bt$, which grows in proportion to elapsed time, and that term is summed in its turn. At sample $i$ the elapsed time is $i\,\Delta t$, so the term contributes $b\,i\,\Delta t$ and the sum is

$$\Delta t \sum_{i=1}^{n} b\,i\,\Delta t \;=\; b\,\Delta t^{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} i \;=\; b\,\Delta t^{2}\,\frac{n(n+1)}{2} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2}\,b\,T^{2}$$

The last step drops the $+1$ against $n$, which matters only when $n$ is small. The factor of a half is the sum of the first $n$ whole numbers. It was not a rule applied from outside. So

$$x(T) \;=\; x_{\text{true}}(T) \;+\; \frac{1}{2}bT^{2}$$

Each integration multiplies the offset error by a further factor of $T$. The offset measured on this page’s channel, −0.2135 m/s², displaces the answer by 1.1 mm after 0.1 s, 10.7 cm after 1 s, and 10.7 m after 10 s. Dividing the offset error by the noise error:

$$\frac{bT}{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t\,T}} \;=\; \frac{b}{\sigma}\sqrt{n}$$

The ratio of the two errors in the sum is the ratio of offset to noise in a single sample, multiplied by $\sqrt{n}$. An offset far smaller than the noise in any individual reading overtakes it once enough readings have been added, and this says how many are needed.

Accumulated Offset and Noise

Both terms can be computed directly from the record, so neither has to be argued about. The offset term is the running total of $b$. The noise term is the running total of the recorded pre-impact residuals about $b$, real instrument noise, accumulated. No model of it is involved.

Switch the axes to logarithmic. Each term becomes a straight line, and the slope of each line is its exponent: 1 for the offset, one half for the noise. That is the claim at the top of the page, drawn.

Drag the offset down and the crossing moves to the right. Drag it up and the crossing moves left until the offset dominates from the first sample. Set it to zero and the offset line disappears entirely, which is what a perfectly calibrated instrument would give and what no instrument does.

Compare the two noise readouts. Over the whole record the predicted size is 0.0128 m/s and this record's actual accumulated noise is 0.0025 m/s, about five times smaller. That is not a failure of the prediction. The prediction is the width of a distribution and this record is one draw from it. A draw well inside the width is the common case, and a second test would give a different value of similar order.

The trapezoid weighting

The code on this page uses the trapezoid rule, which weights the two end samples by a half instead of giving every sample equal weight. That changes $bT$ by one sample's worth, $b\,\Delta t$, and leaves the $\sqrt{n}$ behaviour of the noise term unchanged. For $n$ in the hundreds or thousands neither difference is visible, and the results above are used as written.

Separating Offset from Noise

Separating the three terms requires a stretch of record where the first one is zero.

Every crash test recording begins before the impact. During those samples the vehicle is travelling at constant speed on a tow line, so its true acceleration is zero. Anything the instrument records in that window is offset plus noise, with no signal underneath. The recording used throughout this page is from a 2019 BMW X5 driven into a rigid barrier at 15.65 m/s. The channel is a longitudinal accelerometer on the left rear seat crossmember, sampled at 12 500 Hz, with 625 samples, 50 milliseconds, recorded before the impact.

Over that pre-impact window the mean of the recorded values is −0.2135 m/s² and their standard deviation is 2.377 m/s². The mean is the offset. The scatter about the mean is the noise.

The Formulas Against This Record

The pre-impact window supplies every quantity the formulas need: b = −0.2135 m/s², σ = 2.377 m/s², Δt = 8×10⁻⁵ s, T = 0.050 s, and therefore n = 625 samples.

The ratio row has no measured entry on purpose. Dividing the two measured numbers gives 105, and that is not a disagreement with the prediction. The predicted ratio compares a fixed number against the typical size of a noise draw, and this record's draw happened to be a small one, so the ratio of these two particular measurements says nothing about typical sizes.

The offset error is a prediction of a single number and it lands within 2% of the integrated record. The dotted red line on the velocity panel is that prediction, bT, drawn over the measured curve.

The noise error is a predicted spread. Reading it as a predicted value claims more than it says. Subtracting b from every sample removes the bT term and leaves the noise sum on its own. The corrected curve ends at +0.0001 m/s. That is one draw from a distribution centred on zero with a width of 0.0048 m/s, and this draw came out small. The same calculation on a different test would give a different value of similar size.

Where √n comes from

Adding values that are equally likely to be positive or negative is a random walk: each step moves up or down with equal probability, so steps in opposite directions cancel and the expected distance from the start after n steps grows as √n. The same result explains why averaging repeated measurements improves precision only as √n. A hundred repeats reduce the scatter by a factor of ten. The square root is what rules out a hundred.

Checking Against the Measured Impact Speed

A velocity obtained by integration can be checked against a velocity that was measured some other way. The test report gives the vehicle’s speed at the moment of impact as 15.65 m/s. Integrating the baseline-corrected acceleration over the whole 360 ms record gives a change in speed of 17.51 m/s. Those two numbers differ by 12%. Two things can produce that difference, and they are not distinguishable from the change in speed alone:

  • An uncorrected offset. A negative b accumulates over the record as bT and inflates the total.
  • A rebound. If the vehicle leaves the barrier travelling backwards, the total change in speed is the approach speed plus the rebound speed, and genuinely exceeds 15.65 m/s.

The offset was measured in the pre-impact window, so its contribution is computed and not guessed. Over 360 ms, bT = −0.0769 m/s, which is 0.44% of 17.51 m/s. It is too small to account for 12%. The rebound is settled by looking at the speed itself and not at the change in speed. Adding the known 15.65 m/s to the integrated velocity gives the speed of the vehicle at every instant:

The speed falls, crosses zero at 62 ms, and continues into negative values. A negative speed means the vehicle is travelling backwards, away from the barrier. The curve reaches −2.52 m/s at 87 ms, comes back up, and is level at −1.86 m/s from about 180 ms to the end of the record. The rebound is therefore observed directly, and it accounts for the discrepancy, since 15.65 + 1.86 = 17.51 m/s. The ratio of rebound speed to approach speed, 1.86 / 15.65 = 0.119, is the coefficient of restitution for this impact.

The curve is the speed of one point

This accelerometer is bolted to the left rear seat crossmember, so the curve is the speed of that point on the structure and not of the vehicle as a whole. The overshoot to −2.52 m/s and the recovery to −1.86 m/s are the mounting point moving relative to the rest of the vehicle while the structure deforms and springs back. The flat section at the end, once that relative motion has stopped, is the part of the tail that can be read as the vehicle’s rebound speed. Reading it off the overshoot instead would give a coefficient of restitution of 0.161, from the same record.

The offset error is small here because the record is short. Holding b and σ at the values measured for this channel and evaluating the two formulas at longer durations:

def integrate(y, dt):
    return np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum((y[1:] + y[:-1]) * 0.5 * dt)])

def offset(a, t):
    return a[t < 0].mean()

end

b = mean(a(t < 0));

function integrate(y, dt) {
  const s = [0];
  for (let i = 1; i < y.length; i++)
    s.push(s[i - 1] + (y[i] + y[i - 1]) * 0.5 * dt);
  return s;
}

A note on units

Accelerations in this record are stored in m/s², and every calculation on this page uses those units. Crash engineering conventionally quotes peak accelerations as multiples of standard gravity: the peak here is −1541 m/s², reported in that convention as −157 g. Both describe the same acceleration.

Check
  1. An accelerometer has an offset of 0.05 m/s². You integrate two seconds of its output. What velocity error does the offset produce, and what displacement error?
    Answer

    Velocity: 0.1 m/s. A constant 0.05 m/s² integrated for 2 s gives 0.05 × 2 = 0.1 m/s.

    Displacement: 0.1 m. Integrating again gives ½at² = 0.5 × 0.05 × 4 = 0.1 m.

    Note what happened to the exponent. The offset is constant, its effect on velocity grows as t, and its effect on displacement grows as t². A bias too small to see on the acceleration trace, 0.05 against peaks of hundreds, becomes ten centimetres of displacement, and would become 40 cm in four seconds.

  2. The same instrument has a noise standard deviation of 2 m/s² and samples at 10 kHz. Over the same two seconds, is the velocity error dominated by the offset or by the noise?
    Answer

    By the offset, by a factor of about 3.5. Two seconds at 10 kHz is n = 20 000 samples with dt = 10−4 s. Zero-mean noise partly cancels, so its contribution to the sum grows as √n:

    σv = σ · dt · √n = 2 × 10−4 × 141.4 = 0.028 m/s, against 0.1 m/s from the offset. And the gap widens with time: the offset term grows as n and the noise term as √n, so doubling the duration doubles one and multiplies the other by 1.41. The noise is forty times larger than the offset in the raw signal, 2 against 0.05, and contributes less than a third as much to the answer. Which of two errors matters is a question about the operation, not about their sizes.

  3. An integrated speed change exceeds the known approach speed by 12%. What measurement distinguishes an uncorrected offset from a rebound?
    Answer

    Measure the mean of the acceleration in the pre-impact window, before anything has happened. The true value there is zero, so whatever you get is the offset, and you can then subtract it and integrate again.

    The two produce the same symptom by different routes. A rebound is real: the vehicle bounces back off the barrier, so the total change in velocity genuinely exceeds the approach speed. An offset is arithmetic, a small constant accumulating over the whole record. They also look different after the event. A rebound leaves the velocity flat at a new value once the vehicle is free. An offset leaves it still drifting, at a constant slope, for as long as the record runs. The pre-impact window is the better check because it is quantitative and available first.
