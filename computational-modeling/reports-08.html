Example reports

Example reports · Assignment 7

These are three reports for Assignment 7, Drag, written in three different styles. All three use the training arcs in the example pack (test 9793, which no phrase assigns). Nothing here answers anybody's assignment. The three students made different choices about the stepping method, the frames the starting state comes from, the value of the drag constant and what "worth it" is measured against, and each one says why. Please see the Example reports page for what every report needs and who it is written for.

Each report is followed by a short note on what it does well. The three verdicts are not quite the same, because the reports measure different things. The memo scores the position just before the hoop, the technical report scores the whole unseen flight and the rim crossing (on the arcs where the crossing can be trusted), and the notebook scores the height and the position across the floor separately. Drag helps on most of these measures. It makes the height slightly worse in the notebook, and at the rim crossing the technical report's result depends on the drag constant. Each report states what it measured alongside what it found. Every number comes from the program that is attached.

The technical report's program is read one section at a time, beside a MATLAB version that prints the same results, on Reading the assignment 7 program.

A Memo

Example 1 · Memo

To: Bob

Re: Whether to add drag to the flight model

Data phrase: example pack (test 9793)

Add drag. I stepped the flight of 23 of the 24 training arcs from a starting state fitted to the first 10 frames of free flight, once without drag and once with it, and compared each prediction with where the ball actually was at the last frame before the hoop. With drag the prediction was closer on 22 of the 23 arcs, by 4.8 cm on the median arc. That gain is larger than the tracker's own noise (3.07 cm in height and 2.84 cm across the floor, from the file header), so it is unlikely to be noise. The median error fell from 12.4 to 10.8 cm and the worst from 26.4 to 19.2 cm. (The two medians come from different arcs, which is why they are closer together than the median gain.)

The drag is quadratic. It decelerates the ball at k times the speed squared, against its velocity, with k = 0.016 per meter. That is the value the notes page State as a vector gives for a drag coefficient of 0.35, a 600 g ball and air at 20 °C. The coefficient is the middle of the 0.3 to 0.4 that Zhang, Zhu, Li and Hong measured for match balls in a wind tunnel (iScience 29(8), 115873, 2026), and the notes give the whole range of k as 0.013 to 0.019. I stepped the flight with Heun's method in steps of 0.01 s. Without drag, Heun gave back the fitted parabola to within a millionth of a millimeter on every arc, which checks the stepper. With drag, halving the step to 0.005 s moved no prediction by more than 0.011 mm. Each halving cut the change by about four (0.18, 0.041 and 0.011 mm), which is what a second-order method should do.

How I decided. The file does not say when the ball leaves the hand, so I estimated the vertical acceleration at each frame from a parabola through the 9 frames around it and started each flight 5 frames after the last upward reading. I ended it at the last frame where the ball was still 1 m or more from the hoop, measured across the floor, before it can touch the rim. For the starting state I fitted straight lines to the first 10 positions across the floor and a parabola with gravity held at 9.81 m/s² to the first 10 heights. Measured from the 10th frame, the fits give a position and a velocity there, and the ball is stepped on from that frame. The error is the straight-line distance between the predicted and the measured position at the last frame of the flight. I counted drag as worth adding if it brought the prediction closer by more than the tracker's noise (about 3 cm) on the median arc.

Three things need your attention.

  • Most of the error is still there. With drag the median arc is still 10.8 cm off. That is about twice the 5.1 cm that the tracker's noise alone puts between a perfect prediction and the measured ball in three dimensions (worked out on the notes page State as a vector). Drag accounts for part of the gap, and the rest is likely in the starting state, since a velocity from 10 noisy frames is itself uncertain, though I have not tested that.
  • Arc 1151 is worse with drag (15.0 cm against 12.1 cm without). It is the only arc where drag did not help, and I have not found why.
  • Two arcs are odd. Arc 325 has a stretch the tracker filled in with straight lines, so I left it out. My rule found no upward push at all in arc 739, so its flight starts at its first frame. It has the largest error without drag (26.4 cm).

Assumptions. The coordinates are in meters, since the header gives the rim height in meters and the coordinates no unit. Gravity is 9.81 m/s². The drag constant is the same for every shot and every moment of flight, and the ball does not spin. A straight line through 10 frames gives the average velocity over them, and I used it as the velocity at the 10th frame. With drag the ball is slowing, so this probably overstates its speed there. On the notes page State as a vector, where the fitted velocity is placed moved the error with drag by several centimeters and changed the verdict on most of its eight arcs, so this choice may matter here too, and I have not tested it. The times are as recorded in the file.

Program. a7_drag_at_hoop.py (attached) finds each flight, fits the starting state, steps it both ways, prints the step check and a line for every arc, and draws both figures.

What to notice

Bob asked whether drag was worth it, and the first word tells him. The deciding number is the median gain per arc, and the memo sets it beside the tracker's noise from the header so that Bob can see why 4.8 cm counts. The step evidence takes three sentences. One shows that the stepper gives back the parabola, one that halving the step changes nothing he could see, and one that each halving cuts the change by about four, as a second-order method should. The first bullet says how much of the error drag leaves behind (about twice what the noise alone would give), which probably keeps Bob from reading the verdict as larger than it is.

A Technical Report

Example 2 · Technical report

Data phrase: example pack (test 9793)

Summary

Quadratic drag makes the stepped flight follow the ball more closely, and it is worth adding when the starting state comes from 10 or 14 frames. Started from 10 fitted frames, the median rms distance between the stepped and the measured ball over the rest of the free flight fell from 12.1 cm without drag to 6.7 cm with k at the low end of its range and 7.4 cm at the high end, over 23 training arcs. Drag was closer on 20 and 18 of them. Both gains are larger than the tracker's 3.07 cm noise. From 6 frames the gain is about the size of the noise. At the rim crossing the result depends on k and the frames fitted. On the 8 arcs whose free flight reaches the crossing, drag passed the same test only with k at the low end and 10 frames fitted (median 13.6 to 9.9 cm, closer on 5 of 8). With k at the high end its median error was larger than without drag at every length. On 8 arcs this is weak evidence either way.

Data

The training file holds 24 arcs of a basketball's position, tracked at 25 frames a second (one frame every 0.04 s). The header gives the tracking noise as 3.07 cm vertically and 2.84 cm horizontally, "mildly correlated frame to frame". Each training record starts before the ball leaves the shooter's hand and ends 15 frames after the rim crossing. Arc 325 was dropped (see Errors and Anomalies), which leaves 23.

Method

The model. The state is the position and velocity, [x, y, z, vx, vy, vz]. Its rate is the velocity and the acceleration a = −k|v|v − gẑ, with g = 9.81 m/s². The speed |v| includes all three components, so the three directions are stepped together as one state. With k = 0 this is the parabola from assignment 5.

The drag constant. k = ρCDA/(2m), with the values on the notes page State as a vector. The air density ρ is 1.20 kg/m³ (air at 20 °C and sea-level pressure). The cross-section A = πr² is 0.0449 m² for the 0.1195 m radius in the header. The drag coefficient CD is between 0.3 and 0.4 above about 6 m/s, as measured in a wind tunnel by Zhang, Zhu, Li and Hong ("Experimental investigation on the aerodynamic drag of basketballs", iScience 29(8), 115873, 2026). The mass m is between 580 and 620 g (FIBA, Official Basketball Rules 2026, Basketball Equipment, Table 1). The least drag these allow (CD = 0.3 and 620 g) is k = 0.0130 per meter, and the most (0.4 and 580 g) is 0.0186. Both ends were run, so the result does not rest on one assumed value.

Finding the flight. The rules are the ones from assignment 5. The vertical acceleration at each frame comes from a parabola through the 9 frames centered on it. Release is the first frame after the last upward reading at which the acceleration is within 5 m/s² of −9.81 and stays there for 4 frames. The flight ends at the frame before the acceleration leaves that band for 4 frames in a row, or before the ball drops below rim height (3.048 m), whichever comes first.

The starting state. Straight lines are fitted to x and y and a parabola with gravity held at 9.81 to z, over the first 6, 10 or 14 frames of flight (the lengths matched to the test records in assignment 5). Time is measured from the middle of the fitted frames, so each fit's constant is the position there and its slope is the velocity there. A straight line through the frames of a slowing ball has a slope nearer the speed at the middle of those frames than at either end, which is why the middle was used.

Stepping. RK4, in steps of 0.04 s (one frame), from the middle of the fitted frames to each later frame of the free flight. Where frames are not exactly 40 ms apart the gap is filled with equal steps of nearly 0.04 s, so that a step always lands on a frame.

Scoring. Two scores are kept for each arc and each model.

  • Unseen frames. The rms distance between the stepped and the measured ball over the frames after the fitted ones, to the end of the free flight.
  • Rim crossing. The measured crossing is a straight-line interpolation between the last frame above 3.048 m after the top of the arc and the frame after it. The stepped crossing is found by stepping until the ball is below the rim on the way down, then bisecting on the length of that last step to a microsecond. The score is the distance between the two across the floor. It counts only when the free flight ends no more than 4 frames before the measured crossing. Each acceleration estimate reaches 4 frames either side, so a ball that touches the rim at the crossing can end its flight up to 4 frames early, and a longer gap means the ball probably touched something first.

Drag counts as worth adding where it lowers the median error by more than the tracker's vertical noise (3.07 cm) and is closer on most arcs.

A Python program, a7_drag_model.py (listed in the appendix and submitted with this report), does all of the above and draws every figure.

With no drag the acceleration is constant, and RK4 should give back the fitted parabola exactly. On every arc the stepped position at the end of the flight matched the parabola to within a millionth of a millimeter, which checks the stepper. With drag (k = 0.0186, 10 frames fitted) the largest change in any arc's final position when the step was halved is below.

Every error entry is a median over the arcs. On the unseen frames, drag lowers the median by 4.7 to 5.4 cm at 10 frames and by 3.6 to 3.9 cm at 14, more than the 3.07 cm noise in both cases, and it is closer on most arcs. From 6 frames the gain is 2.5 to 3.0 cm, about the size of the noise, and drag is closer on only 14 or 15 of 23. At that length the error without drag is 17.8 cm, so the starting state itself is probably the larger problem. Figure 2 shows a typical arc and Figure 3 shows both scores against the frames fitted.

The two scores disagree. Along the flight, drag helps at every length tested. At the crossing it passes the test only with the low end of k from 10 frames, where the median falls by 3.7 cm and drag is closer on 5 of 8. From 14 frames the low end lowers the median by only 0.5 cm, and the high end of k makes the median worse at every length. The crossing is set by the moment the height comes down through 3.048 m, so a model that places the ball better across the floor may still put the crossing in the wrong place if its height is off. I have not separated the two, and the crossing score rests on only 8 arcs. The low end of k did as well as the high end or better on both scores at 10 and 14 frames. Whether that says the drag on these balls is at the low end has not been tested, since the starting state comes from a fit that assumes no drag over the fitted frames.

For predicting where the ball goes along its flight, I would build on the stepped model with drag at k = 0.0130. For the rim crossing, the result changes with k and the frames fitted and rests on 8 arcs, so these arcs are weak evidence for or against drag there.

Errors and Anomalies

Arc 325 is filled in. Its height runs in a straight line for six steps or more, which measured heights with 3 cm of noise do not do. The tracker probably lost the ball, and the arc was dropped.

Most measured crossings are not in free flight. On 15 of the 23 arcs the free flight ends more than 4 frames before the measured crossing. On those arcs the ball has probably hit the rim or the board first, so the measured crossing is not where a flight model would put it, and they were left out of the crossing score.

Drag is worse on three arcs. At 10 frames, arcs 1092, 794 and 1307 have a larger unseen error with drag than without, at both ends of k. The cause has not been looked for.

Correlated noise. The header says the tracking noise is "mildly correlated frame to frame". The comparison with 3.07 cm treats the noise as one number and does not allow for that.

Assumptions

  • The coordinates are in meters. The header gives no unit for them, but it gives the rim height in meters.
  • Gravity is 9.81 m/s², as in assignment 5.
  • The drag constant is the same for every shot and through the whole flight. The notes give the drag coefficient for speeds above about 6 m/s. Near the top of the arc the ball moves at about its speed across the floor, which on many of these arcs is a little under 6 m/s, and I have assumed the same range of the coefficient holds there.
  • The air is at 20 °C and sea-level pressure. The header does not say where the arena is.
  • The ball does not spin.
  • The starting fit assumes no drag over the fitted frames, and its slopes are the velocity at the middle of them.
  • The flight runs from the release rule to the end rule above.

Appendix. Program

"""Assignment 7: is quadratic drag worth adding to the shot-flight model?

Run this from the unzipped pack folder. For every training arc it finds the free
flight (the release and end rules from assignment 5) and takes a starting position
and velocity from a fit to the first 6, 10 or 14 frames of it. From there it steps
the flight forward with RK4 three ways: with no drag, and with quadratic drag at
each end of the range of k worked out on the notes page State as a vector. Each
stepped flight is scored on the frames the fit did not see, and on the measured rim
crossing where the ball reaches rim height before it touches anything. It prints
the drag constants, the step-size check, a line per arc and a summary, and saves
convergence.svg, one_arc.svg and errors.svg.
"""
import json

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = 9.81               # m/s^2
WINDOW = 9             # frames in each acceleration estimate (0.32 s)
TOL = 5.0              # m/s^2, how close to -g counts as free flight
RUN = 4                # frames in a row it has to stay that close
RIM = 3.048            # m, rim height (from the header)
FIT_LENGTHS = [6, 10, 14]
H = 0.04               # s, the step (one frame), chosen by the halving check below
SHOW = 662             # the arc drawn in one_arc.svg

# k = rho C_D A / (2 m), with the numbers given on the notes page State as a vector
RHO = 1.20             # kg/m^3, air at 20 C and sea-level pressure
CD_LOW = 0.30          # drag coefficient, low end of the wind-tunnel range
CD_HIGH = 0.40         # high end
MASS_LOW = 0.580       # kg, lightest ball the rules allow
MASS_HIGH = 0.620      # kg, heaviest


def load(name):
    with open(f'shots/{name}.json') as f:
        return json.load(f)


def accel(t, z):
    """Vertical acceleration at every frame, from a parabola through the 9 frames
    centered on it. NaN where the window runs off either end."""
    h = WINDOW // 2
    a = np.full(len(t), np.nan)
    for i in range(h, len(t) - h):
        c = np.polyfit(t[i - h:i + h + 1] - t[i], z[i - h:i + h + 1], 2)
        a[i] = 2 * c[0]
    return a


def release(t, z):
    """The first frame after the last upward push at which the acceleration is
    within TOL of -g and stays there for RUN frames."""
    a = accel(t, z)
    top = int(np.argmax(z))
    first = WINDOW // 2
    for i in range(top):
        if a[i] > 0:
            first = i + 1      # the hand is still pushing at frame i
    for i in range(first, len(t) - RUN + 1):
        if np.all(np.abs(a[i:i + RUN] + G) < TOL):
            return i
    return None


def flight_end(t, z):
    """Last frame of the free flight: the frame before the acceleration leaves the
    band for RUN frames in a row (the ball has hit something), or the frame
    before the ball drops through rim height, whichever comes first."""
    a = accel(t, z)
    top = int(np.argmax(z))
    for i in range(top, len(t) - RUN + 1):
        if np.all(np.abs(a[i:i + RUN] + G) >= TOL) or z[i] < RIM:
            return i - 1
    return len(t) - 1


def straight_run(z, frames=6):
    """True if the height falls on a straight line for 'frames' steps or more.
    Measured heights carry about 3 cm of noise, so a straight line means the
    tracker filled a gap."""
    flat = np.abs(np.diff(z, 2)) < 0.001
    for i in range(len(flat) - frames + 2):
        if np.all(flat[i:i + frames - 1]):
            return True
    return False


def start_state(t, x, y, z, first, n):
    """Position and velocity at the middle of n frames starting at 'first', from
    straight lines in x and y and a parabola in z with gravity held at 9.81.
    Time is measured from the middle of the fitted frames, so each fit's constant
    is the position there and its slope is the velocity there."""
    t_mid = np.mean(t[first:first + n])
    dt = t[first:first + n] - t_mid
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(dt), dt])
    cx = np.linalg.lstsq(A, x[first:first + n], rcond=None)[0]
    cy = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y[first:first + n], rcond=None)[0]
    cz = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z[first:first + n] + 0.5 * G * dt ** 2, rcond=None)[0]
    return t_mid, np.array([cx[0], cy[0], cz[0], cx[1], cy[1], cz[1]])


def rate(s, drag_k):
    """The rate of the state s = [x, y, z, vx, vy, vz]. Drag acts against the
    velocity and grows with the speed squared, so every direction needs the speed."""
    vx = s[3]
    vy = s[4]
    vz = s[5]
    speed = np.sqrt(vx ** 2 + vy ** 2 + vz ** 2)
    return np.array([vx, vy, vz,
                     -drag_k * speed * vx,
                     -drag_k * speed * vy,
                     -G - drag_k * speed * vz])


def rk4_step(s, h, drag_k):
    k1 = rate(s, drag_k)
    k2 = rate(s + h / 2 * k1, drag_k)
    k3 = rate(s + h / 2 * k2, drag_k)
    k4 = rate(s + h * k3, drag_k)
    return s + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)


def step_to(s, t_from, t_to, drag_k, h_max):
    """Step the state from t_from to t_to in equal steps no longer than about
    h_max, so that the last step lands exactly on t_to."""
    n = max(1, int(np.floor((t_to - t_from) / h_max + 0.5)))
    h = (t_to - t_from) / n
    for i in range(n):
        s = rk4_step(s, h, drag_k)
    return s


def unseen_error(t, x, y, z, first, last, t_mid, s, drag_k):
    """rms distance (m) between the stepped ball and the measured ball over
    frames first .. last."""
    t_now = t_mid
    gaps = []
    for j in range(first, last + 1):
        s = step_to(s, t_now, t[j], drag_k, H)
        t_now = t[j]
        gaps.append(np.sqrt((s[0] - x[j]) ** 2 + (s[1] - y[j]) ** 2 + (s[2] - z[j]) ** 2))
    return np.sqrt(np.mean(np.array(gaps) ** 2))


def stepped_crossing(t_now, s, drag_k):
    """Time and floor position where the stepped ball comes down through rim
    height: steps of H until it is below the rim on the way down, then bisection
    on the length of that last step, to a microsecond."""
    while True:
        s_next = rk4_step(s, H, drag_k)
        if s_next[2] < RIM and s_next[5] < 0:
            break
        s = s_next
        t_now = t_now + H
    lo = 0.0           # a step this long ends above the rim
    hi = H             # and this long ends below it
    while hi - lo > 1e-6:
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if rk4_step(s, mid, drag_k)[2] > RIM:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    s = rk4_step(s, lo, drag_k)
    return t_now + lo, s[0], s[1]


def find_shot(shots, wanted):
    """The shot whose id is wanted."""
    for s in shots:
        if s['id'] == wanted:
            return s


def measured_crossing(t, x, y, z):
    """The rim crossing in the record: the last frame above rim height after the
    top, and a straight line from it to the next frame (which is below)."""
    i = int(np.argmax(z))
    while z[i + 1] >= RIM:
        i = i + 1
    f = (z[i] - RIM) / (z[i] - z[i + 1])     # how far from frame i to frame i+1
    return i, t[i] + f * (t[i + 1] - t[i]), x[i] + f * (x[i + 1] - x[i]), y[i] + f * (y[i + 1] - y[i])


train = load('train')
header = train['header']
shots = train['shots']
noise_z = header['tracking_noise']['vertical_m']
noise_xy = header['tracking_noise']['horizontal_m']
print(f'tracking noise from the header: vertical {100 * noise_z:.2f} cm, '
      f'horizontal {100 * noise_xy:.2f} cm')

area = np.pi * header['ball_radius_m'] ** 2
K_LOW = RHO * CD_LOW * area / (2 * MASS_HIGH)      # least drag: low C_D, heavy ball
K_HIGH = RHO * CD_HIGH * area / (2 * MASS_LOW)     # most drag: high C_D, light ball
DRAGS = [0.0, K_LOW, K_HIGH]
print(f'ball cross-section {area:.4f} m^2, k from {K_LOW:.4f} to {K_HIGH:.4f} per m')

# the free flight in every training arc
arcs = []
for s in shots:
    t = np.array(s['t'])
    x = np.array(s['x'])
    y = np.array(s['y'])
    z = np.array(s['z'])
    if straight_run(z):
        print(f"arc {s['id']} dropped: its height runs in a straight line (a filled gap)")
        continue
    arcs.append((s['id'], t, x, y, z, release(t, z), flight_end(t, z)))
print(f'{len(arcs)} arcs used')

# with no drag the stepped ball should follow the fitted parabola exactly
print()
worst = 0.0
for arc_id, t, x, y, z, r, e in arcs:
    t_mid, s0 = start_state(t, x, y, z, r, 10)
    dt = t[e] - t_mid
    parabola = np.array([s0[0] + s0[3] * dt, s0[1] + s0[4] * dt,
                         s0[2] + s0[5] * dt - 0.5 * G * dt ** 2])
    stepped = step_to(s0, t_mid, t[e], 0.0, H)[:3]
    worst = max(worst, np.max(np.abs(stepped - parabola)))
print(f'no drag, step {H} s: largest gap from the fitted parabola {1000 * worst:.6f} mm')

# step size: halve it and watch the position at the end of each free flight

steps = [0.08, 0.04, 0.02, 0.01, 0.005]
ends = []
for h in steps:
    row = []
    for arc_id, t, x, y, z, r, e in arcs:

        row.append(step_to(s0, t_mid, t[e], K_HIGH, h)[:3])
    ends.append(np.array(row))
changes = []
for i in range(1, len(steps)):
    gap = np.sqrt(np.sum((ends[i] - ends[i - 1]) ** 2, axis=1))    # one per arc
    changes.append(np.max(gap))
    line = f'  {steps[i - 1]:.3f} s to {steps[i]:.3f} s: {1000 * changes[-1]:.2e} mm'
    if i > 1:
        line = line + f'   ratio {changes[-2] / changes[-1]:.1f}'
    print(line)

# every arc, every fit length, all three models
results = {}
crossings = {}
for n_fit in FIT_LENGTHS:
    rows = []
    cross_rows = []
    for arc_id, t, x, y, z, r, e in arcs:
        t_mid, s0 = start_state(t, x, y, z, r, n_fit)
        row = []
        for drag_k in DRAGS:
            row.append(unseen_error(t, x, y, z, r + n_fit, e, t_mid, s0, drag_k))
        rows.append(row)
        # the measured crossing counts only if the free flight reaches it: no more
        # than half an acceleration window between the end of the flight and it
        i, tc, xc, yc = measured_crossing(t, x, y, z)
        if i - e <= WINDOW // 2:
            row = [arc_id]
            for drag_k in DRAGS:
                tp, xp, yp = stepped_crossing(t_mid, s0, drag_k)

Assignment 7, drag in the flight model. Data phrase: example pack (test 9793)

I did this in Google Colab. The notes page State as a vector gives quadratic drag as an acceleration of −k|v|v, with k = ρCDA/(2m), and a drag coefficient between 0.3 and 0.4 from a wind tunnel (Zhang, Zhu, Li and Hong, iScience 2026). My plan is to fit a starting state to the first half of each training flight, step it with and without drag, and compare the second half with where the ball actually was. Next, the free flight of each arc, with the same rules as in assignment 5 (the release is where the upward speed stops growing, and the flight stops 1 m from the hoop).

G = 9.81

def release(t, z):
    # walk back from the top while the upward speed keeps growing;
    # the frame where it stops growing is where the hand let go
    vz = np.full(len(t), np.nan)
    vz[2:-2] = (z[4:] - z[:-4]) / (t[4:] - t[:-4])
    i = int(np.argmax(z))
    while i > 3 and (vz[i - 1] > vz[i] or vz[i - 2] > vz[i]):
        i = i - 1
    return i

def flight(s):
    # release to the last frame more than 1 m from the hoop (across the floor)
    t = np.array(s['t'])
    x = np.array(s['x'])
    y = np.array(s['y'])
    z = np.array(s['z'])
    near = np.hypot(x - s['hoop_x'], y - s['hoop_y']) < 1.0
    end = int(np.argmax(near))
    r = release(t, z)
    return t[r:end], x[r:end], y[r:end], z[r:end]

That leaves 23 flights of 23 to 31 frames. Next, a stepper. Euler's method is the simplest, and it is first order, so it will need a small step. Before trusting it with drag I check it without drag, where the answer is known. With k = 0 the stepped height should be the fitted parabola.

def rate(s, k):
    # s = [x, y, z, vx, vy, vz]; drag is k |v| v, against the motion
    v = s[3:]
    speed = np.sqrt(np.sum(v**2))
    return np.concatenate([v, -k*speed*v - np.array([0, 0, G])])

def euler(s, t_from, t_to, k, h):
    # whole number of equal steps of about h, the last landing on t_to
    n = max(1, int(np.floor((t_to - t_from)/h + 0.5)))
    dt = (t_to - t_from) / n
    for i in range(n):
        s = s + dt * rate(s, k)
    return s

def start(t, x, y, z):
    # lines in x and y, parabola in z with g held, timed from the middle
    tm = np.mean(t)
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t - tm])
    cx = np.linalg.lstsq(A, x, rcond=None)[0]
    cy = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]
    cz = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z + 0.5*G*(t - tm)**2, rcond=None)[0]
    return tm, np.array([cx[0], cy[0], cz[0], cx[1], cy[1], cz[1]])

# one arc: fit the first half, step to its last frame
arc_id, t, x, y, z = arcs[0]
half = len(t) // 2
tm, s0 = start(t[:half], x[:half], y[:half], z[:half])
T = t[-1] - tm

Without drag, Euler's height ends 14.9 mm above the parabola at h = 0.004 s, and the gap halves every time the step halves. That is what a first-order method should do, and it errs on the high side, as the notes say Euler does on a curve that bends down. With drag the change between halvings also halves (7.20, 3.60, 1.80, 0.90 mm). For a first-order method the change from h to h/2 is about the error left at h/2, so at h = 0.0005 s the error is about 1.8 mm, under a tenth of the tracker's noise. That is the step I used. It takes 80 steps per frame, which is slow but fine for 23 arcs. I only checked this on one arc (1092, 0.76 s of stepping).

STEP = 0.0005

def errors(arc, k):
    # rms height error and rms floor error over the second half (cm)
    arc_id, t, x, y, z = arc
    half = len(t) // 2
    tm, s = start(t[:half], x[:half], y[:half], z[:half])
    t_now = tm
    dz = []
    dxy = []
    for j in range(half, len(t)):
        s = euler(s, t_now, t[j], k, STEP)
        t_now = t[j]
        dz.append(s[2] - z[j])
        dxy.append(np.hypot(s[0] - x[j], s[1] - y[j]))
    dz = np.array(dz)
    dxy = np.array(dxy)
    return 100*np.sqrt(np.mean(dz**2)), 100*np.sqrt(np.mean(dxy**2))

The two halves of the plot tell different stories. Across the floor, drag helps a lot. The median error falls from 10.2 to 4.4 cm, drag is closer on 22 of 23 arcs, and the median gain of 5.6 cm is about twice the 2.84 cm horizontal noise (more than the noise on 20 arcs). In height, drag is slightly worse on most arcs. Drag is closer on only 8 of 23, and the median goes from 4.3 to 4.5 cm, a change far smaller than the noise. Without drag the height error is already only about 1.2 cm above the 3.07 cm vertical noise, so there was likely little for drag to fix.

My answer for Bob is that drag is worth adding for where the ball goes across the floor, where it removes about twice the tracker's noise, and that it makes the height slightly worse on these flights, by much less than the noise on the median. I have only tried the low end of k and only one starting fit (the first half of each flight), so a different choice of either might move these numbers.

Flags. Arc 325 has a straight-line fill (from assignment 5) and was left out. Arc 83 has the largest floor error with drag (13.7 cm, down from 21.1) and I have not looked at why.
