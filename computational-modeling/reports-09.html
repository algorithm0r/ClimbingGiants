Example reports

Example reports · Assignment 8

These are three reports for Assignment 8, Uncertainty Intervals, written in three different styles. All three use the short records and training arcs in the example pack (test 9793, which no phrase assigns). Nothing here answers anybody's assignment. The three students put their intervals on different quantities (a distance from the hoop center, a position along and across the line of the shot, and a position in the court's own coordinates), drew the noise in different ways (at the header's level, or from the residuals of fitted flights) and checked their intervals against different answers, and each one says why. Please see the Example reports page for what every report needs and who it is written for.

Each report is followed by a short note on what it does well. The purpose of the notes is to show that different methods can meet the same standard. That is, plots are identified at a glance, errors are flagged and assumptions are noted. Their counts differ because their checks differ (one arc is about 14 points of a count out of 7 arcs, and about 4 out of 23), and every number in them comes from the program that is attached.

The technical report's program is read one section at a time, beside a MATLAB version, on Reading the assignment 8 program.

How I built the intervals. A short record starts with the ball still in the hand, so I found the last frame where the ball's vertical acceleration is upward (from a parabola through the 9 frames around each frame) and fitted everything after it. That leaves 6 to 10 frames, 7 on most records. The fit is a parabola in height with gravity held at 9.81 m/s² and a straight line in each floor direction, and the crossing is the later of the two times the parabola is at rim height (the ball coming down). What I varied is the tracker's noise. The header gives it as 3.07 cm in height and 2.84 cm across the floor, so I added random noise of that size to every fitted frame, fitted again and found the crossing again, 1000 times per record. The interval runs from the 5th to the 95th percentile of the 1000 distances, and the value is their median. What I held fixed is gravity, the model (no drag), the frame the fit starts on and the position of the hoop.

How I counted. I cut every training arc 7 frames after its last push, the median for the short records, so it holds what a short record would. Then I built its interval the same way and compared it with where the ball really came down, found from the frames on either side of rim height. That comparison only makes sense if the ball got to rim height in free flight. For this reason I counted an arc only if every acceleration estimate from the top of the arc to 4 frames before the crossing stayed within 5 m/s² of −9.81. A bounce off the rim or the board breaks that. 7 of the 24 arcs pass. The other 17 fail the test, and on most of them the ball has hit the rim or the board before it comes down through rim height (arc 325 also has a stretch of height the tracker filled in). One estimate outside the band is enough to fail an arc, so a few of the 17 may have come down in free flight and been left out anyway.

What the count means. 6 of 7 is 86%, which is as close to 90% as 7 shots can get without being 7 of 7. Each shot is worth 14 points, though, so the count cannot separate 90% from a rate one or two shots lower. I would not quote the interval to the coaches as 90% on this check alone. The one miss is arc 1144. Its interval ran from 0.33 to 1.01 m, and the ball came down 0.06 m from the center. A distance cannot be less than zero, so every low end sits above zero (the lowest is 0.04 m, on record 332), and a ball that comes down near the middle of the hoop is the case this kind of interval is most likely to miss. That floor does not explain arc 1144, though. Its low end is 0.33 m, well clear of zero, so the fit to its 7 frames of flight put the crossing too far from the center by more than the tracker's noise allows. The cause may be something the reruns hold fixed (the model has no drag, for instance), and I have not tested that.

Three things need your attention.

  • Record 685 has the fewest frames. It has 6 frames after the last push, and its interval (0.50 to 1.51 m) is about twice as wide as most of the others. The prediction may be less trustworthy than the interval says.
  • Most arcs could not be checked. 17 of the 24 fail the free-flight test, most of them because the ball hit the rim or the board first. The count rests on the 7 that pass, and these may not be typical of the others.
  • The noise may be correlated. The header notes that the noise is mildly correlated frame to frame. My draws are independent. If neighboring frames tend to be off in the same direction, the fit could be pulled further than my reruns allow, and the intervals would be too narrow.

Assumptions. Coordinates are in meters. Gravity is 9.81 m/s², with no air drag or spin. The tracker's noise is normal, at the header's level and independent from frame to frame. The flight starts on the frame after the last upward push. The random numbers are seeded, so the program prints the same thing every time.

Program. a8_ninety_percent.py (attached) builds every interval, runs the check and draws both figures.

What to notice

The first paragraph gives Bob the intervals, the count and what the count can and cannot support. The memo puts its interval on the distance from the hoop center, since that is likely what Bob will use to decide whether a shot goes in. It checks the interval only against crossings it could see, says why the other 17 arcs were left out, and looks at the one miss. The floor at zero that a distance has is the first reason to suspect, and the memo checks it against the interval's low end (0.33 m) before setting it aside. The miss is left as a prediction that came down too far from the center, with its cause untested.

A Technical Report

Example 2 · Technical report

Data phrase: example pack (test 9793)

Summary

Each short record's rim crossing is given here as two numbers with a 90% interval on each: how far past the hoop center it lands along the line of the shot (long or short) and how far to the right of that line. The median interval is 0.67 m wide long/short and 0.45 m wide left/right. On 23 training arcs cut to the length of a short record, the answer from the whole arc fell inside its interval on 18 (78%) long/short and on 22 (96%) left/right. Four of the five long/short misses are on the short side. This means the left/right interval does about what it claims, while the long/short interval covers less than 90% (likely nearer 80%) and may lean long, which would point to something the noise alone does not account for. On the 8 arcs where the crossing can be seen in the frames, 7 were inside both ways.

Data

The header says the short records begin partway into the flight, though each one holds an upward push, so each starts with the ball still in the hand. After the last upward push they have 6 to 10 frames of flight (median 7), at 25 frames a second. The training file holds 24 whole arcs. The header gives the tracker's noise as 3.07 cm vertical and 2.84 cm horizontal, with the note that it was measured across the set and is mildly correlated frame to frame. The hoop's inner radius (0.2286 m) is used only to draw the rim in Figure 1.

Method

The prediction. The fit starts on the frame after the last upward push, found as in assignment 5 from a parabola through the 9 frames around each frame. The height is fitted with z = c0 + c1t − ½gt² (g = 9.81 m/s²), and each floor coordinate with a straight line. The crossing is the later root of z(t) = 3.048 m, which is the ball coming down. The crossing point is then measured from the hoop center along the line from the first fitted frame to the hoop (positive is long) and across it (positive is to the right, as the shooter sees it). Both numbers can take either sign, so neither has the floor at zero that a distance from the hoop center has (Coverage shows what that floor does to an interval).

The interval. Noise is added to every fitted frame, drawn from a normal distribution with the header's standard deviations, and the fit and the solve are run again. This is repeated 1000 times per record. The 1000 answers are sorted, the 50 lowest and 50 highest are left out, and the remaining 900 span the 90% interval. The value reported is the crossing from the frames as measured.

The first fitted frame was chosen, and the reruns never move it. As a check, every fit was started one frame later. The value moved by a median of 0.06 m long/short and 0.05 m left/right, about a tenth of the interval widths.

The check. Each training arc is cut 7 frames after its last push (the median for the short records) and given an interval in exactly the same way. Its answer comes from the whole arc: the free flight found by the assignment 5 rules, fitted with gravity free, and carried down to rim height. Most training arcs hit the rim or the board before the ball's center reaches rim height, so this answer is itself a fit, and the check is a fit against a fit. Where the flight does run down to rim height (to within the 4 frames an acceleration estimate looks ahead), the crossing is also read off the frames by interpolating between the frame just above rim height and the one just below.

A Python program, a8_crossing_intervals.py (listed in the appendix and submitted with this report), does all of the above and draws every figure.

Of the five long/short misses against the whole-flight answer, four came down shorter than the interval allowed and one longer (Figure 3). The one left/right miss, arc 83, came down further left. Against the crossings seen in the frames, the long/short miss is arc 1144, whose interval ran from +0.25 to +0.95 m while the ball came down at +0.05 m. Its left/right miss is on the edge of the interval (−0.03 m against an upper end of −0.03 m at two decimals).

What the count means. Left/right, 22 of 23 is close enough to 90% that the count gives no reason to doubt the label. Long/short, 18 of 23 is short of 90% by about three arcs, and the misses fall mostly on one side. A miss that is only a matter of width should fall on either side about equally. Four of five on the short side may mean that the predictions run long, though the count alone is weak evidence of it. If each miss were as likely to fall on one side as the other, a split of 4 to 1 or more lopsided would still come up about 37% of the time. The stronger reason is the drag finding on Coverage, where fitting gravity freely gives less than 9.81 m/s², most likely because of drag. The model here has no drag, and a ball slowed by the air would likely come down shorter than a drag-free fit to its first frames predicts. I have not tested that here. Until it is tested, the long/short interval is better quoted as below 90%, likely nearer 80%, than as 90%.

Errors and Anomalies

Most arcs hit something before rim height. Only 8 of the 23 arcs run in free flight down to rim height. On the rest, the first downward pass through rim height comes after a bounce. As a result the main check is against a fitted answer, and Coverage notes that such a check is if anything gentler than reality. The fitted answer agreed with the seen crossing to within 0.12 m on the 8 arcs that have both.

Arc 325 is filled in. A stretch of its height runs in straight lines, as found in assignment 5, and it was left out, which leaves 23 arcs.

Record 685 is the shortest. It has 6 frames after the last push, and its long/short interval (+0.52 to +1.49 m) is the widest of the 12 and reaches furthest past the hoop. It may be less trustworthy than its interval suggests, since the fewer the frames, the further the fit has to reach.

Assumptions

  • The coordinates are in meters, as the rim height is.
  • The tracker's noise is normal, at the header's level and independent from frame to frame. The header says it is mildly correlated. Correlated noise would likely move the fit further than independent draws do.
  • The flight starts on the frame after the last upward push. Starting a frame later moves the answers by about a tenth of the interval width.
  • Gravity is 9.81 m/s², and the ball follows a parabola with no drag or spin.
  • A training arc cut 7 frames after its last push stands for a short record.
  • The whole-flight fit, with gravity free, is close enough to where the ball would have come down to serve as the answer.

Appendix. Program

"""Assignment 8: 90% intervals on where each short record comes down through rim height.

Run this from the unzipped pack folder. For each short record it fits the frames
after the last upward push (a parabola in height with gravity held at 9.81, and
straight lines across the floor), solves for the point where the falling ball
reaches rim height, and splits that point into long/short (along the line
from the first fitted frame to the hoop) and left/right (across that line). It
then adds noise at the tracker's stated level to the fitted frames and fits and
solves again, 1000 times. The 50 lowest and 50 highest answers are left out, and the other 900
span the 90% interval.

The training arcs are cut to the length of a short record and given the same
intervals, which are checked against where each whole arc says the ball came
down. It prints a line per record and per arc and the counts, and saves
cloud.svg, short_intervals.svg and coverage.svg.
"""
import json

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = 9.81               # m/s^2, held fixed
RIM = 3.048            # m, rim height (from the header)
WINDOW = 9             # frames in each acceleration estimate (0.32 s)
TOL = 5.0              # m/s^2, how close to -g counts as free flight
RUN = 4                # frames in a row, for the end of a whole flight
DRAWS = 1000           # reruns per record
LEAVE = DRAWS // 20    # 50 answers left out at each end, which leaves 90%
SEED = 8               # the same random numbers on every run
SHOW = 1299            # the short record drawn in cloud.svg


def load(name):
    with open(f'shots/{name}.json') as f:
        return json.load(f)


def accel(t, z):
    """Vertical acceleration at every frame, from a parabola through the 9 frames
    centered on it. NaN where the window runs off either end."""
    h = WINDOW // 2
    a = np.full(len(t), np.nan)
    for i in range(h, len(t) - h):
        c = np.polyfit(t[i - h:i + h + 1] - t[i], z[i - h:i + h + 1], 2)
        a[i] = 2 * c[0]
    return a


def last_push(t, z):
    """The last frame before the top where the acceleration is upward (the hand
    is still on the ball). With no such frame, the first frame that has an
    acceleration estimate."""
    a = accel(t, z)
    top = int(np.argmax(z))
    last = WINDOW // 2
    for i in range(top):
        if a[i] > 0:
            last = i
    return last


def release(t, z):
    """Assignment 5's release on a whole arc: the first frame after the last push
    where the acceleration is within TOL of -g for RUN frames in a row."""
    a = accel(t, z)
    for i in range(last_push(t, z) + 1, len(t) - RUN + 1):
        if np.all(np.abs(a[i:i + RUN] + G) < TOL):
            return i


def flight_end(t, z):
    """Assignment 5's end of a whole flight: the frame before the acceleration
    leaves the band for RUN frames (the ball hit something), or before the ball
    drops below rim height, whichever comes first."""
    a = accel(t, z)
    for i in range(int(np.argmax(z)), len(t) - RUN + 1):
        if np.all(np.abs(a[i:i + RUN] + G) >= TOL) or z[i] < RIM:
            return i - 1
    return len(t) - 1


def filled_in(z):
    """True if the height runs in a straight line for 6 steps or more, which
    3 cm of tracking noise cannot do (the tracker filled a gap)."""
    flat = np.abs(np.diff(z, 2)) < 0.001
    for i in range(len(flat) - 4):
        if np.all(flat[i:i + 5]):
            return True
    return False


def line(t, v):
    """Least-squares straight line v = c0 + c1 t."""
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t])
    return np.linalg.lstsq(A, v, rcond=None)[0]


def crossing(t, x, y, z, fit_gravity=False):
    """Fit the frames given and solve for the falling crossing of rim height.
    Returns the time from the first frame given, and x and y there."""
    t = t - t[0]
    if fit_gravity:
        A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t, t ** 2])
        c0, c1, c2 = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z, rcond=None)[0]
    else:   # the known term moves to the left-hand side
        c0, c1 = line(t, z + 0.5 * G * t ** 2)
        c2 = -G / 2
    # c2 t^2 + c1 t + (c0 - RIM) = 0; with c2 < 0 the later root has the minus sign
    tc = (-c1 - np.sqrt(c1 ** 2 - 4 * c2 * (c0 - RIM))) / (2 * c2)
    bx = line(t, x)
    by = line(t, y)
    return tc, bx[0] + bx[1] * tc, by[0] + by[1] * tc


def observed(x, y, z):
    """The frame just before the ball comes down through rim height, and where it
    crossed, by straight-line interpolation between that frame and the next."""
    for k in range(int(np.argmax(z)), len(z) - 1):
        if z[k] >= RIM and z[k + 1] < RIM:
            f = (z[k] - RIM) / (z[k] - z[k + 1])
            return k, x[k] + f * (x[k + 1] - x[k]), y[k] + f * (y[k + 1] - y[k])


def split(x, y, s, x0, y0):
    """A crossing point as (long, right): meters past the hoop center along the
    line from (x0, y0), the first fitted frame, to the hoop, and meters to the
    right of that line as the shooter sees it."""
    ux = s['hoop_x'] - x0
    uy = s['hoop_y'] - y0
    n = np.hypot(ux, uy)
    ux = ux / n
    uy = uy / n
    dx = x - s['hoop_x']
    dy = y - s['hoop_y']
    return dx * ux + dy * uy, uy * dx - ux * dy


def interval(t, x, y, z, first, s, rng):
    """The crossing from the frames, and its 90% interval from DRAWS reruns with
    the tracker's noise added to the fitted frames. Returns the value, the low
    and high ends (each as long, right) and every rerun's answer."""
    t = t[first:]
    x = x[first:]
    y = y[first:]
    z = z[first:]
    x0 = x[0]
    y0 = y[0]
    tc, xc, yc = crossing(t, x, y, z)
    value = split(xc, yc, s, x0, y0)
    runs = np.zeros((DRAWS, 2))
    for k in range(DRAWS):
        zk = z + SIG_Z * rng.standard_normal(len(z))
        xk = x + SIG_XY * rng.standard_normal(len(x))
        yk = y + SIG_XY * rng.standard_normal(len(y))
        tc, xc, yc = crossing(t, xk, yk, zk)
        runs[k] = split(xc, yc, s, x0, y0)
    ends = np.sort(runs, axis=0)
    return np.array(value), ends[LEAVE], ends[DRAWS - 1 - LEAVE], runs

What to notice

The summary gives both intervals, both counts and what the counts mean, in that order. The report splits the crossing into two signed numbers so that each interval can fall on either side of zero (a distance cannot), and it gives a reason for doing so. The table of what was varied and what was held fixed answers one of Bob's questions directly, and the report tests one of the held-fixed choices (the first fitted frame) against the width of the interval. The check is made two ways, and the report says which of the two is a fit and what that may cost. When the count comes out short, the report looks at which side the misses fall on before saying what the count means, since misses that fall mostly on one side may point to a cause the noise does not include (see Coverage).

My plan is to predict where each short record crosses rim height in the court's own coordinates (x along the court and y across it, measured from the hoop center), then rerun the prediction with noise added to the frames. For the noise I am going to use the residuals left over when I fit the training arcs' whole flights, so the noise is drawn from what the data show. First, what is in the files.

I take the flight as starting on the frame after the last upward push, and fit a parabola with gravity held at 9.81 to the height and a straight line to each floor coordinate.

G = 9.81
RIM = 3.048

def last_push(t, z):
    # the last frame before the top where a parabola through the 9 frames
    # around it curves upward, so the hand is still pushing
    top = int(np.argmax(z))
    last = 4
    for i in range(4, min(top, len(t) - 4)):
        c = np.polyfit(t[i-4:i+5] - t[i], z[i-4:i+5], 2)
        if c[0] > 0:
            last = i
    return last

def fit_cross(t, x, y, z, free_g=False):
    # fit the frames, then find where the falling ball reaches rim height
    t = t - t[0]
    if free_g:
        cz = np.polyfit(t, z, 2)
    else:                            # gravity held: fit z + (g/2) t^2 with a line
        b = np.polyfit(t, z + 0.5*G*t**2, 1)
        cz = np.array([-G/2, b[0], b[1]])
    roots = np.roots([cz[0], cz[1], cz[2] - RIM])
    tc = max(roots.real)             # the later root: the ball coming down
    cx = np.polyfit(t, x, 1)
    cy = np.polyfit(t, y, 1)
    return np.polyval(cx, tc), np.polyval(cy, tc)

def whole_flight(s):
    # from the frame after the last push to the last frame more than 1 m from
    # the hoop (measured across the floor), before anything can touch the ball
    t, x, y, z = [np.array(s[k]) for k in 'txyz']
    first = last_push(t, z) + 1
    d = np.hypot(x - s['hoop_x'], y - s['hoop_y'])
    end = int(np.argmax(d < 1.0))
    return t[first:end], x[first:end], y[first:end], z[first:end]

A whole-flight fit has three numbers to bend to the noise, so the residuals may come out a little small. I am using them anyway, since they are what the data show. Next, the interval. I add residuals drawn at random (with replacement) from the pools to the fitted frames, refit, and take the mean of 1000 answers ± 1.645 standard deviations, which is the 90% convention on Reporting an interval.

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
DRAWS = 1000

def interval(t, x, y, z, hoop):
    # refit DRAWS times, each time adding residuals drawn (with replacement)
    # from the pools; return mean and 1.645 sd for x and y, from the hoop
    xs = []
    ys = []
    n = len(t)
    for k in range(DRAWS):
        xc, yc = fit_cross(t, x + rng.choice(res_xy, n),
                           y + rng.choice(res_xy, n), z + rng.choice(res_z, n))
        xs.append(xc - hoop[0])
        ys.append(yc - hoop[1])
    return (np.mean(xs), 1.645*np.std(xs, ddof=1),
            np.mean(ys), 1.645*np.std(ys, ddof=1))

What to notice

The notebook takes its noise from the data, says how that compares with the header's figure, and reports both (2.33 against 3.07 cm in height). Its interval is the ±1.645 σ convention, which is simple to read and gives 90% when the spread is near-normal. That is likely a fair assumption on these records, since the technical report's percentile ends sit about evenly either side of its values (on no short record do the two sides differ by more than 0.05 m), though neither report checks the shape of the spread directly. When the count comes out at 70% the notebook says so in bold and works out which way the misses fell, which takes some care in court coordinates where long and short depend on the hoop. It also names the two choices that may have narrowed its intervals, and says why neither explains misses on one side.
