Week 4 · Noise and Filtering

Differentiation amplifies noise

Video: Differentiation by Differences Amplifies Noise (0:13:47). Slides

Claim

Differencing two samples subtracts their noise as well as their signal, and dividing by the sample interval $h$ then multiplies the result by $1/h$. Write $\sigma$ for the standard deviation of the noise on a single sample. For a central difference the noise in the output has standard deviation $\sigma/(h\sqrt{2})$. The amplification factor is set by the sample interval, not by the quality of the data.

Integration adds noise up and it partly cancels, while differentiation divides noise by a very small number. On a record whose noise is independent from sample to sample, nothing cancels at all.

Deriving the Amplification Factor

Take samples $y_i = s_i + \varepsilon_i$, with noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ independent from sample to sample. The central difference is

$$d_i = \frac{y_{i+1} - y_{i-1}}{2h} = \underbrace{\frac{s_{i+1} - s_{i-1}}{2h}}_{\text{signal}} + \underbrace{\frac{\varepsilon_{i+1} - \varepsilon_{i-1}}{2h}}_{\text{noise}}$$

The difference of two independent values each of standard deviation $\sigma$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{2}$. Dividing by $2h$:

$$\text{noise in } d_i \;=\; \frac{\sigma\sqrt{2}}{2h} \;=\; \frac{\sigma}{h\sqrt{2}}$$

The amplification factor is $1/(h\sqrt{2})$. It contains no property of the signal, only the sample interval. A record sampled ten times faster has ten times as much noise in its derivative, from the same instrument.

The true peak of the derivative is 12.57 in every row. At σ = 10⁻² the noise is 1% of the signal and invisible in a plot of it, while in the derivative it is larger than the quantity being measured, and the computed peak of 31.2 is not a property of the sine at all.

The 30% Discrepancy

At σ = 10⁻² the formula gives $0.01/(0.001\times1.414) = 7.1$ and the measured value is 5.5. The formula is over-predicting by about 30%, consistently, in the rows where the noise is large enough to measure.

The noise on this page may look synthetic. It is 1000 consecutive samples from the pre-impact window of a real accelerometer. Consecutive samples of that recording are not independent. Their lag-1 correlation is +0.78, and it falls to zero only by lag 3. The laboratory's 2000 Hz anti-alias filter, running at a 12 500 Hz sample rate, will do that, because a filter that removes fast content is a filter that makes neighbouring samples resemble one another. That recorded sequence is laid onto this page's 1 kHz grid, so its sample-to-sample correlation comes with it even though the original time axis does not. The derivation on this page assumed independence in one step, where the difference of two values each of standard deviation $\sigma$ has standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{2}$. For correlated values that becomes

$$\sqrt{2\sigma^2(1 - \rho)} \;=\; \sigma\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}$$

with $\rho$ the correlation between the two samples being differenced. Positively correlated noise partly cancels in a difference, so the amplification is smaller than the independent formula predicts. The ratio is $\sqrt{1-\rho}$, and the measured shortfall of 5.5/7.1 = 0.77 implies an effective $\rho$ of about 0.4 across the two samples the central difference uses. A central difference touches the samples either side of the point, so the figure it depends on is the lag-2 correlation, and measuring that directly on the recording gives +0.38. The value recovered from the shortfall and the value measured on the record agree. The formula is not wrong, though its independence assumption is wrong for this record, and it errs in the safe direction by over-stating the noise and not under-stating it. Substituting a Gaussian generator for the real recording would have agreed with the formula, and the discrepancy that sent us back to the independence assumption would not have appeared.

Correlated Noise Explains the Shortfall

The broadband part of the noise is close to uncorrelated from sample to sample, which is another way of saying it changes about as fast as the sampling allows. Differentiation asks how fast things are changing, so it responds most strongly to whatever changes fastest, and in a noisy record that is the noise. That is also why the two operations behave oppositely. Integration adds neighbouring samples, so independent errors partly cancel and the total grows only as $\sqrt{n}$. Differencing subtracts them, so the errors add in quadrature and are then divided by a small number.

IntegrationDifferentiation
operation on neighboursaddsubtract
thenmultiply by hdivide by h
effect on random errorpartly cancels, grows as √n reinforces, scales as 1/h
effect on a constant offsetgrows without limitremoved entirely

The last row is the one that usually surprises people. A constant offset is the thing integration cannot survive and the thing differentiation is immune to, because a constant differences to zero. That is, neither operation is safer than the other, and the two are vulnerable to different things.

Two Ways to Reduce the Noise

The sample interval is fixed, so the amplification factor cannot be reduced. What can be changed is the noise entering the difference, and there are two ways:

  • Smooth first. Reduce σ before differencing. This is the next page, and it has a cost.
  • Widen the stencil. Fit a line or a parabola over several samples and take its slope. More samples means more averaging of the noise, and a derivative that belongs to a wider region than a single point.

Both amount to the same trade, and neither is free. Every reduction in the noise of a derivative is bought with resolution in time.

Check
  1. A record has σ = 0.5 m/s² and is sampled at 10 kHz. What is the standard deviation of the noise in its central-difference derivative?
    Answer

    About 3.5 × 103 m/s³. σout = σ / (h√2), with h = 10−4 s:

    0.5 / (10−4 × 1.414) = 3536 m/s³.

    The amplification is a factor of 7071. Half a metre per second squared of noise on the input, a good instrument, becomes thousands on the output, and the jerk being estimated is unlikely to be that large. Calling the derivative noisy understates it. The derivative is predominantly noise.

  2. Your colleague resamples the same signal at 20 kHz to "get more detail" in the derivative. What happens to the noise, and why?
    Answer

    It doubles, to about 7 × 103 m/s³. The output noise goes as 1/h, and halving the sample interval halves h.

    The reason it does not help is that the noise on each sample is a property of the instrument, not of the sampling rate. Taking samples closer together does not make any one of them more accurate. The two values being subtracted move closer together, their true difference shrinks, and the noise on that difference does not, so the ratio of signal to noise falls, and then the result is divided by a smaller number. If they resampled by interpolating the existing record instead of re-recording, it is worse still. No new information has entered at all, and the extra points are a smooth invention whose derivative is the derivative of the interpolant.

  3. You have a record with a constant offset and noise. Which of the two survives differentiation, and which survives integration?
    Answer

    Noise survives differentiation. The offset survives integration. Each operation is blind to one error and ruinous with the other. A constant offset has zero derivative, differencing two samples subtracts it from itself, so differentiation removes it entirely. Integration accumulates it, and the error grows in proportion to the number of samples.

    Noise is the mirror image. Integration averages it, so its contribution grows only as √n and is progressively suppressed relative to the signal. Differentiation amplifies it by 1/h. Which is why the order of operations matters. Removing a baseline before integrating is essential and before differentiating is pointless. Smoothing before differentiating is essential and before integrating is close to pointless.
