Week 7 · Root Finding and Search

Conditions as roots

Claim

A root finder answers "where is $f$ zero". Engineering questions are usually of the form "where does $g$ equal $c$". The two are the same question after defining $f(x) = g(x) - c$, because the values of $x$ satisfying the condition are exactly the roots of $f$. Which of those roots is meaningful is decided by the domain of the problem, not by the algebra.

Subtract the target from both sides, and the question the routine answers becomes the question you asked.

Rewriting a Condition as a Root

Design conditions arrive as equalities against a target:

  • the deflection reaches the allowable limit, $\delta(x) = \delta_{\text{allow}}$
  • the bending moment reaches the section capacity, $M(x) = M_{\text{cap}}$
  • the ball reaches the plane of the hoop, $y(t) = 3.048$
  • two loadings produce equal stress, $\sigma_1(x) = \sigma_2(x)$

Each becomes a root problem by moving everything to one side:

$$f(x) \;=\; g(x) - c \qquad\text{or}\qquad f(x) \;=\; \sigma_1(x) - \sigma_2(x)$$

Moving everything to one side is a convention. Root finders are written to look for zero because zero is the one target that needs no extra argument, and every other target is reached by subtraction.

Subtraction and Ratio Forms

The same condition can be written as a ratio, which is tempting when comparing a demand against a capacity:

$$g_{\text{ratio}}(x) \;=\; \frac{c}{g(x)} - 1$$

It vanishes wherever the subtraction form vanishes, so it has the same roots. It also has a pole wherever $g(x) = 0$, though, and a function may change sign across a pole without having a root there. That difference is what page 22's second failure is made of, and it is the reason to prefer the subtraction even though both forms are algebraically correct.

Plotting the Function

Once $f$ is defined it can be evaluated anywhere, and a few hundred evaluations across the domain cost almost nothing. Here is what the plot shows that a returned number does not:

  • How many roots there are. A routine returns one regardless.
  • Roughly where they are. This is what a bracket or a starting guess needs.
  • Whether any are near a pole or a flat spot. Both are visible immediately and neither is visible in a result.

The Physical Root

$f$ has roots wherever the algebra puts them, including outside the region the model describes. The parabola above crosses 3.048 m twice, and only one of those crossings happened. This shot is released at 3.45 m, above the rim, as 99% of three-pointers in this set are, the median release being 3.59 m against a 3.048 m rim. The earlier root is at −0.079 s, a time before the ball left the hand, where the fitted parabola is an extrapolation backwards into a region the model was never fitted to and the ball was not yet in free flight. Nothing about that root looks wrong. It satisfies $f(t) = 0$ to machine precision, and a routine handed a bracket reaching back past $t = 0$ can return it and report success without qualification. Three filters, applied in this order:

  1. Is it in the domain? A beam from 0 to 6 m has no points at $x = -4$. A flight has no times before release.
  2. Is it in the range the model is valid over? A small-deflection beam formula stops describing a beam that has deflected a long way, and it does not announce this.
  3. Is it the one the question asks about? Ascending or descending, first crossing or last, loading or unloading.

None of the three is available to the routine, because all three are properties of the problem it was pointed at. The first two are properties of the model and the last is a property of the question you asked.

Check
  1. Write "the factor of safety falls to 1.5" as a root problem, in the subtraction form, and say what the pole would be if you wrote it as a ratio.
    Answer

    Subtraction: f(x) = capacity(x) − 1.5 × demand(x). Roots of f are the values where the factor of safety, capacity / demand, is exactly 1.5.

    As a ratio: f(x) = FS(x)/1.5 − 1, or FS(x) − 1.5, or capacity/(1.5 × demand) − 1. All three divide by the demand. The pole is wherever the demand goes to zero, such as an unloaded station or a section where the bending moment changes sign. That is worth avoiding. Where the demand changes sign, FS jumps from +∞ to −∞, and f changes sign across the pole without passing through zero, so a bracketing method sees the sign change, converges tidily on the pole, and reports a location where the structure is safest as the place it fails.

  2. A plot of your f crosses zero three times over the domain. What do you do before calling a root finder?
    Answer

    Decide which root you want, and bracket that one alone. A root finder returns one value. If it is handed an interval containing three roots, the bracket or the starting point decides which one it returns, so it is a good idea to make that choice yourself. Concretely, scan the domain on a grid, find the intervals where the sign changes, and call the routine once on each with its own bracket. Then apply the filters, asking which roots are in the physical domain, which are in the range the model is valid over, and which one the question asks about. Finally, check the count, because a plot only shows the crossings it can resolve. A pair closer together than the plotting resolution is invisible on it, and only a scan on a grid finer than their spacing would find them.

  3. A routine returns a root at x = 7.8 m on a 6 m beam. Which of the three filters caught it, and could the routine have caught it itself?
    Answer

    The domain filter, which asks whether the answer is in the region the model describes. A beam of 6 m has no station at 7.8 m, so the deflection formula was evaluated where it means nothing.

    Could the routine have caught it? Yes, but only if it had been told. A bracketing method given [0, 6] cannot return 7.8, because the domain is built into the bracket and the guarantee enforces it. Newton takes no bracket, so nothing constrains where it goes, and it may converge on a root of the extended formula. The general point is that the domain is not in the function. f is a piece of algebra that happily evaluates at 7.8, and the fact that beams end is information the routine does not have unless it is passed in, as a bracket, or as the bound in the loop condition on page 3.
