Week 7 · Root Finding and Search

Root-finding failures

Claim

A root-finding routine returns a value x satisfying its own stopping test, which is a bound on |f(x)|. It does not test whether that value is the root you wanted, whether it lies in the domain your problem is defined on, or whether it is a root at all. Four situations produce a returned value with no error reported.

The routine answers the question you typed, and it has no way to know the question you meant.

The Root Finder Guarantee

A root finder is given a function $f$ and returns a number. The promise attached to that number is narrow:

  • Bisection promises that its returned value lies inside an interval it maintained, and that $f$ changed sign across that interval at every step. It offers this only if it is handed a bracket to begin with.
  • Newton's method promises nothing in advance. It returns the value at which its stopping test happened to be satisfied.

Both stopping tests take the same form. Stop when

$$|f(x)| < \texttt{tol}$$

That is a statement about the residual, meaning how far the equation is from being satisfied at $x$, and it is not a statement about the error, which is how far $x$ is from the root. The two are related by the slope of $f$, so where the slope is small they are very different numbers.

Four Failures

Each of the four below is a real problem from statics, dynamics or mechanics of materials, with ordinary numbers in it, and in each of them a routine runs to completion and reports success.

  1. More than one root. $f$ has two roots and the routine returns one. Which one depends on where you started.
  2. A bracket that contains no root. $f$ changes sign across a pole, so a bracket exists where no root does, and bisection converges on the discontinuity.
  3. A root the structure does not have. Newton converges cleanly to a value that satisfies the formula but lies outside the object the formula describes.
  4. A root with no sign change. $f$ touches zero without crossing, so no bracket exists anywhere and the residual test is satisfied while the answer is still wrong.

Each case below carries its own demo, running both methods on that case. The controls are Newton’s starting point and the tolerance both methods stop at, while the bracket handed to bisection is fixed and stated in the table.

1. The Wrong Root

A real tracked three-pointer, with a parabola least-squares fitted to its free-flight frames:

$$y(t) = 3.4520 + 4.7428\,t - 4.9153\,t^2, \qquad f(t) = y(t) - 3.048$$

The fitted curvature corresponds to $g = 9.831$ m/s², which is the measurement's own answer and not an assumed one. The ball is released at 3.452 m, above the 3.048 m hoop, as 99% of the three-pointers in this set are, the median release being 3.59 m. So $f(0) = +0.404$, which means the shot starts above the rim. A downward parabola still has two roots, and here they are:

RoottDescription
first−0.0787 sbefore the ball left the hand
second1.0437 sthe ball descending through the hoop plane

Both satisfy $f(t) = 0$ to machine precision, and only the second one happened. The first is where the fitted parabola, extended backwards past the frames it was fitted to, would have crossed rim height if the ball had been in free flight before release, which it was not, because it was still in someone's hand. Three consequences follow, and the demo shows all three:

Only the domain separates the two roots, and the routine does not know it. Hand bisection the flight, $[0,\,1.5]$ s, where $f(0) = +0.404$ and $f(1.5) = -3.541$ have opposite signs, so it brackets, converges, and returns 1.0437 s, which is the right answer. It works only because the lower limit was set to release.

Widen the bracket and it stops working. On $[-0.4,\,1.5]$ both ends are negative, so bisection refuses to start on a function with two roots inside the interval it was given. A sign change detects an odd number of roots, and two of them cancel.

Newton does not take a bracket, so nothing stops it. Started at 0.20 s, left of the apex at 0.482 s, the tangent points backwards and the iteration converges on −0.0787 s. It reports success, its residual is zero to machine precision, and its answer is a time before the shot was taken. Drag the starting point across the apex in the demo and watch the returned value change.

2. A Bracket with No Root

A 6 m beam carries a uniformly distributed load of 10 kN/m. It is simply supported at $x = 0$ and $x = 4$ m, so the last 2 m overhang. The reactions are 15 kN and 45 kN, and the bending moment between the supports is

$$M(x) = 15x - 5x^2 \quad \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

Where along the beam is the moment 8 kN·m? Written as a root problem by subtraction, as on page 19:

$$f(x) = M(x) - 8$$

which has roots at $x = 0.694$ m and $x = 2.306$ m. Now consider the same condition written as a ratio, which is a natural enough thing to do when you are comparing a demand against a capacity:

$$g(x) = \frac{8}{M(x)} - 1$$

$g$ and $f$ vanish at the same places. $M$ passes through zero at $x = 3$ m, though, because this beam has an overhang and so has a point of contraflexure between the supports, and at that point $g$ has a pole. Take the bracket $[2.4,\,3.7]$, where $g(2.4) = +0.111$ and $g(3.7) = -1.618$, which is a sign change. Bisection accepts it and halves 34 times, at 36 evaluations of $g$ in all, two for the ends and one per halving, and it returns

$$x = 3.00000000\ \text{m}$$

The bending moment there is zero. In the subtraction form the residual is $M(3) - 8 = -8$ kN·m, which is the full size of the target, and in the ratio form it is larger still, because $g$ is dividing by a moment of order $10^{-10}$, so $|g|$ at the returned point is about $6\times10^{10}$. Either way the value announces itself the moment anyone evaluates the function there. That is, the sign change came from the pole, and there is no root at the returned point. Handed the same bracket, the subtraction form refuses, because $f(2.4) = -0.800$ and $f(3.7) = -20.950$ have the same sign. The two forms state the same physical condition, and only the ratio form produces a bracket with no root in it.

Origin of the pole

Nothing in the beam is discontinuous. $M(x)$ is a smooth parabola over the whole span and the physical question has two ordinary answers, so the pole was introduced by the dividing, which was a step taken for convenience. This is the general shape of the failure. A rearrangement like this one keeps the roots and can still change the sign structure, and bracketing methods read sign structure.

3. A Root Outside the Structure

A 6 m simply supported beam carries 10 kN/m. Its deflection is

$$\delta(x) = \frac{w\,x\,(L^3 - 2Lx^2 + x^3)}{24EI}$$

With $EI = 1.7 \times 10^7$ N·m² the midspan deflection is 9.93 mm. Where does the deflection first reach 8 mm? Setting $f(x) = \delta(x) - 0.008$ turns that into a root problem. The answers are $x = 1.7765$ m and $x = 4.2235$ m, symmetric about midspan, and bisection on $[0.5,\,3.0]$ returns 1.7762 m in 14 evaluations, on the beam and within a quarter of a millimetre. Newton started at $x_0 = 2.90$ m, a reasonable guess near the middle where the deflection is largest, and converges in three steps to

$$x = -4.246\ \text{m}$$

and reports success. It is not wrong about the arithmetic, since $\delta(-4.246) = 0.008$ m to four figures, because $\delta$ is a quartic in $x$, whose four zeros are $-4.246$, $1.777$, $4.224$ and $10.246$ m, so two of them lie off the beam, one past each end. The beam occupies $0 \le x \le 6$, so there is no beam at $x = -4.246$ m, and the formula was never claimed to describe anything there.

The mechanism is the slope. At midspan the beam is at its lowest point, so $\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}x = 0$ exactly. Newton divides by that slope:

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}$$

Near midspan $f'$ is small, the step is large, and one step is enough to leave the beam. From $x_0 = 2.99$ m the first step lands at $-69.8$ m, while from $x_0 = 2.50$ m, far enough from the flat point, Newton converges to 1.776 m in four steps and is correct. Nothing distinguishes the two runs from the inside. Both report converged and both have a residual below tolerance, and the difference is only visible if you ask whether the answer is on the beam.

4. A Double Root

Return to the basketball, thrown so it only just reaches the rim, at a release speed of 5.84 m/s at the same angle, giving $v_0\sin\theta = 4.539$ m/s. The apex is then exactly 3.05 m. $f(t) = y(t) - 3.05$ now touches zero at $t = 0.4627$ s and is negative everywhere else. This is a double root. Bisection cannot start anywhere, because there is no pair of points on the whole trajectory with $f$ of opposite signs, since $f$ never becomes positive. A method whose guarantee rests on sign changes has nothing to work with here, and it correctly says so. Newton runs, and reports success. What it returns depends on the tolerance:

tolreturned t|f| there error in t
10⁻⁴0.45906 s6.4×10⁻⁵ 3.6 ms
10⁻⁶0.46245 s2.5×10⁻⁷ 0.2 ms
10⁻⁸0.46265 s3.9×10⁻⁹ 0.02 ms

At a tolerance of 10⁻⁴ the residual is 64 micrometres of height, an excellent-looking number, while the time is wrong by 3.6 milliseconds. Tightening the tolerance by a factor of 100 improves the time by a factor of about 10, not 100. That relationship is the general one near a double root. Because $f'$ vanishes there, $f$ is locally quadratic, so a residual $\epsilon$ corresponds to an error of order $\sqrt{\epsilon}$ and not order $\epsilon$. Where a root finder normally reports an answer far more accurate than its tolerance, here it reports one far less accurate.

The cost shows up as well. Both this case and case 1 are Newton applied to a parabola, at the same tolerance. Case 1 finishes in 7 function evaluations. This one takes 23. The dots in the demo bunch up as they approach the touch point and do not leap onto it, which is what the extra evaluations are being spent on. At a simple root Newton roughly doubles the number of correct digits each step, and at a double root it gains a fixed fraction of the remaining error instead.

Three Checks

All four failures survive the routine's own test, and all four are caught by three questions, two asked afterwards and one before. None of them requires knowing the answer.

  1. Evaluate $f$ at the returned value and look at it. Case 2 fails here immediately. The residual is 8 kN·m against a target of 8 kN·m.
  2. Ask whether the value is in the domain. Case 3 fails here, because −4.246 m is not a point on a beam running from 0 to 6 m. This check is a comparison against the physical setup, not against the mathematics, and no routine can make it unless you give it the domain.
  3. Plot $f$ over the whole domain before solving. Cases 1 and 4 are both visible in a single plot, with two crossings in one, a curve that touches and turns back in the other, and neither is visible in a returned number.

The third costs the most of the three and is likely the one most often skipped. A plot costs a few hundred function evaluations. Case 2's bracket cost 36 evaluations to produce a wrong answer that looked converged to eight decimal places.

In Code

This is the verification code, and it runs after the solver. Given whatever a routine returned, these are the three checks above, in the order above.

def verify(f, x, domain, tol=1e-8, h=1e-4):
    lo = domain[0]
    hi = domain[1]
    report = {}

    report['residual'] = abs(f(x))
    report['is_root'] = report['residual'] < tol

    report['in_domain'] = lo <= x and x <= hi

    report['crosses'] = f(x - h) * f(x + h) < 0

    return report
function r = verify(f, x, domain, tol, h)
    if nargin < 4
        tol = 1e-8;
    end
    if nargin < 5
        h = 1e-4;
    end

    r.residual  = abs(f(x));
    r.is_root   = r.residual < tol;
    r.in_domain = x >= domain(1) && x <= domain(2);
    r.crosses   = f(x - h) * f(x + h) < 0;
end
function verify(f, x, domain, tol = 1e-8, h = 1e-4) {
  const lo = domain[0];
  const hi = domain[1];
  const residual = Math.abs(f(x));
  return {
    residual: residual,
    isRoot: residual < tol,
    inDomain: x >= lo && x <= hi,
    crosses: f(x - h) * f(x + h) < 0
  };
}

That test exists only because someone wrote down what the domain was. Had the bracket started at t = 0 the routine would have returned the right answer and the check would have been redundant. That is the usual relationship between a safeguard and the bug it prevents.

Check
  1. A routine returns x = 2.5 with a residual of 10⁻¹². You evaluate f at 2.4999 and 2.5001 and find both values negative. What has the routine found, and what should you expect of the accuracy?
    Answer

    It has most likely found a double root, meaning a point where f touches zero and turns back without crossing. Both neighbours being negative rules out a simple root, at which they would have opposite signs.

    The accuracy is far worse than the residual suggests. Near a double root f ≈ C(x − r)², so a residual of 10−12 corresponds to |x − r| ≈ √(10−12/C), which is about 10−6 for C of order 1. Half the digits are gone, and the stopping test, which measures |f|, cannot see it.

    Two consequences. Bisection cannot find this root at all, because there is no sign change to bracket. Newton, which does converge here, drops from quadratic to linear, so it needs many more steps than usual for an answer that is much less accurate than usual.

  2. You are solving P(x) = Pallow for a column. Give the two formulations by subtraction and by ratio, and say which one you would hand to bisection and why.
    Answer

    Subtraction: f(x) = P(x) − Pallow. Ratio: f(x) = P(x)/Pallow − 1.

    Hand bisection the subtraction. With Pallow a constant the two differ only by a positive scale factor, so they have identical roots and identical sign changes, and the division buys nothing. The moment the denominator is not constant, whether from an allowable that depends on slenderness or from the ratio written the other way up, the division introduces a pole wherever it vanishes, and a sign change across a pole is not a root. The subtraction also keeps the units of P, so a tolerance on |f| is a tolerance in kN and means something physical. Divide, and the tolerance becomes dimensionless and its size has to be reasoned about separately.

    As a general rule, avoid introducing a division you were not given, since it cannot add a root and it can add a pole.

  3. Newton returns a value outside the domain of your problem in three steps, with a residual of 10⁻¹⁰. Is the routine faulty? What would you change to get a usable answer?
    Answer

    Not faulty. It was asked to find a place where f is zero and it found one, to 10−10, in three steps. It has no knowledge of your domain and never claimed any. Three changes, in order of what they cost:

    1. Add the domain to the loop condition, page 3's "against a bound". The iteration stops and reports that it left the region, instead of converging outside it.
    2. Start it somewhere else. Newton goes where the tangent points, so the starting point selects the root. Scan first, find the sign change in the region you care about, and start inside it.
    3. Use a bracketing or hybrid method. Then the domain is the bracket and leaving it is not possible.

    The residual of 10−10 shows that the answer satisfies the equation, and the four cases above show that this alone does not make it the answer to your problem.
