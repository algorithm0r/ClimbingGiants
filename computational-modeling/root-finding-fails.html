Week 7 · Root Finding and Search

Root-finding failures

Claim

A root-finding routine returns a value x satisfying its own stopping test, which is a bound on |f(x)| or on the width of a bracket. It does not test whether that value is the root the question asks for, or whether f is the function you meant. On a fitted formula and on a simulation, four situations produce a wrong value with no error reported, or a refusal where crossings exist.

The routine answers the question you typed, and it has no way to know the question you meant.

The Root Finder Guarantee

A root finder is given a function $f$ and returns a number. The promise attached to that number is narrow:

  • Bisection promises that its returned value lies inside an interval it maintained, and that $f$ changed sign across that interval at every step. It offers this only if it is handed a bracket to begin with.
  • Newton's method promises nothing in advance. It returns the value at which its stopping test happened to be satisfied.

Bisection stops when its bracket is narrower than a tolerance. Newton's method usually stops when

$$|f(x)| < \texttt{tol}$$

That bounds the residual, meaning how far the equation is from being satisfied at $x$. The error, how far $x$ is from the root, is a separate number. The two are related by the slope of $f$, so where the slope is small they are very different numbers.

Both tests read $f$ and the bracket, and nothing about the problem $f$ came from.

A Fitted Shot and a Simulated Slope

The week's two problems give $f$ in two different forms.

  • The shot. The question is whether a tracked three-pointer went in. The rim is 3.048 m above the floor, and the crossing is a root of $f(t) = z(t) - 3.048$, where $z(t)$ is a parabola fitted to the ball's height in free flight. $f$ is a formula, so it can be evaluated anywhere, as often as you like, for well under a microsecond each.
  • The slope. The question is the largest earthquake shaking a rock slope survives, in the Slope Stability Lab. Each run shakes the slope with a recorded earthquake scaled to a peak ground acceleration (PGA) and reports how far the slope moved. With damage taken as moving more than a threshold $d^*$, the answer is a root of $f(\text{PGA}) = \text{slope moved}(\text{PGA}) - d^*$. There is no formula. Each evaluation is one simulation on a GPU, a minute or two of machine time. The lab leaves the definition of damage to you, and its catalog suggests 5 or 15 cm.

Four Failures on Real Data

Each failure below is on real data. In each one a routine either returns a wrong value with no error reported or refuses a bracket that holds crossings.

  1. Two crossings. The fitted height crosses the rim twice, and a routine can return the crossing the question did not ask for.
  2. The root of a wrong formula. A parabola fitted to too few frames has an exact root, and the root is far from where the ball crossed.
  3. A bracket holding several crossings. The slope's movement jumps up and down with PGA, so a bracket can hold more than one crossing of the threshold, and bisection keeps whichever one its midpoints lead to.
  4. A function that answers differently twice. Two runs at the same setting can move the slope by different amounts, so the sign of $f$ at a point is a sample.

Three more failures follow on constructed functions, since neither problem shows them.

1. Two Crossings

Arc 79 is a missed three-pointer, tracked at 25 frames a second. A parabola fitted by least squares to its 24 free-flight frames, with time measured from release, is

$$z(t) = 3.4520 + 4.7428\,t - 4.9153\,t^2, \qquad f(t) = z(t) - 3.048$$

The fitted curvature corresponds to $g = 9.831$ m/s², a value the fit takes from the frames (no gravity was assumed). At release the fit puts the ball at 3.452 m (the tracked frame reads 3.475 m), above the rim, as 99% of the three-pointers in the full set are (their median release is 3.59 m). This gives $f(0) = +0.404$ m. A downward parabola released above a height crosses it twice, and here the two roots are

The tracked frames show both crossings. They rise through 3.048 m between −0.08 and −0.04 s, at −0.072 s by linear interpolation, and fall through it between 1.04 and 1.08 s, at 1.055 s. The rising root is the parabola extended backwards past the frames it was fitted to, so it lands 6 ms from the tracked crossing. The question, did the shot go in, asks for the falling one. Both satisfy $f(t) = 0$ to machine precision, so nothing in the value of $f$ separates them. Three runs show what a routine does with that.

Newton started before the apex returns the rising crossing. Started at 0.20 s, left of the apex at 0.482 s, the tangent points backwards. The estimates go 0.20, −0.216, −0.092, −0.0789 and −0.07875 s. The routine reports success with a residual of $2\times10^{-15}$ m, and its answer is a time before the ball left the hand.

A bracket around both crossings stops bisection before it starts. On $[-0.4,\,1.5]$ s, $f(-0.4) = -2.280$ and $f(1.5) = -3.541$ have the same sign, so bisection refuses a function with two roots inside the interval it was given. A sign change at the ends means an odd number of crossings inside, so two crossings leave the ends with the same sign.

A bracket around one crossing returns that crossing. On $[0,\,1.5]$ s the ends are $+0.404$ and $-3.541$, and bisection returns 1.0437 s. On $[-0.3,\,0.4]$ s it returns −0.0787 s. The bracket you hand it decides which crossing comes back.

The two roots differ in one way a routine can check. At the falling crossing $f$ goes from positive to negative, and at the rising one from negative to positive. The sign of $f$ just before and just after the returned value tells them apart, and it is the last check in verify below.

2. The Root of a Wrong Formula

To predict the crossing before the ball arrives, the parabola has to be fitted to the frames you have so far. Fitted to the first six frames after release, the first 0.20 s of flight, the same shot gives

$$z_6(t) = 3.4675 + 4.3250\,t - 3.1473\,t^2$$

The fit is a good one by its own measure. Its root-mean-square residual is 9.3 mm, half the 19 mm of the fit to all 24 frames. Newton or bisection on $f_6(t) = z_6(t) - 3.048$ returns the falling root,

$$t = 1.4651\ \text{s}$$

with a residual below $10^{-10}$ m. It is a root, it falls through zero, and it lies inside the time the tracking covers. The full fit puts the crossing at 1.0437 s. At that time the six-frame parabola still has the ball at 4.55 m, and the tracked frame there reads 3.14 m. The predicted place is 2.53 m from the hoop center, against 0.356 m from the full fit.

The routine found the root of $f_6$ exactly. $f_6$ is the wrong function, and no test a root finder makes can detect that. A check on the formula can. Its curvature corresponds to $g = 6.29$ m/s², and a ball in free flight falls at about 9.8. As frames arrive the prediction moves:

On another shot in the set, arc 48, six frames give $g = 1.77$ m/s² and a crossing at 4.03 s, 19.8 m from the hoop. A fit to the first frames reaches the crossing by extrapolation, far past the last frame it used.

With $d^* = 5$ cm the trail closes in on the first crossing. 1.5 g moved 14.3 cm, so bisection keeps the lower half, and four runs later the crossing lies between 1.25 and 1.28125 g.

With $d^* = 15$ cm, the lab's other suggestion, the first midpoint goes the other way. 14.3 cm is under 15, so 1.5 g counts as surviving and bisection keeps $[1.5,\,2]$. Every later run is above 1.5 g, and the routine returns a value between 1.5 and 2 g, where $f$ changes sign. The slope also moved more than 15 cm at 1.28125, 1.3125 and 1.375 g, all below it. The question is the largest shaking the slope survives, meaning every smaller shaking too, so it asks for the first crossing.

The movement is not monotone in PGA. On the 5.8 MPa slope the runs read 1.85 cm at 2.7 g, 307 cm at 2.75 g, 23 cm at 2.8 g, 13.5 cm at 2.9 g and 32 cm at 3.0 g. With $d^* = 15$ cm the bracket $[2.7,\,2.9]$ g has the same sign at both ends, so bisection refuses it, though the slope failed twice inside it. The shot's bracket $[-0.4,\,1.5]$ s failed the same way.

Bisection guarantees a crossing inside the bracket, and says nothing about which one. On the shot a plot of $f$ costs nothing and shows every crossing. On the slope each point of that plot is a run, so the runs you already have, sorted by PGA, are that plot at no extra cost. Evaluation cost takes up how many more to buy.

4. A Function That Answers Differently Twice

A formula returns the same value every time it is evaluated. The slope simulation usually does. The example class repeated 13 settings, and 8 of them gave identical readings. The other 5 did not:

strength (MPa)PGA (g)slope moved, each run (cm)
1.00.43756.33, 5.68, 6.33

Bisection reads one thing from each run, the sign of $f$. At 1.28125 g the two runs on the 2.6 MPa slope differ by 66 cm and still agree in sign for any threshold under 140 cm. At 0.4375 g on the 1.0 MPa slope the three runs straddle 6 cm. With a threshold anywhere between 5.68 and 6.33 cm, the same setting fails, survives and fails. Bisection decides each end of the final bracket on one run, and a reading close to the threshold is the one a repeat can flip.

Repeating a run costs another minute or two, so a repeat is worth its cost where its sign decides something, at the runs that set the final bracket. Two repeats that agree make that end of the bracket more trustworthy. Two that disagree mean the threshold sits inside the spread of the simulation, and the answer is a range of PGA however narrow the bracket gets.

A Pole, a Root off the Beam and a Double Root

Neither the tracked shots nor the slope runs show these three, so each is shown on a constructed function with a known answer.

A Bracket with No Root

A 6 m beam carries a uniformly distributed load of 10 kN/m. It is simply supported at $x = 0$ and $x = 4$ m, so the last 2 m overhang. The bending moment between the supports is $M(x) = 15x - 5x^2$ kN·m, and $M = 8$ kN·m at $x = 0.694$ and $2.306$ m. Written as a ratio, a natural form when comparing a demand against a capacity, the condition is

$$g(x) = \frac{8}{M(x)} - 1$$

$M$ passes through zero at $x = 3$ m, a point of contraflexure, and there $g$ has a pole. On the bracket $[2.4,\,3.7]$, $g(2.4) = +0.111$ and $g(3.7) = -1.618$. Bisection accepts the sign change and returns $x = 3.00000000$ m, where the moment is zero. The residual in the subtraction form is $M(3) - 8 = -8$ kN·m, the whole target. Handed the same bracket, the subtraction form refuses, because $f(2.4) = -0.800$ and $f(3.7) = -20.950$ have the same sign. Dividing by $M$ leaves the roots in place and adds a pole wherever $M$ is zero.

A Root Outside the Structure

The shot cannot show this one. Its parabola has two zeros and both lie inside the record, so Newton on the shot can leave the domain and still come back. From 0.50 s, where $f'(t) = -0.17$ m/s, the first step lands at 9.47 s, and the later steps return to 1.0437 s. A root outside the domain needs a function with more zeros than the problem has, such as a quartic.

A 6 m simply supported beam carries 10 kN/m with $EI = 1.7 \times 10^7$ N·m². Its deflection is

$$\delta(x) = \frac{w\,x\,(L^3 - 2Lx^2 + x^3)}{24EI}$$

and the deflection reaches 8 mm at $x = 1.7765$ and $4.2235$ m. Newton on $f(x) = \delta(x) - 0.008$, started at $x_0 = 2.90$ m near midspan, converges in three steps to $x = -4.246$ m and reports success. $\delta$ is a quartic, with zeros of $f$ at $-4.246$, $1.7765$, $4.2235$ and $10.246$ m, and two of them are off the beam. At midspan the slope of $\delta$ is zero, so Newton's step $f(x_k)/f'(x_k)$ is large near it. From $x_0 = 2.99$ m the first step lands at $-69.8$ m, while from $2.50$ m Newton converges to 1.7765 m. Both runs report converged, and a comparison with the beam's 0 to 6 m is what separates them.

A Double Root

A shot released at 2.00 m with 4.5388 m/s upward, a speed chosen so the apex is exactly 3.05 m, reaches it at 0.4627 s. $f(t) = \text{height} - 3.05$ touches zero there and is negative everywhere else. This is a double root, and no bracket exists anywhere. Newton runs and reports success, and what it returns depends on the tolerance:

tolreturned t|f| there error in t
10⁻⁴0.45906 s6.4×10⁻⁵ 3.6 ms
10⁻⁶0.46245 s2.5×10⁻⁷ 0.23 ms
10⁻⁸0.46265 s3.9×10⁻⁹ 0.028 ms

Tightening the tolerance by a factor of 100 improves the time by a factor of 8 to 16, close to the square root of 100. Because $f'$ vanishes at a double root, $f$ is locally quadratic, so a residual $\epsilon$ corresponds to an error of order $\sqrt{\epsilon}$. Newton also slows down there, gaining a fixed fraction of the remaining error each step, and at a tolerance of 10−6 it takes 23 evaluations from 0 s where it takes 9 on the arc 79 parabola from 0.20 s.

Checks on the Value, the Function and the Domain

No failure on this page shows in the routine's own test. Three checks can catch them, one on the returned value, one on the function, and one made before solving. None of them requires knowing the answer.

  1. Check the returned value against f and the problem. Evaluate $f$ there, compare the value with the domain, and look at the sign of $f$ on either side. The residual catches the pole, the domain catches the root off the beam, and the sign on either side catches the rising crossing and the double root.
  2. Check f against what it stands for. A fitted formula can be compared with physics you know and with data that arrive later, which is what catches the six-frame parabola and its gravity of 6.29 m/s². A simulation can be run again at the same setting, which is what shows the spread at 0.4375 g.
  3. Look at f over the whole domain before solving. On the shot a plot costs a few hundred evaluations, well under a microsecond each, and shows both crossings. On the slope the runs you already have, sorted by PGA, show the 2.6 MPa slope failing at 1.28125 g below a survival at 1.5 g.

The first check runs on whatever a routine returned and costs three evaluations. On the slope those are three more runs, and with repeat noise each one is itself a sample. The second check needs knowledge of where $f$ came from, which the routine never has.

In Code

This is the first check as code, and it runs after the solver. Given whatever a routine returned, it reports the residual, the domain and the direction of the crossing.

def verify(f, x, domain, tol=1e-8, h=1e-4):
    lo = domain[0]
    hi = domain[1]
    report = {}

    report['residual'] = abs(f(x))
    report['is_root'] = report['residual'] < tol

    report['in_domain'] = lo <= x and x <= hi

    before = f(x - h)
    after = f(x + h)
    if before < 0 and after > 0:
        report['crossing'] = 'rising'
    elif before > 0 and after < 0:
        report['crossing'] = 'falling'
    else:
        report['crossing'] = 'none'

    return report
function r = verify(f, x, domain, tol, h)
    if nargin < 4
        tol = 1e-8;
    end
    if nargin < 5
        h = 1e-4;
    end

    r.residual  = abs(f(x));
    r.is_root   = r.residual < tol;
    r.in_domain = x >= domain(1) && x <= domain(2);
    before = f(x - h);
    after  = f(x + h);
    if before < 0 && after > 0
        r.crossing = 'rising';
    elseif before > 0 && after < 0
        r.crossing = 'falling';
    else
        r.crossing = 'none';
    end
end
function verify(f, x, domain, tol = 1e-8, h = 1e-4) {
  const lo = domain[0];
  const hi = domain[1];
  const residual = Math.abs(f(x));
  const before = f(x - h);
  const after = f(x + h);
  let crossing = 'none';
  if (before < 0 && after > 0) crossing = 'rising';
  if (before > 0 && after < 0) crossing = 'falling';
  return {
    residual: residual,
    isRoot: residual < tol,
    inDomain: x >= lo && x <= hi,
    crossing: crossing
  };
}

Check
  1. A parabola fitted to the first eight frames of a shot predicts the crossing at 1.31 s, and verify reports a root, in the domain, falling. The fit's residual is 8 mm. Name one number you could compute from what you already have that would tell you whether to trust the 1.31 s.
    Answer

    The fitted gravity. The t² coefficient of the parabola is −g/2, so the fit implies a value of g. A ball in free flight falls at about 9.8 m/s², and a fit that implies 6 or 13 is extrapolating a shape the ball does not have. On arc 79 six frames give 6.29 m/s² and a crossing 0.42 s late.

    Neither the 8 mm nor verify can say this. The fit's residual measures how well the parabola matches the frames it was fitted to, and verify measures how well 1.31 s satisfies the parabola. Both can be small while the parabola is wrong where the root is. A second number to watch is how far the prediction moves when the next frame arrives.

  2. You are bisecting a slope with d* = 10 cm and the midpoint run moves the slope 9.6 cm. You have 12 runs left. What do you do with the next run?
    Answer

    Repeat the midpoint. 9.6 cm is 0.4 cm under the threshold, and in the example class repeats at one setting differed by 0.65 cm at about 6 cm of movement (5.68 against 6.33 cm at 0.4375 g). A sign decided by 0.4 cm may flip on a second run, and bisection discards half the bracket on that sign.

    If the repeat agrees, the bracket moves as planned and the repeat cost one run. If it disagrees, the threshold sits inside the spread of the simulation at that PGA, and your answer is that PGA with a width around it, which more halving cannot narrow. Either way, look at the runs you already have, sorted by PGA, for a failure below the bracket.

  3. You are solving P(x) = Pallow for a column. Give the two formulations by subtraction and by ratio, and say which one you would hand to bisection and why.
    Answer

    Subtraction: f(x) = P(x) − Pallow. Ratio: f(x) = P(x)/Pallow − 1.

    Hand bisection the subtraction. With Pallow a constant the two differ only by a positive scale factor, so they have identical roots and identical sign changes, and the division changes nothing. The moment the denominator is not constant, whether from an allowable that depends on slenderness or from the ratio written the other way up, the division introduces a pole wherever it vanishes, and a sign change across a pole is not a root. The subtraction also keeps the units of P, so a tolerance on |f| is a tolerance in kN and means something physical. Divide, and the tolerance becomes dimensionless and its size has to be reasoned about separately.

  4. Newton returns a value outside the domain of your problem in three steps, with a residual of 10⁻¹⁰. Is the routine faulty? What would you change to get a usable answer?
    Answer

    Not faulty. It was asked to find a place where f is zero and it found one, to 10−10, in three steps. It has no knowledge of your domain and never claimed any. Three changes, in order of what they cost:

    1. Add the domain to the loop condition, page 3's "against a bound". The iteration stops and reports that it left the region, instead of converging outside it.
    2. Start it somewhere else. Newton goes where the tangent points, so the starting point selects the root. Scan first, find the sign change in the region you care about, and start inside it.
    3. Use a bracketing or hybrid method. Then the domain is the bracket and leaving it is not possible.

    The residual of 10−10 shows that the answer satisfies the equation, and the cases above show that this alone does not make it the answer to your problem.
