Interpolation and regression

Claim

Interpolation constructs a curve that passes through every data point. Regression constructs one that does not, and is not meant to. The two are applied to the same inputs and encode opposite assumptions about them. Interpolation assumes the values are exact, regression assumes they carry error. Choosing wrongly can produce a confident curve that is wrong between the points.

Going through every point is a virtue if the points are exact and a fault if they are noisy, and the data do not say which they are.

The third row is the mechanical difference and the second is the one that decides. An interpolating curve has exactly enough freedom to hit every point, so it must hit them. A regression has less freedom and usually cannot, so it averages.

Interpolation Versus Regression

Eleven points from a parabola with noise of σ = 0.02. The quadratic regression has exactly the right form for the underlying curve, so what separates the three methods below is only how each one treats the noise.

quadratic regression3 of 110.0185 0.00420.0086

Both interpolants have a residual of exactly zero and neither is the most accurate. The regression, which honours none of the points, is closest to the curve they came from (twenty-four times closer than the interpolant that passes through every one of them). Its residual of 0.0185 is roughly the noise the fit declined to follow. That makes the residual an estimate of σ, which is a good deal more useful than a zero. Nothing in the eleven points themselves distinguishes the cases. The interpolants' zero residual is not evidence in their favour, and the regression's non-zero residual is not evidence against it. The ranking above is only available because the truth is known here, which on your own data it usually will not be.

Runge's phenomenon

The swings of the degree-10 interpolant above are it following the noise. A high-degree interpolant can also misbehave near the ends with exact data, though. Carl Runge showed in 1901 that interpolating $1/(1 + 25x^2)$ at equally spaced points gives an error that grows without limit as points are added. Adding data makes it worse, which is the opposite of what intuition offers, and it is why high-degree polynomial interpolation is rarely used in practice. Splines, which are low-degree polynomials on each interval joined smoothly, are what is used instead, and they do not have the problem.

Kinds of Interpolation

Optional, open to read

When the values are exact, as in a tabulated material property or a lookup table from a standard, interpolation is the right operation and the only question is which kind:

  • Linear. Join adjacent points with straight lines. Cannot oscillate, has a kink at every point, and the error is bounded by the second derivative times the spacing squared. Almost always the right first choice.
  • Cubic spline. A cubic on each interval, matched in value and first two derivatives at the joins. Smooth and local, so moving one point changes the curve mostly near it and very little at the far end, and free of Runge's problem.
  • Single high-degree polynomial. One polynomial through all $n$ points. Global, oscillatory, and on equally spaced points worse the more points you have. Almost never what you want.

Choosing by Provenance

Asking "which fits better" does not help, because the interpolant always fits the points at least as well by construction. The question is where did these numbers come from:

  • Read off an instrument, tracked from video, sampled from a sensor. They carry error, and a curve that honours each one exactly is honouring the error too. Regress.
  • Taken from a table of standard values, or computed exactly from a formula. They are treated as exact, and a curve that misses them is losing information you had. Interpolate.

The failure mode is interpolating measured data because the zero residual looks like success, which is the same misreading as the previous page arriving through a different door.

Check
  1. You are given eleven points and a curve through all eleven, with residuals of zero. What does that tell you about the curve's accuracy between the points?
    Answer

    Nothing. An interpolant is constructed to pass through every point. Zero residuals follow from the definition of the method, so they would be zero for any eleven points. Between the points the curve is unconstrained by the data and controlled by the model form. For a degree-ten polynomial on equally spaced nodes, what it does between them is likely to be bad and worst near the ends, with swings far larger than anything in the data, from a curve that matches every measurement exactly. To say anything about accuracy between the points you need something the fit cannot supply, such as a held-out point, a bound on the derivative, or a physical argument about how the quantity can behave.

  2. You need a material property at 340 °C from a table giving values every 50 °C. Interpolate or regress? What if you had measured those values yourself with a thermocouple?
    Answer

    Interpolate the published table. Its values are the best available statements of the property and are quoted as exact. A curve that missed them would be substituting your judgement for the compiler's. Use a low-order local interpolant, linear or cubic between the neighbouring entries, not one polynomial through the whole table.

    Regress your own measurements. A thermocouple reading carries noise, so the values are draws around the true property, and a curve forced through every one of them is a curve fitted to your noise. Fitting a smooth low-order model averages much of the error out and will likely predict better at 340 °C than any of your individual points would.

    The two methods use similar mathematics and make opposite assumptions. Interpolation says the values are exact and regression says they carry error, and which of the two is true is a fact about where the numbers came from. The numbers themselves do not record it.

  3. Adding more points makes a high-degree polynomial interpolant worse. Explain to a colleague why that is not a contradiction.
    Answer

    Adding a point to an interpolant changes the model as well as adding data. With n points the interpolant is the polynomial of degree n − 1, so every extra point makes it one degree more flexible.

    The comparison being imagined is "more data, same model, better answer", which is true. What happens here is "more data, more flexible model", and the added flexibility costs more than the added information is worth. On equally spaced nodes the extra degrees go into oscillation near the ends, and for a function like Runge's the error there grows without limit as points are added. Both fixes restore the intuition. Fix the degree and regress, and more points then improve a model of unchanged flexibility. Or choose where to sample, clustering the nodes towards the ends as Chebyshev points do, and for a smooth function the interpolant converges as more points are added. "More data is better" applies to estimating a fixed set of parameters, and does not apply to a model that grows with the data.
