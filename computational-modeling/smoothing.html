Smoothing and phase lag

Video: Smoothing Noise from a Channel (0:06:22). Slides

Claim

Smoothing replaces each sample by a weighted combination of its neighbours, which reduces the noise by averaging it and generally reduces the signal by averaging that too. The total error against the true signal therefore has a minimum at a finite window width. Too narrow leaves noise, and too wide removes the thing being measured. A frequency-specified filter, such as the standard's, applied once in the direction of increasing time, also shifts events later. The shift is the phase lag, it grows as the cut-off frequency is lowered, and it is present in the output with nothing to mark it. Applying the same filter a second time in reverse produces an equal shift in the opposite direction, and the two cancel exactly.

Smoothing is a trade. It buys a reduction in noise with a distortion of the signal, and there is usually a width that buys the most for the least. A filter that only knows the past has to wait before it can react, and running it backwards afterwards undoes the waiting, at the price of a filter that uses the future.

The Simplest Smoother

A moving average of width $w$ replaces each sample by the mean of the $w$ samples centred on it:

$$\tilde y_i \;=\; \frac{1}{w} \sum_{k=-(w-1)/2}^{(w-1)/2} y_{i+k}$$

Two consequences follow from that one line.

The noise is reduced by $\sqrt{w}$. Averaging $w$ independent values of standard deviation $\sigma$ gives one of standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{w}$, which is the same square-root law that governs accumulation in week 3, used deliberately this time.

The square root is what makes this expensive. Halving the noise means dividing $\sqrt{w}$ by two, so the width has to go up by a factor of four:

$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{w}} \;\to\; \frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{w}} \qquad\text{needs}\qquad \sqrt{4w} \;=\; 2\sqrt{w}$$

A window of 25 leaves a fifth of the noise, since $\sqrt{25} = 5$. Going from 25 to 100 quadruples the width and halves the noise again, to a tenth. The halving after that costs a window of 400. Each halving costs four times the width of the one before it, and the second consequence below is what that width does to the signal.

The signal is reduced too. The average of a curve over a window is not the value of the curve at the centre unless the curve is straight there, so wherever it bends, which is to say at every peak and every trough, the average is pulled toward the surroundings and the peak comes out lower than it is. The peak is commonly the number you were after.

The second column falls, reaches a minimum at $w = 9$, and then rises past the unsmoothed value. By $w = 101$ the smoothed signal is seven times further from the truth than the raw noisy one, so the smoothing has done a good deal more damage than the noise it removed. The fourth column is the noise reduction, $1/\sqrt{w}$, and it improves in every row, all the way down, which is where the trap sits. The quantity smoothing is chosen for keeps getting better while the quantity actually wanted gets worse, and a person watching only the smoothness of the plot has no signal at all that anything has gone wrong.

The third column says where the damage lands. The peak falls monotonically, 1.0262, 1.0036, 1.0014, 1.0006, 0.9964, 0.9839, 0.9347, because a wider window averages more of the descent either side of the peak into it. It passes through the true value of 1.0000 between $w = 13$ (1.0001) and $w = 15$ (0.9993), which is a coincidence of this signal and this noise and not a width to choose.

The two error columns do not agree on the best width, and the reason is worth sitting with. The RMS column is smallest at $w = 9$, and it rises at $w = 11$. The peak column is closer to the truth at $w = 11$ than at $w = 9$. Both are correct. Widening from 9 to 11 removes a little more noise everywhere and flattens the peak a little more, and for this signal the first effect is smaller than the second across the record as a whole, while at the single instant of the peak the two happen to cancel more completely.

That is the same lesson as the filter classes on the next page, arriving early. "Best" is not a property of the width. It is a property of the width and the question together. If you are reporting the whole trace, $w = 9$ is the better choice here. If you are reporting the peak and nothing else, a wider window is. Choose the width against the quantity you are going to report, and say which one you chose.

The Width Is a Decision

The smoothing minimum cannot be predicted, because the distortion term depends on how sharply the signal bends, which is a property of the thing being measured and is not known in advance. The minimum in the table above is 9 for this sine, measured on the whole record. For a sharper signal it is narrower, and there is no formula that will tell you. What follows is that the width is a decision, made by a person, that changes the reported answer.

The rest of this page follows the standard's filter, which is specified by frequency instead of by width, and the delay that comes from filtering in one direction, with the second pass that removes it. The filter class page returns to the decision on this page, how hard to smooth, for crash data, where the class is set by what the measurement is for.

A moving average is a crude filter

A rectangular window is the simplest weighting and not a good one. It responds to some frequencies it should reject and rejects some it should pass. Better weightings, such as Gaussian, Savitzky-Golay and Butterworth, trade a little more computation for a much cleaner separation.

The trade-off this page describes survives all of them. Every smoother reduces noise by mixing neighbouring samples, and most therefore distort anything that varies within the region they mix over. The shape of the U-curve changes. Its existence does not.

The Standard's Filter

Everything above specified a smoother by its width: average nine samples. Measurement standards specify one the other way round, by frequency: keep everything that wiggles slower than 180 times a second and remove everything faster. Two reasons for the change, and both matter for choosing a class in the assignment.

  • Frequency is what the physics is stated in. What you want to keep is whatever the thing you are measuring actually responds to, and that is a rate, as the filter class page works through. There is no sample count that means "what a thorax responds to".
  • A width does not travel between records. Nine samples is 0.7 ms at 12 500 Hz and 9 ms at 1 kHz, so the same code does two different things to two recordings of the same event. "Remove above 180 Hz" means the same thing on both.

Asking for a sharp frequency cut-off changes the shape of the arithmetic. A moving average builds each output only from inputs, and to get a sharp cut-off that way you need hundreds of coefficients. The standard's filter instead computes each output from a few recent inputs and the outputs it has already produced, feeding its own results back in. That buys the same sharpness for five numbers, and the trade is the familiar one: keep a little state and you do far less work.

Appendix C of SAE J211 gives a four-pole Butterworth low-pass, and the standard says it should be used for CFC 60 and 180. "Four-pole" is how steeply it cuts, a compromise between a sharp edge and the ringing a sharper one would add, and "Butterworth" is the family that keeps the passband flat so the content you meant to keep comes through at its true size. The number in its name is the channel frequency class. The rule below is one pass of the filter, and one pass is two poles. The standard gets its four by running the same rule twice, once forward and once backward, as the section on forward-backward filtering below describes. The rule gives each output sample in terms of the inputs and the outputs before it:

$$y_i \;=\; a_0 x_i + a_1 x_{i-1} + a_2 x_{i-2} + b_1 y_{i-1} + b_2 y_{i-2}$$

The five coefficients come from the class and the sample interval $T$, and from nothing else. Deriving them is filter design and is outside this course. Using them is not, and the point to hold on to is that they are fully determined: two analysts who implement this correctly get identical output, which is the whole purpose of putting a filter in a standard.

$$\omega_d = 2\pi \cdot \mathrm{CFC} \cdot 2.0775, \qquad \omega_a = \tan\!\left(\frac{\omega_d T}{2}\right)$$ $$a_0 = \frac{\omega_a^2}{1 + \sqrt{2}\,\omega_a + \omega_a^2}, \qquad a_1 = 2a_0, \qquad a_2 = a_0$$ $$b_1 = \frac{-2(\omega_a^2 - 1)}{1 + \sqrt{2}\,\omega_a + \omega_a^2}, \qquad b_2 = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{2}\,\omega_a - \omega_a^2}{1 + \sqrt{2}\,\omega_a + \omega_a^2}$$

Every output sample needs the two before it, so the samples have to be produced in order. A moving average may be computed at any sample you like, in any order, and this one cannot.

Checking your implementation

Five coefficients and two feedback terms give a lot of room for a sign error, and a wrong filter usually still produces a plausible-looking curve. One check catches most mistakes and takes a line of code. Feed the filter a constant. A low-pass filter must pass a constant through unchanged, because a constant has no frequency content to remove, so filtering an array of 1.0 must return 1.0.

In terms of the coefficients, that says

$$\frac{a_0 + a_1 + a_2}{1 - b_1 - b_2} \;=\; 1$$

and it holds to the last bit of double precision for every class. If your coefficients give anything else, the error is in the coefficients and not in the record. The second check is to filter the record twice at a very high class and confirm almost nothing changes, since a filter that removes nothing should return what it was given.

Why a Causal Filter Lags

One pass of the standard's filter computes each output sample from samples at or before that instant. Every term on the right of the rule above is at time $i$ or earlier. Such a filter is called causal, and the constraint is a physical one and not a choice, because a real-time filter in an airbag controller or a control loop has nothing else available to it. The consequence follows immediately. When the input rises sharply the output at that instant is still mostly made of earlier, lower samples, so it rises later, and the sharper the feature and the heavier the filter, the further behind the output falls.

The centred moving average at the start of this page is not causal. It uses as many samples after each point as before it, so it weighs the future and the past alike and has no delay to correct. The lag comes from looking in one direction, and one pass of the CFC filter looks in one direction only.

Measuring the Lag

On a real record there is usually no independently known event time to measure against, so the lag has to be measured on a signal whose timing is known by construction: a half-sine pulse 20 ms wide, starting at 20 ms, with its peak at exactly 30.000 ms.

That quantisation is why the numbers are exact multiples of 0.08, and it is the same limit discrete evaluation describes, since a peak is located only as precisely as the sampling allows.

Forward-Backward Filtering

The standard's answer for this filter is to run it forward over the record and then run the same filter (the same class, and so the same coefficients) backward over its own output. The second pass delays by the same amount in the opposite direction, so the two shifts cancel exactly. The cost is that the result is no longer causal, because a value at 30 ms now depends on samples recorded at 80 ms. That is legitimate for a record already on disk and impossible for a controller acting in real time, which is why the same signal is filtered differently by an airbag computer during the crash and by an analyst afterwards.

In Code

def cfc(y, dt, cls):
    wd = 2 * np.pi * cls * 2.0775
    wa = np.tan(wd * dt / 2)
    d  = 1 + np.sqrt(2) * wa + wa**2
    a0 = wa**2 / d;  a1 = 2 * a0;  a2 = a0
    b1 = -2 * (wa**2 - 1) / d
    b2 = (-1 + np.sqrt(2) * wa - wa**2) / d

    def one_pass(x):
        out = np.zeros_like(x)
        for i in range(2, len(x)):
            out[i] = (a0*x[i] + a1*x[i-1] + a2*x[i-2]
                      + b1*out[i-1] + b2*out[i-2])
        return out

    return one_pass(one_pass(y)[::-1])[::-1]
function out = cfc(y, dt, cls)
    wd = 2*pi*cls*2.0775;
    wa = tan(wd*dt/2);
    d  = 1 + sqrt(2)*wa + wa^2;
    c  = [wa^2/d, 2*wa^2/d, wa^2/d, ...
          -2*(wa^2-1)/d, (-1+sqrt(2)*wa-wa^2)/d];
    out = flip(one_pass(flip(one_pass(y, c)), c));
end

function out = one_pass(x, c)
    out = zeros(size(x));
    for i = 3:numel(x)
        out(i) = c(1)*x(i) + c(2)*x(i-1) + c(3)*x(i-2) ...
               + c(4)*out(i-1) + c(5)*out(i-2);
    end
end
function cfc(y, dt, cls) {
  const wd = 2*Math.PI*cls*2.0775, wa = Math.tan(wd*dt/2);
  const d = 1 + Math.SQRT2*wa + wa*wa;
  const a0 = wa*wa/d, a1 = 2*a0, a2 = a0;
  const b1 = -2*(wa*wa - 1)/d;
  const b2 = (-1 + Math.SQRT2*wa - wa*wa)/d;
  const onePass = x => {
    const o = new Float64Array(x.length);
    for (let i = 2; i < x.length; i++)
      o[i] = a0*x[i] + a1*x[i-1] + a2*x[i-2] + b1*o[i-1] + b2*o[i-2];
    return o;
  };
  return onePass(onePass(y).reverse()).reverse();
}

The Cost of Two Passes

The two side by side:

One passForward then backward
timinglagsno shift
uses future samplesnoyes
usable in real timeyesno
usable on a stored recordyesyes
effective sharpnesstwo polesfour poles, which is what the class specifies

The last row is the one that catches people. Each pass applies the same two-pole filter, so running it twice applies four poles in total. Four poles is what the class specifies, so it is the single-pass version that is the odd one out: a one-pass CFC 180 leaves more high-frequency content in the record than a CFC 180 is meant to. The two are not the same filter with the timing fixed.

So a one-pass CFC 180 and a forward-backward CFC 180 differ in two ways at once: the lag, and the amount of filtering. If you compare their outputs and attribute the whole difference to the lag, you will be wrong by however much the extra two poles removed. To see the lag on its own, compare the forward-backward output against the same output shifted, or against a one-pass filter designed to have the sharpness the two passes actually produce.

The standard

For CFC 60 and 180, SAE J211-1 defines the class by the filter of its Appendix C. That filter passes the two-pole rule above over the data twice to make four poles, forward and then backward, so that it "will not phase shift the data," and backward then forward gives the same result. A single pass is a different filter, two-pole and late, and it leaves nothing in the output to say so. The standard also asks that the type of filter be reported. See The standards behind the crash data.

When the Lag Changes the Answer

The lag changes the answer exactly when a time is part of the answer.

  • It matters for anything that reports when: the time of a peak, the interval between two events on differently filtered channels, the moment an airbag should fire. A 0.64 ms error is meaningless in one context and disqualifying in another.
  • It does not matter for an integral over the whole record. Shifting a curve in time does not change the area under it, so Δv is unaffected by the lag, though it is affected by the filtering itself, which is the next page.

Two channels filtered at different classes are shifted by different amounts, so any comparison between them acquires a timing error equal to the difference. That is a mistake that produces plausible numbers, and it is the reason a standard specifies the class per measurement instead of leaving it to whoever is analysing.

The Minimum Rate

The class you ask for and the rate you recorded at are not independent, and the constraint between them is one you have already met. A filter set to keep everything below some frequency is a promise about content at that frequency, and a record cannot make that promise above its Nyquist frequency, which is half its sample rate. Asking for a class above Nyquist is asking the record to preserve something it was never able to represent.

The coefficient formula in the standard's filter enforces this by itself. The $\tan$ in the coefficients passes through infinity at $\omega_d T/2 = \pi/2$, and putting $\omega_d = 2\pi \cdot \mathrm{CFC} \cdot 2.0775$ into that gives the limit directly:

$$\frac{\omega_d T}{2} < \frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_s \;>\; 2 \times 2.0775 \times \mathrm{CFC} \;=\; 4.155\,\mathrm{CFC}$$

The 2.0775 is the standard's own conversion from the class number to the design frequency the coefficients are built around. Taking it as given, the rule to carry into the assignment is that the sample rate must exceed about 4.16 times the class:

classCFC 1000CFC 600 CFC 180CFC 60
cannot be built below4155 Hz2493 Hz 748 Hz249 Hz

Below that rate the coefficients do not degrade gracefully. They change sign, and the output stops being a smoothed version of anything. Nobody imposed this as a rule and the algebra forces it, so it sets a floor on the logger before any argument about accuracy has begun.

The crash channel is recorded at 12 500 Hz, which supports classes up to about CFC 3000, so every class in the standard is available on it and the constraint never binds. It binds the moment you reduce the rate. A record thinned as in discrete evaluation to 1 kHz still supports CFC 180 and can no longer be filtered at CFC 1000 at all, which is worth checking before you decimate anything you intend to filter afterwards.

The standard

On a crash record the starting point is SAE J211-1 Table 1, which recommends a class for each kind of measurement and use: CFC 1000 for head acceleration, CFC 180 for thorax spine, and for vehicle structure CFC 60 for comparing vehicles or for collision simulation input, CFC 600 for component analysis and CFC 180 before integrating. Check
  1. Widening a moving average from 9 to 36 samples. By what factor does the noise fall, and what would you check before accepting the result?
    Answer

    By 2. Averaging w samples divides the noise by √w, and √(36/9) = 2. Quadrupling the window buys one halving, which is the same diminishing return integration gives.

    Before accepting it, check what the window did to the signal. At 12.5 kHz, 36 samples is 2.9 ms, comparable to the width of a crash pulse feature, so the averaging is no longer acting only on the noise. Compare the smoothed peak against the unsmoothed one. If it has dropped, the window is eating signal, and the drop is the part of the "improvement" you paid for. Noise reduction is measurable and free to quote. Signal attenuation has to be looked for.

  2. Between w = 11 and w = 25 the smoothed peak passes through the true 1.0000, and w = 13 comes closest (1.0001). Why would choosing that width be a mistake?
    Answer

    Because you would be choosing the parameter to reproduce the answer, and the answer then stops being evidence for anything.

    Two errors are moving in opposite directions as w grows. Noise is being suppressed and signal is being flattened. At one width they happen to cancel. That cancellation is a property of this record, this noise realisation and this peak. A different sample of the same experiment would cancel at a different width, and you would have no way to find it without already knowing the true peak. It is also unjustifiable to a reader. "I chose w = 13" invites the question "why", and the honest answer is "because it gave 1.0001", which is the same as having no method at all.

  3. A colleague filters a 10 kHz record at CFC 1000 in a single forward pass, reads off the time of the peak, and reports it. What is wrong with the time, and would comparing it against the unfiltered record reveal it?
    Answer

    The reported time is late. A single pass delays every feature, so the peak in the output sits after the peak in the record. The standard's second, backward pass exists to remove exactly this.

    Comparing against the unfiltered record does reveal it, and that is the one comparison worth making, because the delay is visible only against something that has not been through the filter. What will not reveal it is any check done inside the filtered signal. The shape is intact, the peak is smooth and well formed, and every interval between features is preserved. A delay shifts everything equally, so nothing internal to the output is inconsistent with anything else in it. On a crash record the shift is a fraction of a millisecond at CFC 1000 and grows as the class falls, which is small enough to survive review and large enough to matter when the number being reported is a time.

  4. You filter a channel at CFC 180 in one pass and read the peak at 24.64 ms. What is your best estimate of the true peak time, and how confident are you in it?
    Answer

    The true peak is earlier than 24.64 ms, and the way to say how much earlier is to measure it and not look it up: filter the same record again forward-and-backward and read the peak from that. The two passes shift by equal and opposite amounts, so the second reading carries no lag, and the difference between them is this filter's delay on this record. On the record tabulated on the next page that comparison gives 24.0 ms, so the lag is about 0.6 ms.

    A heavier filter can change which feature is the largest, which is the next page. Confidence in the magnitude is much lower, because the delay depends on the class, on the implementation, and on the shape of the pulse. That asymmetry is worth reporting as it stands. "no later than 24.64 ms, and about 0.6 ms before it" says more than a single number.

  5. Why can a real-time controller not use the forward-backward method?
    Answer

    Because the backward pass runs from the end of the record, and a controller has no end. To filter sample i in reverse you need the samples after it, and in real time those have not happened yet. Forward-backward filtering is the same filter run twice, and the cancellation is bought entirely with knowledge of the future, which is available to an analyst working on a finished record and never to a controller, so the controller must accept the lag and design around it, which is why control engineers care about phase margin, and why an airbag decision is made on a one-pass filter with its delay budgeted for.

  6. Two channels are filtered at CFC 600 and CFC 60, both one pass, and you report the interval between their peaks. What error have you introduced, and in which direction?
    Answer

    You have introduced a spurious difference in timing, because the two channels have been delayed by different amounts. The heavier filter lags more, so CFC 60 is delayed substantially more than CFC 600.

    The direction follows from which event you put where. The CFC 60 peak is pushed later than it truly is, so if that is the second of the two events, the reported interval is too long. If it is the first, the interval is too short, and it can be driven through zero and reverse the apparent order of two events. The obvious fix is to correct for the delays afterwards, though the better one is to remove the asymmetry altogether, by filtering both channels at the same class or by filtering both forward-and-backward. Any comparison of timing across channels requires identical processing on each.
