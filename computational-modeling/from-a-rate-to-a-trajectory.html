Week 8 · Time Stepping

From a rate to a trajectory

Claim

When the rate of change of a quantity is known as a function of the quantity itself, the trajectory is obtained by stepping: evaluate the rate at the current state, advance by the rate times a small step, and repeat. That is Euler's method, and it is the cumulative integral of week 3 with the integrand recomputed at every step and not read from a record.

You do not have the rate as a list of numbers any more. You have a rule that gives the rate from where you are, so you have to go there before you can ask.

The Difference from Week 3

In week 3 the acceleration was a recorded array, so every value was available before any integration started and the whole cumulative sum could be formed in one pass. Here the rate is a function of the state:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}t} \;=\; f(t, y)$$

The rate at $t_1$ cannot be computed until $y(t_1)$ is known, and $y(t_1)$ cannot be computed without the rate. The resolution is to alternate between the two: use the rate where you are to get where you go next, and then ask again.

Euler's Method

$$y_{k+1} \;=\; y_k \;+\; h\, f(t_k, y_k)$$

Read it as the rectangle rule from week 3, where the change over one step is the rate times the step width, with the rate taken at the left-hand end. It is the same approximation and it has the same weakness, because the rate is treated as constant across the step when it is not. The difference is that the error now compounds. In week 3 a bad panel affected only the total, while a bad step here moves the state, so the next rate is evaluated in the wrong place and every subsequent step inherits the mistake.

Three figures, not four: an independent Python implementation of the same method agrees with the demo to three and differs in the fourth, because the two accumulate the same sum in a different order. Quoting a digit that two correct implementations disagree about would be claiming precision that is not there. Halving the step halves the error, so Euler is first order, and that is poor. An error of 0.49 m at $h = 0.1$ on a flight that peaks at 3.97 m is not a small correction, and reaching a millimetre would need a step of about 25 microseconds, which is 40 000 steps for one second of flight. The instinct is to take a smaller step, and the remedy is usually a better rule. Week 8's later pages are about what "better" costs and how to check that you have it.

Why Euler always undershoots here

The projectile's height is concave, so the curve bends downwards everywhere. Euler follows the tangent at the left of each step, and a tangent to a concave curve lies above it, so every step overshoots the height and the accumulated result is too high. The error has a consistent sign and not a random one. It is not noise and it does not average out. A hundred steps do not partly cancel the way a hundred noise samples do, they add up in the same direction, which is the difference between this and week 3's √n.

Choosing the Step

In week 3 the interval was the instrument's and was fixed, while here it is yours, and choosing it is the work:

  • Too large and the answer is wrong, possibly wildly.
  • Too small and the run is slow, and, from week 4's error minimum, eventually less accurate once rounding dominates.
  • There is no formula that gives the right value, because it depends on how fast the solution is changing, which is what you are trying to find out.

What can be done is measure: halve the step, see whether the answer moves, and stop when halving it again changes nothing you care about. That is a convergence study, and it is the honest way to defend a step size when there is no exact answer to compare against.

Check
  1. Euler with h = 0.02 gives 3.19 m and with h = 0.01 gives 3.25 m. Estimate the answer at h = 0, and say what property of the method you used.
    Answer

    3.31 m.

    Euler is first order, so the error is proportional to h. Writing I(h) = I + Ch, the two runs give I + 0.02C = 3.19 and I + 0.01C = 3.25. Subtracting, 0.01C = −0.06, and I = 2 × 3.25 − 3.19 = 3.31 m.

    The property used is first-order convergence, and it is the whole of the step. Note the extrapolation is outside the two values, not between them. Both runs are on the same side of the answer, so the estimate is further out than either. Averaging them, the instinct when two runs disagree, gives 3.22 and is worse than the finer run alone. The same manoeuvre with a factor of 4 in place of 2 is the trapezoid extrapolation on page 5. What changes between them is only the order.

  2. Why does an error in one Euler step affect every later step, when an error in one panel of a week-3 integral does not?
    Answer

    Because of what each step is computed from.

    A quadrature panel is computed from the data. The area of panel 40 depends on the samples at panel 40 and on nothing else. The panels are independent terms in a sum, so an error in one is an error in one, and the total is off by that amount and no more. Errors add.

    An Euler step is computed from the result of the previous step. The state at step 40 is the starting point for step 41, so an error made at step 40 displaces the trajectory, and every subsequent step is taken from the wrong place, evaluating the rate at a state the system never occupied. Errors propagate, and can grow. This is the structural difference between quadrature and time stepping, and it is why an ODE solver can be unstable and a quadrature rule cannot. It is also why the global error of an order-p method is hp and not hp+1: the local error per step is one order better, and accumulating over 1/h steps gives back the order that was won.

  3. Your solution is convex and not concave. Which way does Euler err, and why?
    Answer

    It underestimates. Euler steps along the tangent at the current point. For a convex solution, meaning one curving upward with y″ > 0, the tangent lies below the curve everywhere except where it touches, so each step lands low, and the errors are of one sign, so they accumulate and do not cancel. For a concave solution the tangent lies above and Euler overestimates. The rule is that Euler errs on the side away from the curvature, and the sign of the error is knowable in advance from the sign of y″ without running anything. Which is a useful thing to have. A method whose error has a consistent sign can be bracketed. Run it and something that errs the other way, and the answer is between them, and that is a much stronger statement than an error estimate.

In Code

def euler(f, s0, t0, dt, n):
    s = np.array(s0, dtype=float)
    out = np.empty((n + 1, s.size))
    out[0] = s
    for k in range(n):
        s = s + dt * f(t0 + k*dt, s)
        out[k + 1] = s
    return out
function out = euler(f, s0, t0, dt, n)
    s = s0(:).';
    out = zeros(n+1, numel(s));
    out(1,:) = s;
    for k = 0:n-1
        s = s + dt * f(t0 + k*dt, s);
        out(k+2,:) = s;
    end
end
function euler(f, s0, t0, dt, n) {
  let s = Float64Array.from(s0);
  const out = [s.slice()];
  for (let k = 0; k < n; k++) {
    const ds = f(t0 + k*dt, s);
    s = s.map((v, i) => v + dt*ds[i]);
    out.push(s.slice());
  }
  return out;
}
