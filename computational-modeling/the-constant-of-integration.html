Week 3 · Numerical Integration

The constant of integration

Video: The Constant of Integration (0:06:19). Slides

Claim

Integration determines a curve only up to an additive constant: if $F$ is an integral of $f$, so is $F + C$ for every constant $C$. The data fix the shape of the curve and do not fix its position. An initial condition, one known value of the integrated quantity at one instant, supplies $C$.

The integral tells you how much things changed, and it cannot tell you what you started with. Something outside the record has to supply that.

The Undetermined Constant

Differentiating removes a constant, because the derivative of a constant is zero, so integration running the other way cannot recover one. Stated formally, if $F' = f$ then $(F + C)' = f$ as well, so every value of $C$ gives a curve with the same derivative and the same claim to be "the" integral. In the sampled version the same thing is visible in the arithmetic. The cumulative sum on the previous page began by setting $S_0 = 0$:

$$S_k \;=\; S_{k-1} + \frac{y_k + y_{k-1}}{2}\Delta t, \qquad S_0 = 0$$

Nothing in the data chose the value the curve starts from. $S_0 = 0$ is where the array begins, and adding any constant to the whole curve produces a curve of identical shape sitting somewhere else. That is, the height of a cumulative sum is a property of the code that built it. It tells you nothing about the vehicle.

Which Quantity the Integral Gives

The distinction that matters in practice is between the two quantities the integral could be:

QuantityDetermined by the Record?Needs
change in velocity, Δvyesnothing further
velocitynothe velocity at one known instant

An accelerometer measures acceleration. It does not measure velocity. Integrating it gives how much the velocity changed, which is a real, complete answer to a real question, and for crash severity it is usually the question, because Δv is the standard measure. If what is wanted is the speed of the vehicle and not the change in its speed, the record is not enough and something else must supply the missing number.

Supplying the Constant

Move the slider. The shape of the curve does not change, and only where it sits on the axis does.

For this test the constant is known independently. The vehicle was towed into the barrier at a measured 15.65 m/s, so its speed at the instant of impact is 15.65 m/s and that is the initial condition. Setting $C$ to that value converts the change-in-velocity curve into a speed curve, and the zero crossing and the rebound become readable. Three ways of getting the constant, in descending order of how much they are worth:

  1. Measured independently. Here, a tow speed recorded by the laboratory. It has its own uncertainty, which propagates directly into every value of the output.
  2. Implied by the physics. A body known to be at rest at some instant fixes the constant at that instant. A vehicle that has come to a stop, a beam before loading.
  3. Set to zero and stated. Legitimate when the quantity of interest is the change. Not legitimate silently, because a reader cannot tell a deliberate zero from a forgotten one.

The Second Integration

Integrating twice needs two constants, and they are not the same kind of thing. Integrating acceleration to velocity needs an initial velocity. Integrating that velocity to displacement needs an initial position. Getting the first wrong does more damage than getting the second wrong. An error $\delta$ in the initial velocity becomes an error $\delta t$ in the displacement, growing without limit, while an error in the initial position stays exactly what it was.

$$x(t) \;=\; x_0 \;+\; v_0 t \;+\; \int_0^t\!\!\int_0^{\tau} a \,\mathrm{d}t' \,\mathrm{d}\tau$$

The $v_0 t$ term is the one to watch. It is the same shape as the offset error from week 3's other page, arising for a different reason, and it is why the initial condition on a doubly integrated record is usually worth more care than the record itself.

A zero that is not a choice

Setting $S_0 = 0$ in the cumulative sum is not the same decision as setting the physical constant to zero. The array has to start somewhere and zero is the natural place, and the physical constant is then added afterwards. Keeping the two separate matters because they are answerable to different things. The array's zero is an implementation detail. The physical constant is a measurement or an assumption, and it belongs in the report.

Check
  1. Two analysts integrate the same channel and report velocity curves that differ by a constant 15.65 m/s everywhere. Has either made an arithmetic error? What would you ask them?
    Answer

    Almost certainly not. Two integrals of the same data that differ by a constant differ in the one thing integration cannot supply, which is the initial condition. An arithmetic error would show up as a difference that changes with time. What to ask: what did you take the velocity to be at the start of the record, and how did you decide? The likely answers are "zero, because the record starts before impact" and "the test's approach speed, because that is what the vehicle was doing", and the gap between those two is the approach speed itself, which for this test is 15.65 m/s.

    The second question is the more useful one. Ask which of them checked the velocity against something outside the integration, because a constant offset does not show up in the integrated record itself.

  2. You integrate an acceleration record twice to get displacement and set both constants to zero. Which of the two decisions will dominate the error after one second, and by how much if the true initial velocity was 2 m/s?
    Answer

    The velocity constant, by 2 m. An error in the initial velocity enters displacement as v0t, so setting a true 2 m/s to zero costs 2 m/s × 1 s = 2 m after one second, 4 m after two, and so on. An error in the displacement constant is a fixed offset. It never grows, and it is usually not an error at all, because the origin of a displacement is normally a choice and not a fact. That asymmetry is the general rule. An error in the constant of the first integration is multiplied by time in the second. The earlier the mistake, the more the arithmetic amplifies it.

  3. A report gives a vehicle's velocity history with no statement of the initial condition. Name one thing you could check in the numbers that would reveal which choice was made.
    Answer

    Look at the part of the record before the event. Crash records usually begin with a pre-impact window in which the acceleration is zero. If the reported velocity sits at or very near zero there, the analyst set the constant to zero and is reporting a velocity change. If it sits at a steady non-zero value, and especially if that value matches the test's stated approach speed, then they used the known initial condition and are reporting velocity.

    A third possibility is worth watching for. If the pre-event stretch drifts steadily instead of sitting flat, neither constant is the problem. That is an uncorrected offset in the acceleration, which is the next page.
