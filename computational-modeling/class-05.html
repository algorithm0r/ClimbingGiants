TME 310 · Computational Physical Modeling

Class 5

We look at seven kinds of tools used for computational modeling.

For each one we look at the work it did and what was reported about its accuracy.

By hand, every panel of an integral was labor.

1 of 7 · Pencil, tables and the desk calculator, through the 1960s

manual workThe method and the bookkeeping A person chose the method, looked up or computed each function value, and recorded every step on a computing sheet. The Mathematical Tables Project (New York, 1938–48) had about 450 staff at its peak.
automated workThe arithmetic Mechanical calculators did the multiplying and adding. A 1949 photograph of Langley's computers shows Friden calculators on their desks.
a consequenceFew panels, so better methods With every panel costly, a method that reached the same accuracy in fewer panels saved work.

To four decimal places, rectangles need 17 183 panels, trapezoids 54 and Simpson's rule 4.

ancientTrapezoids Babylonian astronomers used a trapezoid construction to follow Jupiter, more than 2 000 years ago (Ossendrijver, 2016).
1743Simpson's rule Thomas Simpson published the rule that fits parabolas through the samples instead of straight lines.
1895–1901Runge–Kutta Carl Runge and Wilhelm Kutta designed methods for differential equations that take large, accurate steps by hand.

The integral of $e^x$ from 0 to 1, whose exact value is $e - 1 = 1.71828$. Each panel count is the fewest that brings the error under 0.00005.

Printed tables carried errors, and a survey in 1834 counted more than 3 700 of them.

1 of 7 · Pencil, tables and the desk calculator

doubtTables built by people Engineers and navigators took logarithms, sines and other function values from printed tables, computed by hand and set in type by hand. Charles Babbage designed his Difference Engine in the 1820s to compute and print tables by machine, because of the errors in tables made by people.
documented failureErrata in 40 volumes Dionysius Lardner (Edinburgh Review, 1834) went through 40 volumes of tables and counted more than 3 700 errors that the publishers themselves had acknowledged.

An electronic computer does the same steps over and over, as many times as it is told.

2 of 7 · The electronic computer, 1940s on

manual workThe program People wrote the equations as instructions, transcribed them onto coding sheets and punched cards, and checked the output.
automated workThe repetition For John Glenn's orbital flight on February 20, 1962, IBM computers in Washington, Cape Canaveral and Bermuda were programmed with the orbital equations.
a consequenceMany small steps Once the program was written, more panels added mostly machine time.

Before he flew, Glenn asked for the computer's numbers to be checked by hand.

2 of 7 · The electronic computer

doubt · February 1962Glenn asks for a hand check NASA's biography of Katherine Johnson says Glenn asked engineers to "get the girl" to recalculate the orbital equations by hand, on a desktop mechanical calculating machine. He said that if she said they were good, he was ready to go.
documented failure · July 1962Mariner 1 In July 1962, NASA's first Venus probe went off course after launch and was destroyed. NASA attributes it to a missing overbar on the symbol for radius, $R$ instead of $\bar R$, in an equation of the guidance program.

At Langley, the Black women computers worked in a segregated office, West Area Computing, until segregated facilities were abolished in 1958.

manual workThe program, in a language People write in a language with exact rules, such as FORTRAN or C. A program that breaks the rules is refused, with an error message.
automated workThe translation The compiler chooses the machine instructions, the registers, and the order of the instructions. Assembly is a readable name for each instruction, one line to one instruction.
a consequenceOne program, many machines Each CPU family has its own machine language, so the same FORTRAN can be compiled for each of them.

Programmers doubted that a compiler could write code as fast as theirs.

3 of 7 · The compiler

doubt · 1952Grace Hopper's A-0 Hopper later recalled that she had a working compiler and nobody would use it, because she was told computers could only do arithmetic.
doubt · 1957FORTRAN John Backus wrote that if FORTRAN's programs ran "only half as fast as" hand-coded ones, "acceptance of our system would be in serious danger." His team made the speed of the code it produced its main emphasis.
documented failure · 2011Csmith A research group fed randomly generated C programs to compilers and reported more than 325 previously unknown bugs in GCC, LLVM and commercial compilers. Twenty-five of the GCC bugs were given its top, release-blocking priority.

Backus, "The History of FORTRAN I, II, and III" (1978). Yang, Chen, Eide and Regehr, "Finding and Understanding Bugs in C Compilers" (PLDI 2011).

In 2019 chemists found that a widely used Python script gave different answers on different computers.

5 of 7 · Python and its libraries

documented failure · 2019The Willoughby–Hoye scripts The scripts, published in 2014 for computing NMR chemical shifts, gave correct results on Windows 10 and macOS Mavericks and wrong results on Ubuntu and macOS Mojave. Press reports estimated that more than 100 published studies had used them.
the causeAn assumed file order The scripts read their files with Python's glob, which does not promise any order. They assumed the files came back sorted, which was true on some systems and not on others. Python's documentation for glob says whether the results are sorted depends on the file system.

Neupane et al., Organic Letters (2019), who also published corrected scripts.

A computer algebra system does calculus in symbols, with the steps.

6 of 7 · Computer algebra, 1968 on: Macsyma, Mathematica (1988), Wolfram|Alpha (2009)

manual workStating the problem The user types the integral, the equation or the expression, along with any assumptions about the variables.
automated workThe algebra The system differentiates, integrates, simplifies and solves exactly, and Wolfram|Alpha can show the steps.
a limitNot every answer exists in closed form Most integrals and nonlinear differential equations have no solution in elementary functions, and then the system returns a special function, a numerical answer, or the problem unsolved.
no elementary answer$\int e^{-x^2}\,dx = \tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\,\mathrm{erf}(x) + C$ The function erf is defined by this integral.
no elementary answer$y' = x^2 + y^2$ Its solutions need Bessel functions.
an assumption$\sqrt{x^2} = x$ only if $x \ge 0$ In general $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, so a simplification depends on what is assumed about $x$.

In 2014 three mathematicians found Mathematica computing determinants wrongly.

6 of 7 · Computer algebra

documented failure · 2014Wrong determinants in Mathematica 9.0.1 Durán, Pérez and Varona reported that Mathematica 9.0.1 gave wrong determinants for some matrices with large integer entries, and different values when the same determinant was computed twice. The bug was fixed in Mathematica 10.0.2.
how they found itResults that disagreed Mathematica reported counterexamples to a conjecture they were testing. One of the three checked them in Maple and found they were not counterexamples at all. Maple and Sage agreed with each other.

Durán, Pérez and Varona, "The Misfortunes of a Trio of Mathematicians Using Computer Algebra Systems. Can We Trust in Them?" Notices of the AMS (October 2014).

In 2021 about 40% of Copilot's programs in 89 security scenarios were vulnerable.

7 of 7 · AI coding assistants

documented failure · 2021Security Pearce and colleagues gave GitHub Copilot 89 security-sensitive scenarios. About 40% of the 1 689 programs it produced were vulnerable.
measured · 2025Speed In a randomized trial by METR, 16 experienced developers took 19% longer on tasks when allowed AI tools. Before the trial, the same developers predicted a 24% speed-up.
doubt · 2025Trust In Stack Overflow's survey of about 49 000 developers, 46% distrusted the accuracy of AI tools and 33% trusted it. The most common complaint (66%) was answers that are "almost right, but not quite."

Pearce et al., "Asleep at the Keyboard?" (2021). METR, "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity" (2025). Stack Overflow Developer Survey (2025).
