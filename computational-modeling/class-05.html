In decimal, one third goes on forever, so on paper you stop writing 3s and round.

Every place you stop, the answer comes up short.

0.3 × 3=0.9 0.33 × 3=0.99 0.333 × 3=0.999

The computer works in binary, where one tenth goes on forever.

Binary uses only 0 and 1, with places worth a half, a quarter, an eighth, and so on.

0.0001=1/16=0.0625 0.00011=3/32=0.09375 0.000110011=51/512=0.099609375 0.0001100110011=819/8192=0.0999755859375

Every place you stop, the answer comes up short of 0.1. The computer stores a rounded 0.1, so in Python 0.1 + 0.2 prints 0.30000000000000004.

The computer keeps about 16 digits of every number, counted from the first one.

For a number in the hundreds, that leaves about 13 decimal places.

you type−407.2 One of our four accelerations.
the computer stores−407.199999999999988… The closest number it can make from 53 binary digits. It is off by about 0.00000000000001.
the mean of all four−404.29999999999995 Each of the four was rounded when stored, and each addition rounds again. The answer is off by about 0.00000000000006.

An accelerometer's own digits run out long before the 16th, so this rounding is far smaller than anything a crash measurement can show.

Step 1 of 7 · What does one arc look like?

what we foundA climb, then a straight fall At 25 frames a second, the vertical speed climbs to 5.2 m/s at 0.44 s and then falls on a nearly straight line. The height tops out at 4.75 m.
what we learnGravity is the slope In free flight the only vertical force is gravity, so the speed falls by 9.81 m/s each second, or 0.39 m/s a frame. While the speed climbs, the hand is still pushing, and neither model describes that.

Both models are linear in their parameters, so one least-squares solve fits each.

Step 3 of 7 · How do we fit each model?

gravity fittedThree unknowns
$$z = c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 \qquad g = -2c_2$$
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t, t**2])
c = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z, rcond=None)[0]
gravity held at 9.81Two unknowns
$$z + \tfrac{1}{2}(9.81)\,t^2 = c_0 + c_1 t$$
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t])
c = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z + 0.5*9.81*t**2, rcond=None)[0]
what we learnThe known term moves to the data side With gravity held, the gravity term is no longer unknown, so we add it to every height and fit a straight line to what is left.
nextWhich one fits better? Fit both to the whole flight of every arc and compare.

Which model will pass closer to the frames it was fitted to?

Step 4 of 7 · Which model fits the data better?

a new wordResidual The gap between a fitted curve and one of the frames it was fitted to. A flight of 26 frames has 26 residuals.
a new wordRMS Root mean square, one number for the typical size of many gaps. We compare each model's RMS of its residuals. Least squares, in this week's notes, picks the curve with the smallest RMS.

Step 4 of 7 · Which model fits the data better?

what we found2.48 cm against 2.55 cm The median RMS of the residuals, gravity fitted and gravity held. Both are under the 3.07 cm of vertical tracking noise given in the header.
what we learnThe RMS cannot choose between them The free model is able to pick 9.81 itself, so it can never fit worse than the model held at 9.81. Its RMS comes out smaller whatever the truth is.

what we found8.92 to 10.43 m/s² Fitted to the whole flight (mean 9.67). Fitted to the first half only, the range widens to 7.32 to 11.02.
what we learnThe fit follows the noise The spread is far larger than any real change in gravity from one shot to the next, so most of it is probably the fit bending to the tracking noise. With fewer frames it can bend further.

Step 6 of 7 · Which model predicts better?

what we found4.2 cm held, 5.8 cm fitted The median RMS of the errors on the second half. At worst, 11.8 cm held against 35.7 cm fitted.
what we learnResidual and error point different ways The residual is the gap on the frames a model was given. The gap on frames it was not given is its error, and that is what a prediction depends on. Fitting gravity buys a closer fit and costs a worse prediction.
