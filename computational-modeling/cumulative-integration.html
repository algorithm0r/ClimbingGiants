Week 3 · Numerical Integration

Cumulative integration

Video: Cumulative integration to recover velocity records from acceleration (0:14:06). Slides

Claim

A cumulative integral returns one value for every input sample, each holding the area accumulated from the start of the record up to that sample. A definite integral returns one number. The two are computed by the same sum. The cumulative version keeps the running total instead of only its final value.

The definite integral answers "how much altogether", and the cumulative integral answers "how much so far", at every instant along the record.

Keeping the Running Totals

The previous page added the areas of $n$ panels and returned the total. Nothing forces the discarding of the intermediate totals, and keeping them has a cost of its own:

$$S_k \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{y_i + y_{i-1}}{2}\,\Delta t \qquad\text{for } k = 0, 1, \dots, n$$

$S_n$ is the definite integral, the single number. $S_0$ through $S_n$ together are the cumulative integral, a record the same length as the input. The output has one useful property the input lacked. It is a total, so its units are the input's units multiplied by time.

What keeping them costs, and what it buys

It costs memory. A definite integral needs one running total, a single number, however long the record is. A cumulative integral needs an array as long as the input. The crash channel in your data pack is 4501 samples, so its cumulative integral is 4501 doubles, about 36 kB, which is not worth thinking about. A rig test logging four channels at 100 kHz for ten minutes is 240 million samples, and the same decision is 1.9 GB. The cost is the same in kind in both cases. At 1.9 GB you have to plan for it.

It buys time, later, and repeatedly. With $S$ in hand, the integral over any interval between two samples is one subtraction instead of a fresh sum over that interval. Asked for one interval, you have spent an array to save almost nothing. Asked for every window of a given width, which is what the search in week 7 does thousands of times over one record, the array is paid back many times over.

This is the time-space trade-off. It is the same shape as a decision you already make when you keep a machined part on the shelf against the next order, or hold inventory so the line does not stop for a re-tool. Both sides convert to money in the end: storage is a line on a cloud bill, and recomputation is engineer-hours and machine time. Which side is cheaper depends on how many times the interval integral will be asked for.

The first row is how a velocity history is obtained from an accelerometer, and it is probably the single most common measurement operation in crash engineering. There is no velocity sensor recording during the event, so the velocity is obtained by integrating the accelerometer record.

The Running Total Beside the Record

Drag the scrubber along the acceleration record. The shaded area is what has been accumulated so far, and the lower panel is the running total, drawn up to the same instant.

Three things in that picture are general properties of a cumulative integral.

The velocity curve is steepest where the acceleration is largest. A running total grows fastest when the thing being added is biggest, which is the same statement as "the integral's slope is the integrand", read off a plot instead of a formula.

Where the acceleration is zero the velocity is flat, not zero. Before impact the vehicle is coasting. Nothing is being added, so the total stops changing. A flat stretch in a cumulative integral means its input was zero there, barring an input that alternates sign on every sample, which a filtered channel will not do. It says nothing about the value of the total.

Negative input subtracts. The acceleration in this record is mostly negative, the vehicle is decelerating, so the accumulated total runs downward. Positive stretches during the rebound push it back up. The sum is signed, and the curve turns around wherever the input crosses zero.

An Interval's Total Is a Difference of Two

Because every sample of the output is a total, any interval's total is the difference of two of them:

$$\int_{t_i}^{t_j} y \,\mathrm{d}t \;=\; S_j - S_i$$

Set the two ends and watch it. The shaded region is the interval, the two marks on the lower panel are $S_i$ and $S_j$, and the readouts give both the subtraction and the same integral computed from scratch over the interval, so you can watch the identity hold:

That result is used twice later in the course. It is how the constant of integration is dealt with on the next page, and it is what makes the windowed search in week 7 affordable at all.

The length of the output

There are $n$ samples and $n-1$ panels between them, so a naive loop returns $n-1$ values and the output no longer lines up with its own time base. Every plot of it is then shifted by one whole sample, because the array holds the totals after each panel and they are being drawn against the times before it. That is small enough to look right and wrong enough to matter when two records are compared. The fix is to prepend a zero, because the accumulated total before any panel has been added is zero by definition. The code below does this. In Python it is the leading 0.0, in MATLAB the leading 0, and in JavaScript it is already there, because a Float64Array arrives zeroed and the loop starts at i = 1.

The standard

When the record being integrated is a crash channel, the integration is not the first step. SAE J211-1 assigns vehicle structural accelerations CFC 180 when they are to be integrated to a velocity or a displacement, and that filter is applied before any of this. Week 4 builds that filter, and The standards behind the crash data lists what it applies to.

In Code

def cumulative(y, dt):
    panels = (y[1:] + y[:-1]) * 0.5 * dt
    running = np.cumsum(panels)
    return np.concatenate([[0.0], running])

def definite(y, dt):
    return cumulative(y, dt)[-1]
function s = cumulative(y, dt)
    panels = (y(2:end) + y(1:end-1)) * 0.5 * dt;
    s = [0; cumsum(panels)];
end
function cumulative(y, dt) {
  const s = new Float64Array(y.length);
  for (let i = 1; i < y.length; i++)
    s[i] = s[i - 1] + (y[i] + y[i - 1]) * 0.5 * dt;
  return s;
}

The loop version and the cumsum version compute the same thing, and the library call is faster and shorter. The loop is here because it makes the accumulator visible, and because the pattern recurs in places where no library call exists.

Check
  1. A cumulative integral of an acceleration record is flat from 200 to 300 ms at a value of −17.5 m/s. What was the acceleration over that interval, and what was the velocity?
    Answer

    The acceleration was zero and the velocity was constant. S is the running area under the acceleration, so its slope is the acceleration, and a flat stretch means nothing is being added. The acceleration is zero throughout and the vehicle is coasting.

    The velocity is the height of that flat stretch, −17.5 m/s, but only if S was started from the true initial velocity. If it was started from zero, as it usually is, −17.5 m/s is the change in velocity since the start of the record, and the velocity is that plus whatever the vehicle was doing at t = 0. The next page is about which of those you have.

  2. You have S for a whole record. Write down how to get the area between 40 and 90 ms without summing anything.
    Answer

    S(90 ms) − S(40 ms), which is two array lookups and a subtraction. In indices, with a sample interval dt: S[round(0.090/dt)] - S[round(0.040/dt)].

    Every partial sum is already sitting in S, so any interval's area is the difference of its two endpoints. The cost does not depend on how wide the interval is, and that is what makes the windowed search in week 7 affordable. HIC has to evaluate an integral over every admissible window, and with S in hand each one costs a subtraction and not a sum.

  3. A colleague's cumulative integral is one sample shorter than its input. What did they omit, and what will it do to a plot?
    Answer

    They omitted the leading zero, which is the value at the first sample, where no area has yet accumulated. A cumulative integral of n samples has n values, the first of which is 0. On a plot, everything is shifted one sample earlier. Plotted against the original time array it either fails outright on the length mismatch, or, if the software quietly truncates, every feature appears dt early, by 80 µs here, which is invisible by eye and enough to move a reported time of peak. The length mismatch is the symptom you are most likely to notice, and it is a good reason to prefer a library that refuses a length mismatch over one that pads.
