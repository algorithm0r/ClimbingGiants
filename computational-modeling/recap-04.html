Assignment 4, the Request 4:49

Alice writes that the peak acceleration on the crossmember channel goes to the customer this week, and their engineers will recompute it from the same file. She would rather not send a number that comes back different. Chris read it the way he recommends reading any request, by marking what you already know and listing what you do not.

  • Peak. A peak is the largest value, though if what matters is the magnitude, the largest negative value counts too 5:29. A car hitting a wall is decelerating, so the peak here is most likely a minimum. To find the largest magnitude, find both the maximum and the minimum and compare their absolute values.
  • Crossmember. You found and integrated this channel last week.
  • The customer. The request says almost nothing about the customer 8:20. Who are they, and what will they use the number for? Those questions are not answered, and Chris noted that he does not put details into an assignment that do not matter.

The customer's engineers will run their own methods on the same file 9:28. If their peak differs from ours, the likely reason is a different method. Standards exist so that two labs apply the same techniques in the same order and get the same number, and this week you process the crossmember the way the standard says.

This is still Pólya's first step, understanding the problem 11:47. The standard asks for a filter, which takes a channel and changes its values, usually without removing any of them. That is how it differs from decimation (which throws samples away). Subtracting last week's offset from every sample is a very simple filter, and this week's is a special kind that the standard specifies.

Chris's list of questions for the week 14:02 was:

  1. What is the peak in the file as recorded?
  2. What does the standard say, and how do you build its filter?
  3. One pass or two? A forward pass makes features happen later in time (phase lag), and a second, backward pass corrects that. The standard's filter is the two-pass one.
  4. Which filter class? The standard has four classes, CFC 60, 180, 600 and 1000. Roughly, a class lets slower content through and removes content faster than its number in hertz.
  5. How much did the peak change from the raw peak? The plot for this week shows the raw data with its peak and the filtered data with its peak.
  6. What sentence goes to the customer with the number?

The Raw Peak 18:56

The class worked on the Civic (test 9808), which is held back from the phrase assignment. Its crossmember, recorded at 10,000 samples a second, peaks at −964 m/s² at 27.2 ms. The positive peak nearby is about +512, so the negative one is larger, as the reasoning about deceleration predicted.

Zoomed in, the record goes from −964 to about +512 in under 1.5 ms. Taken literally, the car went from its hardest deceleration to its hardest acceleration in about a millisecond, which the whole vehicle is very unlikely to be doing. More likely the structure is ringing from the impact, or the mount that holds the accelerometer on the crossmember is shaking, as last week's discussion of ringing suggested. These features are fast, and it is physically questionable that they describe the car. That is the reason the standard asks you to filter before you read a peak.

The Butterworth Filter 21:54

The standard's filter is a Butterworth filter, and Chris took its first line apart. The new, filtered value yi is a weighted sum. It takes the unfiltered value xi and the two before it, xi−1 and xi−2, each with its own weight (a0, a1, a2).

If every weight were one third, that would be a moving average, a simple smoothing technique that averages each value with the ones before it 24:08. (You could average values after it too, but if the data is arriving as you filter, those do not exist yet.) The Butterworth filter borrows that idea with specially calculated weights, and it adds two more terms 25:10. Those two are the previous two values the filter has just computed, yi−1 and yi−2, weighted by b1 and b2. That means the filter uses its own output to compute its next value.

To build the filter you need two things 26:31. The first is the filter class, which the standard sets by what the data will be used for (back to the question of the customer's purpose). The second is T, the time between samples, which is one over your sample rate. Packs were recorded at 10, 12.5 or 20 kHz. The Civic is 10 kHz, so T = 0.0001 s. The rest of the slide is formulas the standard gives you 28:20. From the class and T you compute ωd, ωa and a denominator D, and from those the five weights. It is a short calculation by hand or a few lines of code.

For CFC 60 at 10 kHz the a weights are very small, b1 is the one large positive weight (on the value just computed), and b2 is negative. A good check on your coefficients is that (a0 + a1 + a2) divided by (1 − b1 − b2) comes out to 1 30:42. In code it may come out very slightly off 1 from rounding, and very close to 1 is fine.

When Chris wrote the solution code, he asked an AI to write the coefficient code. If you do not trust the code, ask for the check to be built in 31:46. Then put the filter in a function and apply the yi line to every sample.

Phase Lag and Two Passes 32:47

Chris drew a signal and a smoothed version of it. Smoothing makes the peaks smaller (which is partly the point), and a forward-only pass also makes them later. A second pass, run backward, lines the peaks back up and smooths a little more. Please see Smoothing and phase lag.

The slide's question gives two smoothed curves and asks which is forward only 34:54. The forward-and-backward curve has the more squashed peaks, and its peaks come earlier, so the other one is forward only. The standard's filter is the forward-and-backward one, so yours should be too. In code, you can run the same loop again from the last sample down to the first, or reverse the list, run the forward pass, and reverse it back 36:13.

Picking a Filter Class 36:58

The slide reproduces a table from SAE J211, the standard for instrumentation in ground vehicle tests. The course uses its 1995 text, which is public. CFC 1000 is left off the slide because it is mostly for injury measurements, and Alice's question is not about injury. Please see The standards behind the crash data. For vehicle structure the table lists four uses.

  • Total vehicle comparison (CFC 60). The customer may be another lab comparing its cars to ours.
  • Collision simulation input (CFC 60) 39:09. Simulations are Chris's own area. A simulation of a car hitting a wall needs a pulse for the force, and the people who write simulations asked for a smooth pulse and not the jagged one.
  • Component analysis (CFC 600) 40:41. The customer may care about the crossmember itself (it sits under the left rear seat) and not the whole car.
  • Integration for velocity or displacement (CFC 180) 43:10. That is what you did last week without filtering first. Filtering last week's data at CFC 180 barely changes the Δv, and CFC 60 changes it a little more.

The class voted on which would be smoother, CFC 600 or CFC 60. CFC 60 smooths the most, then 180, then 600, then 1000. Some uses are sensitive to fast content and keep more of it (injury measurements, for instance), and others want only the slow pulse.

The class then voted on the customer's purpose 44:34. Votes went to every row, with the most for component analysis. The request does not say, and there is no single right answer. Chris chose total vehicle comparison (CFC 60) for the slides, partly because two of the four rows use 60. If you chose component analysis, that is just as complete. Chris noted that the class's choice may fit the request better than his.

Whatever you pick, put your assumption in the report 46:26. For instance, "I don't know what the customer is using it for. I assumed component analysis, so I used CFC 600. If they are doing something else, let me know and I will rerun it." You may also report more than one class, though one is enough.

The Filtered Peak 47:36

The class voted on what CFC 60 would do to the peak. Filtering will not make the peak larger, so the vote was on how much smaller it gets. With a light filter (600 or 1000) it might shrink a little, and with CFC 60 it might shrink a lot.

Filtered at CFC 60, the jagged gray trace becomes a smooth red line that keeps only the slow motion 48:42. The sharp value at 27.2 ms is gone (going by the filtered data it was noise), and the peak is now −355.5 m/s² at 62.4 ms. Whether a change from −964 to −355.5 is a lot or a little is your judgment.

Is the later peak phase lag 50:42? The filter ran forward and backward, so if it was done correctly it has no lag. The old peak was most likely a fast feature, which the filter removed, and the new peak is the slow pulse. It sits where the middle of the gray trace bottoms out. If the slow shape is what you care about, the red line captures the peak better.

The Sentence for the Customer 53:04

A number alone does not say how it was found, so the sentence around it should let the other lab repeat every step. Chris's sentence is mostly details for that reason.

"Peak X acceleration at the left rear seat crossmember (channel 10FORALERE00ACXP, NHTSA test 9808): −355.5 m/s² at 62.4 ms after contact, filtered at CFC 60 per SAE J211-1, forward and then backward."

It labels the number (X acceleration, the crossmember Alice asked for), gives the channel code and test so another lab can pull the same data in code, then gives the value and time, the class and the passes. If the other lab used CFC 600 or did not filter, the sentence shows where the difference came from. It could also say whether you removed the offset, since that is another place two labs might differ.

Checks and Where Your Evidence Runs Out 56:19

  • The coefficients. They pass the check that a constant comes through unchanged.
  • The sample rate. The filter's formulas only work when the sample rate is above 4.155 times the class, which is 249 samples a second for CFC 60, far below the Civic's 10,000. Every pack's rate (10, 12.5 or 20 kHz) is high enough for all four classes. That also connects to Week 2, since a 1 kHz logger could not use some of these filters at all.
  • The start 57:58. At the first sample there are no earlier x or y values. Chris's solution uses zeros for them, and there are a couple of other good choices. The standard's answer is to have enough record before and after the event that the start does not matter.

The main assumption for your report is the customer's purpose 59:21. You do not know it, so say what you assumed. The other choices along the way (how you started the filter, the two passes) belong in your assumptions too.

Assignment 4 With the Course Tutor 70:22

Over the weekend Chris solved Assignment 4 as a student would, with the course tutor on the assignment page. It took 13 prompts, and he then edited the report the tutor drafted. The whole conversation (including the tutor's reasoning), the report and the program are on Assignment 4 with the course tutor. The second half of class stepped through the highlights.

Understanding the Problem 72:11

Chris opened by telling the tutor what he thought he understood (the crossmember again, a filter, and a standard he did not know yet). The tutor is built around Pólya's steps, and their first question is usually whether you understand the problem, so saying what you understand is a good start. The first four prompts were all spent on understanding.

The tutor's replies usually hold several questions, and Chris answered one at a time. If a question matters, the tutor will usually bring it back later. The first one he took was why Alice mentions the customer's engineers 74:32. A student in class answered that the customer's engineers may process the data a different way, which is why both labs should use the standard.

The tutor then asked what else could make the two peaks differ even with the same standard 76:02. Chris did not know, and said so 76:57. It is fine to tell the tutor "I don't know, give me a hint." The answers were the sign of the peak (one lab reports a positive number and the other a negative one) and whether the offset is removed before filtering. When the tutor asked him to trace his program, he pointed out that he did not have a program yet.

The Plan 79:40

The tutor asked for the program's steps in plain words. Chris still wanted to learn more about the standard first, but he went along and gave a sketch. Open the file and find the crossmember, filter it into a second channel, plot both to see that the filter worked, find the peak, and write the report. He also said he did not yet know what the standard's filter was.

Asked where the standard was, he said he had read the course pages but could not find a clear source and asked for guidance 82:17. (He has since made the course site clearer on this.) The tutor showed the same table as the slides and asked which row matched the customer, and for his sample rate, since the coefficients need the class and T.

Chris reasoned that Alice asked for a peak and not a Δv or displacement, which ruled out CFC 180, and picked CFC 60 for total vehicle comparison 84:20. The tutor pushed back, asking what made total vehicle comparison more likely than component analysis. That was a fair challenge, and the choice went into the report as an assumption.

Carrying Out the Plan 86:03

Chris asked the tutor to look up the coefficient formulas and compute them for his own sample rate, so he could check them against his code. He then spent 19 minutes writing a first program that loads the data, finds the crossmember and plots it, without the filter 87:06. The tutor would not finish the filter until he made the choices that were his to make (one pass or two, how to start the filter). He answered them, and he asked for the whole edited file back, and not pieces to paste in 88:47.

Looking Back 89:21

The tutor had started the filter from zeros. That makes little difference here, but Chris did not understand it and did not want to ship code he could not explain, so he asked. The tutor explained and suggested a test in the code, which reports the filtered values at the edges. Whether those are close to zero depends on the scale of the signal.

In the final prompt Chris gave the tutor the numbers from his program's output and asked for a report that opens with a clean sentence (the filtered peak, the class, the double pass and the zero start), then the plot, then every choice listed as an assumption 90:38. The tutor drafted it and listed what they could not fill in, such as units. Chris made those edits himself, and the result is the report on the site.

Chris used 13 prompts, and he did not ask the tutor to solve the whole problem in one 92:19. That would probably have produced an answer, though Chris works with the AI to solve the problem and does not let the AI replace his work. Using the tutor is not required, and the walk-through shows one way to work with them.
