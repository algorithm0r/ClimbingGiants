Week 3 · Numerical Integration

Area under a curve

Video: Integrating from samples with rectangles or trapezoids (0:19:06). Slides

Claim

A definite integral of sampled data is computed as a sum of the areas of simple shapes fitted between adjacent samples. Fitting rectangles leaves a discrepancy that falls in proportion to the panel width. Fitting trapezoids leaves one that falls in proportion to its square.

You cannot integrate the curve, because you do not have the curve. You have points, so you fill the gaps between them with shapes whose area you know, and add those up.

The Definite Integral

The definite integral of $f$ from $a$ to $b$ is the signed area between the curve and the axis:

$$I \;=\; \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

Signed, because area below the axis counts as negative. A record that spends as long negative as positive can integrate to zero, and that is not an error. The zero is the result. For sampled data you have $n+1$ values at spacing $h = (b-a)/n$, and the integral is replaced by a sum over the $n$ gaps between them. Each gap is called a panel, and the question is what shape to put in it.

Rectangles

The simplest choice gives each panel a flat top at the height of its left-hand sample.

$$I \;\approx\; h \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f(x_i)$$

Each panel is $h$ wide and $f(x_i)$ tall, so its area is the product, and the sum is the total. The discrepancy is visible at every panel. On this integrand the flat top sits below the curve where the curve rises and above it where the curve falls.

Trapezoids

Use both ends of the panel instead of one, and join them with a straight line. The panel becomes a trapezoid of width $h$ with heights $f(x_i)$ and $f(x_{i+1})$, whose area is the width times the average of the two heights:

$$I \;\approx\; h \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{f(x_i) + f(x_{i+1})}{2}$$

Collecting terms, every interior sample appears in two panels and the two end samples appear in one each, which gives the form usually written and the form the code uses:

$$I \;\approx\; h\left[\frac{f(x_0)}{2} + f(x_1) + f(x_2) + \dots + f(x_{n-1}) + \frac{f(x_n)}{2}\right]$$

The rectangle rule never used the last sample at all, since it sums from $i = 0$ to $n-1$. So going from one to the other means halving the first sample and adding the last one at half weight, and the whole difference between the two rules is

$$T - R \;=\; \frac{h}{2}\left[f(x_n) - f(x_0)\right]$$

Both Rules Against a Known Area

Take $f = \sin x$ from 0 to $\pi/2$, where the exact area is 1. Both rules can be run at several panel counts and compared against a number that is known and not estimated.

The rectangle rule is on this page because it is the simplest thing that works and because the comparison between the two is what the rest of the page measures.

The Curve's Shape Decides the Sign

Both rules were described above as losing area, which is true on the rising, concave quarter of a sine measured there and not true in general. The sign of the error is decided by the shape of the curve, and the two rules answer to different features of it.

Watching those two features separately needs an integrand that does not change sign, and $\sin x$ is the wrong instrument for it. For a sine, $f f'' = -\sin^2 x$ is negative wherever the curve is not flat, so the integrand turns negative at exactly the points the curvature reverses. Every quarter moves both at once and neither can be read on its own.

The cubic $x^3 - 3x + 3$ separates them. Over $-2$ to $2$ it runs between 1 and 5, so it never crosses the axis and an answer above the true value is also an answer claiming too much area. Its slope turns at $-1$ and $+1$ while its curvature turns at 0, and because those happen in different places the span divides into four unit sections carrying all four combinations. Concave here means bending downward, so that a chord lies below the curve, and convex means bending upward.

Sixteen panels in every row, and the integrand is positive across all of them, so a positive number means the answer came out above the true value and that the rule claimed too much area. Read down the two error columns and each tracks a different property:

  • The rectangle rule follows the slope. Its top is flat at the left-hand sample, so on a rising curve the top lies below the curve across the whole panel and the sum comes out below the true value. On a falling curve the top lies above, and the sum comes out above. The sign is set by the slope alone.
  • The trapezoid rule follows the curvature. Its top is the chord between the two samples, and a chord of a concave curve lies below that curve while a chord of a convex curve lies above it. Which way the curve is heading makes no difference to the sign, which is why the trapezoid column changes sign once across the four sections and the rectangle column changes twice. The rectangle errors are also the larger ones here, by a factor of forty or more, which is the $h$ term against the $h^2$ term at this panel count.

The two behaviors have a further consequence, and the two do not arrive together. The leading error of the rectangle rule cancels when the integrand ends where it started, and the leading error of the trapezoid rule cancels when the slope ends where the slope started. Those are different conditions, and the whole span tells them apart. The cubic's slope is 9 at both $-2$ and $2$, so the trapezoid's leading term cancels. Its value ends at 5 where it began at 1, so the rectangle's does not.

Select the whole span in the demo and the readout reports a trapezoid error of zero at every panel count, which is more than the cancellation alone would buy. A cubic has no fourth derivative, so the terms that would normally survive have nothing to be built from, and the leading term is the whole error. That makes both rules predictable here in closed form: the trapezoid error on any section is $(h^2/12)[f'(b) - f'(a)]$, which is how the four numbers in the last column above can be obtained without running anything. At sixteen panels they are $-9/3072$, $-3/3072$, $+3/3072$ and $+9/3072$.

The sine, now that the two can be told apart

Both cancellations above came from the two ends agreeing: the rectangle's from the value there, the trapezoid's from the slope. A smooth periodic function integrated over a whole period agrees with itself at the ends in every derivative at once, so every term subtracts to nothing. That is why a whole sine wave returns the exact answer under both rules, to fifteen decimal places. That is a property of the interval, not of either rule.

A sine is the harder case for the question this section asked, which is why it was not the one used. Over a wave the integrand and the curvature reverse together, so the third and fourth quarters are convex and negative at the same time. The trapezoid loses the same amount of area in all four quarters, the same 8.0×10−4 at sixteen panels, and the sign of the reported error still turns over halfway down the column, because the integral itself has turned negative there. Read without the distinction this section drew, that column suggests the rule behaves differently in the trough. It does not.

The Error Against Panel Count

Read down each error column. Every time the panel count doubles:

  • the rectangle error is multiplied by about 0.50. It roughly halves.
  • the trapezoid error is multiplied by about 0.25. It roughly quarters.

Divide each error by the one above it and the rectangle column gives 2.06, 2.03, 2.02, 2.01, 2.00, and the trapezoid column gives 4.01, 4.00, 4.00, 4.00, 4.00. Both are settling onto their exponents from above, and neither is sitting on one. The claim holds in the limit of small h, and the coarsest panel counts have not got there yet. Later weeks test convergence the same way. Change the resolution by a known factor and measure what the error does.

On logarithmic axes each rule's error is a straight line and the slope of that line is the exponent. The dotted line is the slope the rule promises, drawn through the coarsest point and not fitted to the data, so lying along it is a result.

Select both rules together and the gap between them widens to the right. At 16 panels the trapezoid rule is 62 times more accurate, and at 512 panels it is 1957 times more accurate, for the same number of function evaluations. The two lines have different slopes, so the gap between them grows with the panel count.

Halving the width and quartering the error means the error goes as $h^2$. Halving the width and halving the error means it goes as $h$. Those exponents have a name, the order of the rule, and week 8 measures them again for a different kind of method.

Where the two rules coincide

Integrate $\sin x$ over 0 to $\pi$ instead, and the rectangle and trapezoid rules return identical values at every panel count, to every digit.

The reason is in the collected form above. The trapezoid sum differs from the rectangle sum by $h\,[\,f(x_n) - f(x_0)\,]/2$, and $\sin 0 = \sin \pi = 0$, so that correction is exactly zero. The agreement follows from the two end samples being equal, and it disappears when they are not. A test run on this interval will not distinguish the two rules.

Fixed Sample Spacing

Most of the above assumed the panel count could be raised. For a computed function it can, because $f$ is evaluated wherever you ask.

For measured data it cannot. The sample interval was fixed by the instrument, so $h$ is whatever the header says and the discrepancy is whatever that $h$ gives. There is no refinement available and no error estimate obtainable by refining. What can be done is to use the better rule, which costs one extra multiply and one extra add across the whole sum, and to report the interval used.

In Code

def rectangle(y, h):
    return h * sum(y[:-1])

def trapezoid(y, h):
    return h * (0.5 * y[0] + sum(y[1:-1]) + 0.5 * y[-1])

# or, on the panels directly:
def trapezoid_panels(y, h):
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(len(y) - 1):
        total = total + (y[i] + y[i + 1]) / 2
    return h * total
function I = rectangle(y, h)
    I = h * sum(y(1:end-1));
end

function I = trapezoid(y, h)
    I = h * (0.5*y(1) + sum(y(2:end-1)) + 0.5*y(end));
end
function rectangle(y, h) {
  let s = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < y.length - 1; i++) {
    s = s + y[i];
  }
  return h * s;
}

function trapezoid(y, h) {
  let s = 0.5 * (y[0] + y[y.length - 1]);
  for (let i = 1; i < y.length - 1; i++) {
    s = s + y[i];
  }
  return h * s;
}

Check
  1. A trapezoid integration at 50 panels gives 4.812 and at 100 panels gives 4.803. Estimate the value at infinitely many panels, and say what assumption you used.
    Answer

    4.800. The trapezoid rule has order 2, so its error falls as h². Halving the panel width quarters it. Writing I(h) = I + Ch² and I(h/2) = I + Ch²/4, the two estimates differ by three quarters of the coarse error, so the coarse error is (4.812 − 4.803) × 4/3 = +0.012 and I ≈ 4.803 + (4.803 − 4.812)/3 = 4.800.

    The assumption is that h is already small enough for the leading h² term to dominate, which is to say that both estimates sit in the asymptotic regime. Two numbers cannot check that. A third panel count can. If the ratio of successive differences comes out near 4, the assumption held.

  2. A record has 4501 samples at 80 μs. What is h, and what would you have to do to halve it?
    Answer

    h is the sample interval: 80 µs, or 8×10−5 s. It is not something the analysis chooses.

    To halve it you would have to run the test again with the recorder set to 25 kHz. Nothing done to this file can do it. Interpolating to 9001 points produces more numbers at a finer spacing and not one more piece of information about what happened between the samples. This is the distinction the previous page is about.

  3. Someone tests their integrator on f(x) = x over 0 to 1 and finds the rectangle and trapezoid rules both exact to machine precision at every panel count. Is their integrator correct? What has the test established?
    Answer

    The test has established almost nothing, and the result is closer to evidence of a bug than of correctness. The trapezoid rule fits a straight line across each panel, so on a straight-line integrand it is exact by construction, at every panel count, in any correct implementation. That is, passing was guaranteed before the code was written, so the test could not have failed and tells you nothing.

    The rectangle rule is the useful part of the test. Taking the left endpoint of each panel on f = x over 0 to 1 gives (n − 1)/2n, which is 0.4 at five panels and never 0.5. A rectangle rule that is exact here is either the midpoint rule wearing the wrong name, or wrong in a way this test cannot see.

    Test on an integrand with curvature, where neither rule is exact, and check the order. Halve h and look for the error falling by 2 and by 4. A test that passes for a reason unrelated to the code reports success without having checked anything.
