The Mean of Four Accelerations 8:25

The slide asked you to find the mean of four accelerations (−407.2, −388.2, −426.9 and −394.9 m/s²) with any tool you like. Chris used his phone's calculator, and the class got −404.3 (Chris left the minus signs off while typing and put the sign back at the end). NumPy's np.mean gave −404.29999999999995 10:55. Both cannot be right, so you need a way to decide which one to trust.

The class voted, and then checked by hand on the board 12:23. The sum is 1617.2, divided by 4 is 404.3, and multiplying 404.3 by 4 gives 1617.2 back. Chris made a carry error on the way and the class caught it. Your own head can make mistakes too, so working it out together is one more check.

When two tools you normally trust disagree, you go back to a tool you trust more, here hand arithmetic, and test the answer against it 14:59. You will likely do this often in modeling, and in engineering generally. When a number looks a little off, it is usually worth testing it against something you already trust.

Abstraction and the Tools We Compute With 16:04

Abstraction is our ability to take real things and draw ideas out of them. Every chair in the room is a specific chair, soft or hard, good or broken, and the idea "chair" has none of those properties. Much of what we work with in this course lives on the idea side 17:22.

  • Theories. Gravity pulls every shot down at 9.81 m/s². The experiments it comes from are real, such as dropping a ball and timing its fall.
  • Functions. The parabola for a shot is an idealization. The real thing is a ball moving through the air, and the data is our samples of where it was.
  • Plans 18:45. On a blueprint a wall is a rectangle. A friend of Chris's who was finishing his basement measured both sides of every wall anyway, because nothing built is exactly a rectangle.

The tools we compute with are abstractions too, and each layer hides more of the arithmetic than the one before it 20:13.

  • Paper (common practice by the 1800s). The written digits are in some sense a technology too 21:05. Try averaging 17 numbers written as Roman numerals, and the first thing you will want is to convert them.
  • Calculator (on desks from the 1970s) 22:53. It saves the column addition, though you still press + for every number.
  • Spreadsheet, program and library 23:46. A spreadsheet's average gave −404.3. A short loop and NumPy's np.mean both gave −404.29999999999995.
  • AI 25:05. Chris gave Claude Haiku, Sonnet and Opus the same prompt. Haiku and Sonnet added the numbers in their head and gave −404.3. Opus ran Python, saw −404.29999999999995, recognized it was off and reported −404.3. That is close to the steps the class followed. AI is the newest layer on our desks, and as with the calculator in the 1970s, it will likely take some years of use to learn how far to trust it.

Rounding Error in Binary Floats 26:44

In decimal, one third is 0.333… with the 3s going on forever. On paper you stop somewhere, and 0.333 × 3 = 0.999 is not quite 1. That is a rounding error, and rounding errors can add up as you compute.

A computer stores numbers in binary (base 2), where the only digits are 0 and 1 28:09. After the point the places are worth a half, a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth and so on, so binary has no tenths place. One tenth becomes 0.0 followed by 0011 repeating forever 28:49. 0.0001 is 1/16 = 0.0625, 0.00011 is 3/32 = 0.09375, and each further 0011 gets closer to 0.1 without reaching it.

A Python float stops after a fixed amount of memory 31:16. For 0.1 it keeps 55 places and rounds the last one up, so its 0.1 is very slightly too large. That is why 0.1 + 0.2 prints 0.30000000000000004, a favorite of programming forums.

The error is tiny next to the number 32:15. A float keeps about 16 significant digits, and it is rare to need more. However, there are ways for an error in the 16th digit to work its way up toward the first, so it is worth knowing when you lose digits. Adding 10 and 45 always comes out exact. A number whose binary expansion never ends will probably be rounded.

The four accelerations each had four significant digits 34:02. An answer with 17 should make you suspicious. Round it back to the digits the data supports and you get exactly −404.3.

The Square Root of 2 34:42

Numbers such as √2, π and e are irrational 38:50. In any base the digits after the point never end, so every way of writing one down has to stop and round.

Squaring checks a root 39:36. 1.41 × 1.41 = 1.9881, so 1.41 is the square root of 1.9881 and not of 2. You can find more digits by guessing the next one and squaring again. There is no direct formula like the one for multiplying. The quadratic formula would solve x² − 2 = 0, but it needs a square root itself 42:15. In practice you repeat the guess and the check until the digits stop changing.

Technically

In class Chris said there is no step-by-step method for square roots like the one for division. There is one, the digit-by-digit method, set out much like long division, and schools taught it well into the 1900s. Each digit in it is still a small guess and check, so a square root does come from repeating a step. A calculator runs a repeated step like this in its built-in firmware, and some chips use Heron's method below, which is Newton's method. Week 7 covers Newton's method.

Every tool gives an approximation, and each stops at a different digit 43:37.

  • Clay tablet (1700s BCE) 44:27. The oldest value Chris found is on a Babylonian tablet (Yale, YBC 7289), written in base 60 as 1;24,51,10. In decimal that is 1.41421296, and the last three digits are its rounding error.
  • Paper (around 60 CE) 47:10. Heron of Alexandria wrote down this method. Start at 1 and replace the guess with the average of the guess and 2 ÷ guess. From 1, 2 ÷ 1 = 2, and their average is 1.5. Three rounds give 577/408 = 1.4142157.
  • Log tables (1620s) 48:37. Repeating that by hand for every root anyone needed was tedious, so mathematicians published books of computed values to look up. People computed them, so they had some errors.
  • Slide rule (1620s, in use into the 1970s) 49:49. Its precision is how finely you can read a mark on the scale, about 1.41.
  • Calculator (1970s) 52:25. Press √ and you get ten digits.
  • Program and library 53:20. A loop running Heron's method and NumPy's np.sqrt(2) both get as close as a float allows. They land on the two floats on either side of √2, so np.sqrt prints one more digit (1.4142135623730951 against 1.414213562373095).
  • AI 53:57. Claude Haiku gave 16 decimal places from memory. Sonnet and Opus each ran Python to 50 digits, which Chris did not know Python could do. When he asked how they knew it was right, Claude wrote a second, independent program by a different method and got the same digits.
Lecture correction

In class Chris said a circle has 360 degrees because 60 × 60 = 360. It is 6 × 60 = 360 (60 × 60 is 3,600, the number of seconds in an hour). The 60 minutes and 60 seconds do come from Babylonian base 60. The 12 hours on a clock face trace back to Egypt, and both go back thousands of years, not one thousand.

Lecture correction

In class Chris described a slide rule as a ruler with a cylinder inside that rolls out lengths of π. That is not how a slide rule works. Its scales are marked so that the distance to each number is proportional to the number's logarithm. Sliding one scale along another adds two distances, which adds two logarithms, and that multiplies the two numbers. The A scale is the D scale squared, so the D reading under 2 on A is √2, as on the slide. Apollo engineers did use slide rules, alongside the electronic computers that ran the missions.

Heron's method is Newton's method for x² − 2 = 0, which Week 7 covers 55:13. Each round about doubles the digits that agree with √2, so a few guesses give very high precision. In Python, sum(x) / len(x) and np.mean(np.array(x)) both give the mean from the first half 55:42, and both carry the same rounding error.

Assignment 5, Fitting Gravity or Holding It at 9.81 66:51

This week is about fitting a curve to data, which is what the basketball shots are for. Bob writes that he has a flight model working two ways 67:19. In the first he lets the data tell him what gravity is, and in the second he holds gravity at 9.81 and fits everything else around it. He is about to build the rest of the season on one of them, and he wants to know which and how you know.

Pólya's first step is understanding the problem, so start by listing what you do not yet know (what a flight model is, what Bob is using it for, how to do the fitting). Then read each phrase with what you know about shooting and gravity 68:55.

  • "The flight model." In free flight the only force on the ball is gravity, so it slows on the way up, has zero vertical speed at the top and comes back down. Newton's theory says that path is a parabola. The model you expect can change what you see in the data. Early astronomers expected circular orbits and for a long time saw circles where the orbits were ellipses.
  • The parabola 71:02. The axis here is time, so the curve is height over time. The tracking data gives x, y and z, and z is the height, so the model is z(t) = c0 + c1t + c2t². Picture a web page with one slider for each constant, each moving the parabola a different way. Fitting means finding the three slider settings that match the data best.
  • "Let the data tell me what gravity is" 73:14. Fit c2 as well, and each shot gets its own gravity, g = −2c2. If some shots had been taken on the Moon or Mars, this is the fit that would tell them apart.
  • "Hold gravity at 9.81" 74:21. Fix c2 at −9.81/2 (divided by 2, because of the integration). That fixes one slider and leaves two to fit, c0 and c1. That leaves the held model one fewer degree of freedom.
  • Gravity from 9.78 to 9.83 75:10. 9.81 is an average. At the Earth's surface gravity runs from 9.78 m/s² at the equator to 9.83 at the poles, and it is a little weaker the farther you are from the Earth's center.
  • "Build the rest of the season on one" 76:49. Bob does not say what the model is for. A student suggested predicting whether the shot goes in. Chris pictured a broadcast that shows the chance a shot goes in before it gets there, or a robotic backboard that moves to catch every shot. In either case the model would need to say where the ball is going before it gets there.

Chris broke the work into seven steps 78:43. What does one arc look like? Where does free flight start? How do we fit each model? Which model fits the data better? What gravity does the free model find? Which model predicts better? Does the answer make sense? The fitting is least squares, which uses some linear algebra that the libraries do for you. The last steps rest on the difference between residual and error 80:09. A residual is the difference between the data and the model. An error is the difference between the model's predictions and the real world. Please see Model form and free parameters, Linearity in the parameters, Least squares and the choice of norm and Residual and error.

One Arc and Where Free Flight Starts 80:47

The slide plots arc 662 three ways.

  • From above (x against y). The dots bunch up while the player holds the ball, then run in a straight line to the hoop, so a straight line is the shape to expect there.
  • Height over time 81:47. A parabola through the middle, with "shoulders" at each end where the player is still moving the ball or the ball reaches the backboard. Those ends are not free flight, and including them widens the parabola.
  • Vertical speed 82:31. This comes from the height by last week's finite differences. Differencing amplifies noise, so the forward difference is jagged. A central difference has less noise, and stepping two frames before and after cuts it further. Stepping over more frames smooths more, and it also blurs the moment the hand lets go. Please see Finite differences.

As a human you can look at the height plot and see where the parabola starts, and a computer cannot 84:26. The speed plot gives the computer a rule. While the ball is in the hand it is pushed upward and its upward speed grows. Once it is released only gravity acts, and the upward speed never grows again 85:10.

The class voted on the first frame of free flight for arc 662, frames 10 to 13. Chris's eye said frame 10, and each way of computing the speed puts its peak at a different frame 85:42. You probably cannot know from the data when the ball really left the hand 86:27. It could even be frame 9, hidden by noise. All you can do is choose a rule and state it, so that anyone checking your work can use the same rule and get the same answer. Any of these rules is fine this week, and Week 7 looks at other ways to pick the frame.

Student question

Q: The example report picks one arc to analyze. Is that just any arc 87:57?

A: Yes. Pick any arc to start with and work out what you want to do with it. Then run your program on all of your arcs (the example pack has 24, and your own pack has 60). On one shot you could hard-code frame 10, but you do not want to hand-pick a frame for every shot, so you write a rule, such as the peak of the central-difference speed. On arc 662 that rule picks 11 where you might have picked 10, which matters little, since you were not confident in 10 either.

The code starts at the top of the arc and walks back 90:02. For a list s = [7, 4, 10], max(s) gives the largest value, 10, and np.argmax(s) gives the index where it sits, 2. So np.argmax(z) is the frame at the top of the arc, and a loop walks back from there while the upward speed keeps growing. On arc 662 it stops at frame 11.

The end needs a rule too 91:03. The slides end the flight at the first frame within 1 m of the center of the hoop. That close, the ball may hit the rim or the backboard, or someone may block it, and gravity is no longer the only force. The fit uses only the frames between these two rules.

Least-Squares Fits With Gravity Free and Held 92:20

The fit is two lines of code, and the method is least squares, np.linalg.lstsq in NumPy's linear algebra module. The first line builds a matrix with the columns 1, t and t². Multiplied by the constants c0, c1 and c2, it gives the model's height at every frame, and the second line finds the constants that match the measured heights best. The held model is the same with only the columns 1 and t, after the known gravity term is moved to the other side.

Fitted freely, arc 662's gravity comes out at 9.72 93:38. There are two possibilities until you look further. Something physical the model leaves out, such as air resistance, could be changing the curve. The other possibility is that the fit is following the noise, which may lean one way on this arc.

Residuals and RMS 94:36

The top plot lays the fitted parabola out flat and shows each frame's gap from it. Each gap is a residual, what is left over after the fit. To get one number for all of them, you could add them up, but positive and negative residuals cancel. Taking absolute values first works. RMS, the root mean square, squares each residual instead 96:25. Squaring removes the sign, and it makes large gaps count much more, so the fit prefers many small gaps to a few large ones. Then average the squares (the mean) and take the square root (the root), which brings the result back to the size of the residuals.

RMS is a convention 97:56. We use F = ma because we believe it is true about the universe. We use RMS because we all agreed to and it has nice mathematical properties, much as we agree to say the alphabet in one order. Please see Least squares and the choice of norm.

The Free Model Fits Closer, and Its Gravity Varies 99:04

The free model has three degrees of freedom and the held model two. More freedom always fits the frames at least as well, and on every arc the held model had the larger RMS 100:19. A polynomial with as many constants as there are frames would pass through every frame and still predict nothing about the next one. For this reason the RMS of the fit cannot choose between the models, since it mostly reflects how many constants each one has.

The free model's gravity changes from arc to arc 101:02. The plot shows it fitted to the whole flight, and fitted to the first half only, where the spread is wider. Fewer frames give the noise more room to pull the fit toward gravities we know are not true. Held at 9.81, the model does not have that freedom.

Held Gravity Predicts the Unseen Half Better 101:47

Bob will use the model to predict, so Chris split each flight in half. Each model is fitted to the first half, and the second half, which neither model sees, measures its error. On arc 662 the held model's curve tracks the second half closely, and the free model's drifts away 103:15. The residuals on the first half are as small as each model can make them, and on the second half the free model's gaps keep growing.

The RMS on the first half is the fit, and the RMS on the second half is the error 103:54. The error says how well the curve you would use to predict matches the frames you are trying to predict. More freedom fits the data better, and it does not necessarily predict better. In Python, splitting at the middle is half = len(t) // 2, then t[:half] and t[half:] 105:39.

Student question

Q: A student in Monday's section used only the gap at the last frame as the error. Is that all right 104:28?

A: Yes, you get to define your error. However, the last frame has its own noise, and if that noise happens to land it close to the curve, the model looks better than it is. Using the last few frames, or the whole second half, averages that noise out. Other choices are welcome too, as long as the report says which you used.

Arcs Left Out, the Advice for Bob and the Checks 105:57

  • Arc 325, left out. Part of its height curve does not look like a parabola. Its height falls by exactly the same step every frame, so its speed is exactly constant there, which real tracking never shows. Chris's guess is that the tracker lost the ball and someone filled the gap with straight lines. You do not have to know why to throw out data that looks this wrong, and the report should say you did.
  • Hold gravity at 9.81 107:24. The plot puts each arc's error with gravity held against its error with gravity fitted. A point on the diagonal means both did equally well. Most points sit on the side where fitting gravity was worse, so Chris would tell Bob to hold gravity at 9.81. Your own arcs will probably lead to the same conclusion, and if they do not, report the conclusion they lead to.
  • Arc 739, kept 108:52. The release rule put its start at frame 3, which seemed suspicious, but Chris kept it and said so.
  • An earlier split 109:22. Half is convenient. Splitting earlier gave the same result, which shows the conclusion does not hang on where the split falls.
  • About 3 cm of tracking noise 109:48. The tracking data is good to about 3 cm. A model that gets within the instrument's error is about as close as this data can show.
Lecture correction

At the end Chris said both models get their prediction error down to about 3 cm, the size of the tracking noise. That figure describes the fit. On the 23 example arcs the median RMS of the residuals is 2.48 cm with gravity fitted and 2.55 cm held, both under the 3.07 cm of vertical tracking noise given in the header. The error on the unseen half is larger, a median of 4.2 cm held and 5.8 cm fitted. That is, both fits reach the tracking noise, and both prediction errors stay above it.
