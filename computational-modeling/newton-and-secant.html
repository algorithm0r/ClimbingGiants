Newton and the secant method

Claim

Newton's method follows the tangent at the current estimate to where it crosses the axis and takes that crossing as the next estimate. Near a simple root the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. It requires the derivative and maintains no bracket, so it offers no guarantee of convergence. The secant method replaces the tangent with the line through the two most recent estimates, requiring no derivative and converging almost as fast.

Follow the slope down to the axis and start again from there. It is usually much faster than bisection, though it can fail.

The Tangent Step

At $x_k$ the tangent to $f$ has slope $f'(x_k)$, and setting the tangent to zero and solving for its crossing gives the whole method:

$$x_{k+1} \;=\; x_k \;-\; \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}$$

The secant method uses the same formula with the derivative replaced by the slope through the last two points:

$$f'(x_k) \;\approx\; \frac{f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})}{x_k - x_{k-1}}$$

which is a finite difference, namely week 4's backward difference, with the step supplied by the iteration and not chosen.

Convergence Rates

Optional, open to read

Near a simple root Newton's error obeys $e_{k+1} \approx C e_k^2$, so the error is roughly squared each step and the number of correct digits roughly doubles. From an error of 10⁻¹ that goes 10⁻², 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁸, 10⁻¹⁶, which is four steps from one digit to machine precision. Compare with bisection, which gains one bit, about a third of a digit, per evaluation. To reach 12 digits from a bracket of width 1, bisection needs 40 evaluations and Newton needs about 5.

The secant method's exponent is the golden ratio, and that is one reason the method survives alongside Newton. It converges a little more slowly per step, though each step costs half as much, so per evaluation it is usually the faster of the two. It is also the only one of the three that needs neither a derivative nor a bracket.

Steps Without a Bracket

Bisection's bracket is what makes its guarantee possible, because the root is inside and stays inside. Newton and the secant method keep no such interval, so at every step the estimate may land anywhere. Concretely:

  • A small derivative gives a huge step. The tangent is nearly flat, so its axis crossing is far away. This is page 22's third failure.
  • The step can leave the domain. Nothing in the formula knows the beam ends at 6 m.
  • It can cycle. Some functions send Newton back and forth between two points forever, converging to neither.
  • It can converge to a different root than the one nearest the start.

These are what a method without a bracket costs, and the price is usually worth paying, which is why most library root finders take Newton or secant steps whenever they can.

Combining Bisection and Newton

Optional, open to read

In practice the methods are usually combined, and a good library does it in three steps:

  1. Find a bracket by plotting or scanning.
  2. Take Newton or secant steps from inside it.
  3. If a step leaves the bracket, discard it and bisect instead. The bracket is maintained, so the guarantee is kept, and the fast steps are taken whenever they are safe.

This is Brent's method, near enough, and it is what scipy.optimize.brentq and MATLAB's fzero are. Knowing that is likely worth more than being able to implement any of the three, because the practical decision is usually whether the problem has been set up so that a bracket exists.

Check
  1. Newton's error is 10⁻³ at one step. Estimate it after two more, and say what assumption you used.
    Answer

    About 10−12, via 10−6. Newton converges quadratically, at en+1 ≈ Cen². Taking C ≈ 1, the errors go 10−3 → 10−6 → 10−12, so the number of correct digits doubles each step.

    Three assumptions are hiding in that. The root is simple, since at a double root Newton is only linear and the errors would go 10−3, 5× 10−4, 2.5×10−4. The iteration is already in the asymptotic regime, meaning close enough for the quadratic law to hold. The third is that C, which is f″/2f′ at the root, is of order 1, when it may be a good deal larger. The last step also brushes machine precision, because for values of order 1, 10−12 is close to the floor and the step after it would not deliver 10−24.

  2. Your f is expensive and you have no derivative. Which method, and how many evaluations per step?
    Answer

    The secant method, at one evaluation per step. It reuses the previous point, so each step costs a single new evaluation of f and no derivative, at an order of 1.618 (the golden ratio).

    The alternative is Newton with a finite-difference derivative, which costs two evaluations per step for an order of nearly 2. Compare them per evaluation and not per step. Secant gains a factor of 1.618 per evaluation, Newton √2 = 1.414. The secant method is faster per evaluation, which is the currency when f is expensive. The difference-quotient derivative is also the worst of both worlds numerically, because it inherits the step-size dilemma from week 4, on a function you were told is expensive.

  3. Why can a hybrid method keep bisection's guarantee while taking Newton steps most of the time?
    Answer

    It keeps the guarantee because it never lets go of the bracket.

    The hybrid keeps an interval known to contain a root at all times. Each step it proposes a Newton or secant step and then checks it. Does the proposal land inside the current bracket, and does it shrink the interval by enough? If yes, take it and update the bracket. If no, discard it and take a bisection step instead.

    The guarantee survives because it is a property of the bracket and not of the steps, and the bracket is maintained unconditionally. The worst case is that every proposal is rejected, and then the method is bisection, with its bound intact.

    The speed survives because near a simple root Newton's proposals are excellent and are almost always accepted. The guarantee costs one comparison per step, which is small next to an evaluation of f. That is close to Brent's method, and it is why the default root finder in most libraries is a hybrid.
