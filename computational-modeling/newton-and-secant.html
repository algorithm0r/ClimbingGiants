Newton and the secant method

Video: The Newton and the Secant Method for Finding Roots (0:15:33). Slides

Claim

Newton's method replaces $f$ near the current estimate by its linear part, the tangent line, and takes the tangent's zero as the next estimate. Near a simple root (one where $f$ crosses zero with a slope that is not zero) the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. The secant method takes the same step with the slope estimated from the last two estimates, so it needs no derivative. Neither keeps a bracket, so the starting guess decides which root comes back, and a nearly flat slope can throw a step far away. In one unknown both finish in microseconds on a cheap $f$, and bisection is usually fast enough. The same step works for $n$ equations in $n$ unknowns, and in practice it runs on systems of thousands to millions of unknowns, where solving the linear part is a job for linear algebra.

Pretend the function is a straight line, find where that line crosses zero, and start again from there. In one unknown this is a faster way to do what bisection already does well. It is most useful with many unknowns at once, where bisection does not apply.

Newton's Step on the Cube Root of 100

The cube root of 100 is the root of $f(x) = x^3 - 100$, and a calculator gives it as 4.641588833612779. That makes it a good test problem, because every error can be checked exactly. Since $5^3 = 125$, 5 is a reasonable first guess.

Write the root as $x = 5 + p$, where $p$ is the correction still needed, and expand the cube.

$$\begin{aligned} (5 + p)^3 - 100 &= 125 + 75\,p + 15\,p^2 + p^3 - 100 && \text{expand the cube} \\ &= 25 + 75\,p + 15\,p^2 + p^3 && \text{collect the constants} \end{aligned}$$

If the guess is close, $p$ is small, and $p^2$ and $p^3$ are smaller still. Keeping only the linear part gives an equation you can solve in one line.

$$25 + 75\,p = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = -\tfrac{1}{3}, \qquad x_1 = 5 - \tfrac{1}{3} = 4.6667$$

The next guess, $x_1 = 4.6667$, is already right to two digits. Repeating the same move from $x_1$ gives a better guess again. Newton's method repeats this move (keep the linear part, solve it, take the step) until the correction is small.

Newton worked this way on $x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$, in a manuscript circulated in 1669 and printed in 1711. From the guess 2 he wrote $x = 2 + p$, and the equation became $p^3 + 6p^2 + 10p - 1 = 0$. Keeping $10p - 1 = 0$ gave $p = 0.1$. He then set $p = 0.1 + q$, which is the same as starting again from 2.1, and $q^3 + 6.3q^2 + 11.23q + 0.061 = 0$ gave $q = -0.0054$. The guess became 2.0946 (the root is 2.0945515). He used no derivative and no tangent, only the expansion.

The Tangent Line and the Newton Step

The linear part of $f$ at a guess $x_k$ is its tangent line there, the straight line through $(x_k, f(x_k))$ with slope $f'(x_k)$. Here $f'(x) = 3x^2$, and at 5 the slope is 75, the coefficient of $p$ above. In general the step is

$$\begin{aligned} f(x_k + p) &\approx f(x_k) + f'(x_k)\,p && \text{the linear part, with } p \text{ the correction} \\ 0 &= f(x_k) + f'(x_k)\,p && \text{set it to zero} \\ p &= -\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)} && \text{solve it, if } f'(x_k) \neq 0 \\ x_{k+1} &= x_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)} && \text{take the step} \end{aligned}$$

Each step evaluates $f$ and $f'$ once. The first two steps from 5 are

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= 5 - \frac{25}{75} = 5 - 0.3333333 = 4.6666667 \\ x_2 &= 4.6666667 - \frac{1.6296296}{65.333333} = 4.6666667 - 0.0249433 = 4.6417234 \end{aligned}$$

Newton's Method in Code

Your own Newton's method is a short loop. It stops when the correction is smaller than a tolerance, so the last step it takes only confirms the step before it. On the cube root of 100 it takes 5 steps (10 evaluations) where the table needed 4.

def newton(f, df, x, tol=1e-12, max_steps=50):
    for step in range(max_steps):
        slope = df(x)
        if slope == 0:
            raise ZeroDivisionError(f'flat tangent at x = {x:g}')
        dx = -f(x) / slope
        x = x + dx
        if abs(dx) < tol:
            return x, step + 1
    raise RuntimeError(f'no convergence in {max_steps} steps')

x, steps = newton(lambda x: x**3 - 100, lambda x: 3 * x**2, 5.0)
function [x, steps] = newton(f, df, x, tol, max_steps)

    end

function newton(f, df, x, tol = 1e-12, maxSteps = 50) {
  for (let step = 0; step < maxSteps; step++) {
    const slope = df(x);
    if (slope === 0) throw new Error(`flat tangent at x = ${x}`);
    const dx = -f(x) / slope;
    x = x + dx;
    if (Math.abs(dx) < tol) return {x: x, steps: step + 1};
  }
  throw new Error(`no convergence in ${maxSteps} steps`);
}

Correct Digits per Step, Newton and Bisection

Counting the digits that agree with the root, Newton's estimates have 2, 4, 9 and 15 correct after steps 1 to 4 (a little more than double each step, for a reason given below). Bisection on the bracket [4, 5] halves the bracket each step, and since $2^{3.3} \approx 10$ it gains one digit about every 3.3 steps. A Newton step costs two evaluations to bisection's one, which the evaluation counts further down take into account.

Near a simple root Newton's error obeys $e_{k+1} \approx C e_k^2$. Here $e_k$ is the error after $k$ steps and $C = f''/(2f')$ at the root, where $f''$ is the second derivative. For the cube root $f'' = 6x$, so $C = 1/x = 0.215$, and the table follows the law closely. For instance, $0.215 \times (2.51\times10^{-2})^2 = 1.355\times10^{-4}$ against a measured $1.345\times10^{-4}$, and $0.215 \times (1.35\times10^{-4})^2 = 3.9\times10^{-9}$ against a measured $3.9\times10^{-9}$. That is, squaring an error near $10^{-d}$ gives one near $10^{-2d}$, and a $C$ below 1 adds a little more.

Optional, the derivation of the squared error

Let $r$ be the root and $e_k = x_k - r$. A Taylor expansion of $f$ and $f'$ about $r$, with $f(r) = 0$, gives

$$\begin{aligned} f(x_k) &\approx f'(r)\,e_k + \tfrac{1}{2} f''(r)\,e_k^2 && \text{to second order} \\ f'(x_k) &\approx f'(r) + f''(r)\,e_k && \text{to first order} \\ e_{k+1} = e_k - \frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)} &\approx \frac{f''(r)}{2 f'(r)}\,e_k^2 && \text{subtract and keep the lowest power} \end{aligned}$$

The constant $C = f''(r)/(2f'(r))$ needs $f'(r) \neq 0$, which is what a simple root means. At a double root $f'(r) = 0$, and Newton slows to a fixed fraction of the error per step (half, at a double root).

The Secant Step from the Last Two Estimates

The secant method takes Newton's step with $f'(x_k)$ replaced by the slope of the line through the last two estimates.

$$\begin{aligned} f'(x_k) &\approx \frac{f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})}{x_k - x_{k-1}} && \text{backward difference, } h = x_k - x_{k-1} \\ x_{k+1} &= x_k - f(x_k)\,\frac{x_k - x_{k-1}}{f(x_k) - f(x_{k-1})} && \text{put it into Newton's step} \end{aligned}$$

It needs two starting guesses. From $x_0 = 4$ and $x_1 = 5$, where $f$ is $-36$ and 25, the slope is $(25 - (-36))/(5 - 4) = 61$, and the first step is $x_2 = 5 - 25/61 = 5 - 0.4098 = 4.5902$. In the table, $x_k$ comes after $k - 1$ steps.

The slope is the backward difference from the page on finite differences, with $h = x_k - x_{k-1}$. It reuses $f(x_{k-1})$, which the previous step already evaluated, so each step costs one new evaluation of $f$ and no derivative. In exchange, $h$ is not a choice. It is whatever the last step happened to be, large at the start and shrinking only as the estimates close in, which is one way to see why the secant method's order (below) is lower than Newton's.

The convergence order of the secant method is 1.618 (near a simple root), the golden ratio, lower than Newton's 2. That is, its error obeys $e_{k+1} \approx C e_k^{1.618}$, for a different constant $C$. You can measure the order from three errors in a row as $p \approx \log(e_{k+1}/e_k) / \log(e_k/e_{k-1})$. On this table it runs 1.73, 1.58 and 1.63 for the last three triples (the earlier triples give 3.3 and 1.34, before the errors settle).

Without a formula that delivers the root, as the quadratic formula does, each method on this page and on the page on bisection tries a guess, checks $f$ there, and makes the next guess from the check. They differ in what they take from it. A scan takes nothing, since its guesses are fixed in advance and the check says only where the sign changed. Bisection takes the sign at the midpoint, the secant method the values at the last two guesses, and Newton's method the value and the slope at the guess.

The Starting Guess Decides Where Newton Lands

Newton keeps no bracket. Each step goes wherever the tangent crosses zero, so the result depends on where the steps start, and nothing in the method knows which root you wanted.

A Nearly Flat Slope Throws the Step Far Away

The step divides by the slope, so where the slope is close to zero the step is large. On $x^3 - 100$ from $x_0 = 0.5$, $f = -99.875$ and the slope is $3 \times 0.5^2 = 0.75$, so the correction is $99.875 / 0.75 = 133.2$ and $x_1 = 133.67$. Far from the root the tangent of a cube crosses zero about two-thirds of the way to the origin, so each step then takes off about a third of $x$.

Checking a Returned Root in Code

A check run after the solver catches the wrong crossing. Given whatever a routine returned, verify reports the residual, whether the value lies in the problem's domain, and which way $f$ crosses zero there.

def verify(f, x, domain, tol=1e-8, h=1e-4):
    lo = domain[0]
    hi = domain[1]
    report = {}

    report['residual'] = abs(f(x))
    report['is_root'] = report['residual'] < tol

    report['in_domain'] = lo <= x and x <= hi

    before = f(x - h)
    after = f(x + h)
    if before < 0 and after > 0:
        report['crossing'] = 'rising'
    elif before > 0 and after < 0:
        report['crossing'] = 'falling'
    else:
        report['crossing'] = 'none'

    return report

function verify(f, x, domain, tol = 1e-8, h = 1e-4) {
  const lo = domain[0];
  const hi = domain[1];
  const residual = Math.abs(f(x));
  const before = f(x - h);
  const after = f(x + h);
  let crossing = 'none';
  if (before < 0 && after > 0) crossing = 'rising';
  if (before > 0 && after < 0) crossing = 'falling';
  return {
    residual: residual,
    isRoot: residual < tol,
    inDomain: x >= lo && x <= hi,
    crossing: crossing
  };
}

Bisection evaluates both ends to check the sign change, then 40 midpoints, after which the bracket is $9.1\times10^{-13}$ wide. That guarantees 12 digits (the midpoint is within $4.5\times10^{-13}$ of the root). Newton and the secant method reach the full 15 to 16 digits of double precision in fewer evaluations, though neither guarantees anything in advance.

On a desktop, in Python, bisection's 42 evaluations take a few microseconds and Newton's 8 take under 1 (both far below anything a person notices). When $f$ is a formula like this one, the count of evaluations rarely matters, and bisection's guarantee is often worth more than Newton's speed. The count matters when each evaluation is expensive, such as one run of a simulation, and then the secant method's single evaluation per step is usually the better trade. (Page 22 counts evaluations in detail.)

Library root finders in one unknown combine the two ideas. SciPy's scipy.optimize.brentq and MATLAB's fzero use Brent's method. It keeps bisection's bracket and proposes secant steps, or steps through a sideways parabola ($x$ as a quadratic in $f$) through the last three points, inside it. It falls back to a bisection step whenever a proposal would leave the bracket or shrink it too little. On [4, 5], brentq returns 4.641588833612774 after 8 evaluations of $f$ (at its default tolerance of $2\times10^{-12}$), with a bracket kept at every step. SciPy's scipy.optimize.newton is plain Newton when given fprime and the secant method when not, with no bracket in either case.

Newton's Step on Thousands to Millions of Unknowns

Bisection needs a sign change, and $n$ equations in $n$ unknowns have no single sign to change. Newton's step needs only the linear part, and the linear part exists in any number of unknowns. Each equation has a slope in every unknown, so a step uses $n^2$ slopes, and solving the linear part means solving all $n$ equations together. That generalization is the work of linear algebra. In practice Newton's method does most of its work on systems like those in the table.

Optional, Newton's step in many unknowns, worked through on two

Write the unknowns as a list $\mathbf{x} = (u_1, \ldots, u_n)$ and the equations as a list $F(\mathbf{x}) = (F_1(\mathbf{x}), \ldots, F_n(\mathbf{x}))$, each equal to zero at the root. Each equation has a slope in each unknown, so there are $n^2$ slopes. They form the Jacobian, the $n \times n$ matrix with

$$J_{ij} = \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial u_j} \qquad \text{the slope of equation } i \text{ in unknown } j$$

With $\Delta$ the list of corrections and $\mathbf{x}_k$ the estimate after $k$ steps, the step is the same three moves as before.

$$\begin{aligned} F(\mathbf{x}_k + \Delta) &\approx F(\mathbf{x}_k) + J(\mathbf{x}_k)\,\Delta && \text{the linear part} \\ J(\mathbf{x}_k)\,\Delta &= -F(\mathbf{x}_k) && \text{set it to zero, a linear system} \\ \mathbf{x}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{x}_k + \Delta && \text{take the step} \end{aligned}$$

In one unknown, $J$ is $f'$ and solving the system is dividing by it. In $n$ unknowns each step solves an $n \times n$ linear system, the same kind of problem as the normal equations.

A Circle and the Curve $y = e^x - 1$

Take a circle of radius 2 and the curve $y = e^x - 1$, written as two equations equal to zero.

$$F_1(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 4 = 0, \qquad F_2(x, y) = e^x - 1 - y = 0$$

They cross once with $x > 0$, at (1.004169, 1.729637). Start from the guess (1, 1). The slopes and the residuals there are

$$\begin{aligned} J &= \begin{bmatrix} \partial F_1/\partial x & \partial F_1/\partial y \\ \partial F_2/\partial x & \partial F_2/\partial y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2x & 2y \\ e^x & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2.718 & -1 \end{bmatrix} && \text{at } (1, 1) \\ F &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 + 1 - 4 \\ e - 1 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 0.718 \end{bmatrix}, \quad -F = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -0.718 \end{bmatrix} && \text{at } (1, 1) \end{aligned}$$

so $J\Delta = -F$ is two linear equations in the corrections $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$. Each row of $J$ times $\Delta$ equals the matching entry of $-F$, and elimination solves them.

$$\begin{aligned} 2\,\Delta x + 2\,\Delta y &= 2 && \text{first row} \\ 2.718\,\Delta x - \Delta y &= -0.718 && \text{second row} \\ \Delta y &= 1 - \Delta x && \text{from the first row} \\ 2.718\,\Delta x - (1 - \Delta x) &= -0.718 && \text{put it into the second} \\ 3.718\,\Delta x &= 0.282, \quad \Delta x = 0.0758, \quad \Delta y = 0.9242 && \text{solve} \\ (x_1, y_1) &= (1.0758,\ 1.9242) && \text{take the step} \end{aligned}$$

Each row of $J\Delta = -F$ sets one equation's linear part to zero, and each of those is a straight line in the plane. The step lands where the two lines cross, as the demo below shows. The code is the one-unknown loop with the division replaced by a linear solve.

import numpy as np

def newton_system(F, J, x, tol=1e-12, max_steps=50):
    x = np.array(x, dtype=float)
    for step in range(max_steps):
        dx = np.linalg.solve(J(x), -F(x))
        x = x + dx
        if np.max(np.abs(dx)) < tol:
            return x, step + 1
    raise RuntimeError(f'no convergence in {max_steps} steps')

def F(v):
    x, y = v
    return np.array([x**2 + y**2 - 4, np.exp(x) - 1 - y])

def J(v):
    x, y = v
    return np.array([[2 * x, 2 * y], [np.exp(x), -1.0]])

x, steps = newton_system(F, J, [1, 1])
function [x, steps] = newton_system(F, J, x, tol, max_steps)
  for steps = 1:max_steps
    dx = -(J(x) \ F(x));
    x = x + dx;
    if max(abs(dx)) < tol
      return
    end
  end
  error('no convergence in %d steps', max_steps);
end

F = @(v) [v(1)^2 + v(2)^2 - 4; exp(v(1)) - 1 - v(2)];
J = @(v) [2*v(1), 2*v(2); exp(v(1)), -1];
[x, steps] = newton_system(F, J, [1; 1], 1e-12, 50)
function newtonSystem(F, J, x, tol = 1e-12, maxSteps = 50) {
  for (let step = 0; step < maxSteps; step++) {
    const [[a, b], [c, d]] = J(x);
    const [r1, r2] = F(x).map(v => -v);
    const det = a * d - b * c;
    const dx = [(r1 * d - b * r2) / det, (a * r2 - c * r1) / det];
    x = [x[0] + dx[0], x[1] + dx[1]];
    if (Math.max(Math.abs(dx[0]), Math.abs(dx[1])) < tol) return {x: x, steps: step + 1};
  }
  throw new Error(`no convergence in ${maxSteps} steps`);
}

const F = ([x, y]) => [x ** 2 + y ** 2 - 4, Math.exp(x) - 1 - y];
const J = ([x, y]) => [[2 * x, 2 * y], [Math.exp(x), -1]];
newtonSystem(F, J, [1, 1]);

Library Solvers and Estimated Jacobians

General-purpose library solvers, given only $F$, estimate $J$.

SciPy's scipy.optimize.fsolve and MATLAB's fsolve (in the Optimization Toolbox, and built into Octave) take only $F$ and a starting guess. Given no Jacobian, they estimate one from finite differences of $F$, one small step in each unknown, which are secant slopes in each direction. SciPy's version then updates the estimate from each step it takes (Broyden's update), the secant idea in $n$ unknowns, so it rarely pays for a fresh estimate (on the example, one estimate at the start). Both also shorten or bend a step that would not reduce the residuals, which is a safeguard plain Newton lacks.

On the example, fsolve(F, [1, 1]) returns array([1.00416874, 1.72963729]) after about a dozen evaluations of $F$, with no $J$ supplied. In MATLAB or Octave, fsolve(F, [1; 1]) returns the same root to about 9 digits (its default tolerance stops sooner). Brent's method has no standard version in $n$ unknowns, since it rests on a bracket, so these solvers keep no guarantee. For this reason you will need a starting guess near the root, and a plot of the curves is one way to find one.

Check
  1. Newton's error is 10⁻³ at one step. Estimate it after two more, and say what assumptions you used.
    Answer

    About 10−12, via 10−6. Newton converges quadratically, at ek+1 ≈ Cek². Taking C ≈ 1, the errors go 10−3 → 10−6 → 10−12, so the number of correct digits doubles each step.

    The estimate rests on three assumptions. The root is simple, since at a double root Newton is only linear and the errors would go 10−3, 5×10−4, 2.5×10−4. The iteration is already close enough for the quadratic law to hold. In addition, C, which is f″/2f′ at the root, is of order 1, when it may be a good deal larger. For the cube root of 100 it is 0.215, so the errors would go 10−3, 2×10−7, 10−14. The step after these would not deliver much more, because for values of order 1 double precision bottoms out near 10−16.

  2. Evaluating f′ costs about as much as evaluating f. Per evaluation, which converges faster, Newton or the secant method?
    Answer

    The secant method, usually. A Newton step raises the error to the power 2 and costs two evaluations (f and f′), so each evaluation raises it to about the power √2 = 1.414. A secant step raises it to the power 1.618 for one evaluation.

    On the cube root of 100 Newton needs 8 evaluations and the secant method 7, which is the same ordering. With a formula this cheap the difference is a fraction of a microsecond. It matters when each evaluation of f is expensive.

  3. Why can Brent's method keep a bracket while taking fast steps most of the time?
    Answer

    It keeps an interval known to contain a root at every step. Each step it proposes a fast step (a secant step, or one through a sideways parabola through the last three points) and then checks it. Does the proposal land inside the current bracket, and does it shrink the bracket by enough? If yes, it takes the step and updates the bracket. If no, it takes a bisection step instead.

    Because the bracket is kept, Brent always converges. Its worst case is slower than bisection (about the square of bisection's step count), though in practice it is rarely worse. Near a simple root the fast proposals land close to the root and are almost always accepted, so Brent is usually about as fast as the secant method.

  4. A structural model has 1000 unknowns. How many slopes does one Newton step use, and how many extra evaluations of F does estimating them from small changes in each unknown cost?
    Answer

    106 slopes, one for each pair of equation and unknown. A forward difference moves one unknown at a time and evaluates all of F once per move. For this reason the estimate costs 1000 extra evaluations of F, on top of the one at the current point.

    A solver that re-estimated J every step would spend almost all its time on the estimate. Broyden's update reuses the step just taken to correct the old estimate, at no extra evaluations, which is how SciPy's fsolve avoids most of that cost. Large structural codes usually avoid it another way, by building J from the model's own derivatives.
