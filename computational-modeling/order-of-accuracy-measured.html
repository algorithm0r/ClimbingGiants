Halving the step, in evaluations

Claim

With drag there is no formula for the shot's path, so a stepped run has nothing exact to be checked against. Running it again with half the step and comparing where the ball ends up shows whether the step is short enough. When halving the step moves the answer by less than the precision you need, the answer has settled. This is a convergence study. Every step works out the forces on the ball at least once. Heun's method and RK4 look at the forces more than once a step, and on the shot they settle with far fewer of these evaluations than Euler's method.

Halve the step until the answer stops moving. A stepper that looks at the forces more than once a step usually gets there with less work.

Halving the Step Until Two Runs Agree

Page 24 stepped the tracked shot with Euler's method. With one step a frame (40 ms) the ball ended 135 mm above a run with 0.1 ms steps, and shorter steps followed the path more closely. That leaves two questions open, how short the step has to be and whether the 0.1 ms run is itself close to the path. With drag there is no formula to compare either run against. The tracked frames scatter about 3 cm either side of the path, so they cannot settle a question about a few millimeters either.

If you have run a finite element model, you may have refined the mesh until the stress you cared about stopped changing. The same idea works for the step. Run the shot, halve the step, run it again, and compare where the ball is at the end. If the two runs agree to the precision you need, keep the finer run (the section below shows why it is the one within that precision). If they do not, halve again. This is the convergence study. All it needs is the program and a precision to aim for, with no exact answer.

The precision is a choice, and the example on this page takes 3 mm, about a tenth of the tracking noise. A run with drag switched off ends 157 mm from the run with drag at the end of the flight, so a 3 mm uncertainty from the step is probably small next to the effect a model with drag is trying to measure. A tighter precision costs more work, and the sections below count that work.

Halving Euler's Step on the Shot

The runs below start from the state at frame 5 on page 24, 4.202 m up and climbing at 2.75 m/s, with the drag constant $k$ = 0.016 per meter. Each run steps 0.72 s to frame 23, the last frame before the ball nears the hoop. In the table each halving of Euler's step about halves the move. Page 24's second check gives the reason. A step half as long lands about a quarter as far off the path, and there are twice as many steps, so the drift at frame 23 is about half. If the halving keeps up, the moves still to come after a given run add up to about its last move (half of it, plus a quarter, and so on). For this reason the move between two runs is about how far the finer run still is from the path. The coarser run is about twice as far off, which is why the finer run is the one to keep.

Euler's move first falls under 3 mm at the sixth halving, where the run of 1 152 steps (0.625 ms each) is 2.1 mm from the run of 576. With Euler, then, a step of 0.625 ms is short enough for 3 mm. One step a frame is far too long. The 0.1 ms run on page 24 is six times shorter again, so it is very likely within a millimeter of the path. The code below runs the study. It uses the euler function from page 24 and the shot function and starting state s0 from page 25. With $m$ steps a frame the step is $h = 0.04/m$ s, and $18m$ steps reach frame 23.

def halving(stepper):
    for m in [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128]:
        h = 0.04 / m
        p = stepper(shot, s0, 0.20, h, 18*m)[-1, :3]
        if m > 1:
            change = 1000 * np.linalg.norm(p - p_prev)
            print(f'{1000*h:7.4f} ms  {18*m:5d} steps  {change:.3g} mm')
        p_prev = p

halving(euler)
f = @(t, s) shot(t, s, 0.016);
halving(@euler, f, s0)

function halving(stepper, f, s0)
    for m = [1 2 4 8 16 32 64 128]
        h = 0.04 / m;
        p = stepper(f, s0, 0.20, h, 18*m);
        p = p(end, 1:3);
        if m > 1
            fprintf('%7.4f ms  %5d steps  %.3g mm\n', 1000*h, 18*m, 1000*norm(p - p_prev));
        end
        p_prev = p;
    end
end

Counting the Work in Evaluations

Each Euler step works out the forces on the ball once, where the ball is at the start of the step, and the program does this by calling shot. Each of those calls is an evaluation. A study costs every evaluation in every run it makes, from one step a frame up to the pair that agrees. For Euler on the shot that is 18 + 36 + … + 1 152 = 2 286 evaluations. Page 22 compared root finders by the same count.

On the shot the forces are three lines of arithmetic, and a laptop runs Euler's whole study in a blink. Working out the forces is usually the expensive part of a bigger model, though, and there the count of evaluations likely matters more than anything else about a run's cost. A model of a seated occupant with many body segments, or of the air flowing around the ball, may spend almost all of its time there. In a model like that, a stepper that settles in fewer evaluations finishes sooner, and the next section describes two that do.

Heun's Method and RK4

Euler holds the velocity and the acceleration from the start of a step for the whole step. Over the first frame of the shot, gravity and drag slow the climb from 2.75 to about 2.35 m/s, but Euler moves the ball at 2.75 m/s for the whole 40 ms. That is why it lands 8.1 mm high on page 24. A better step would use a velocity closer to the average over the step. Under a fixed acceleration, physics gives that average exactly. The distance is the average of the starting and ending velocities times the time, $\Delta z = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_{\text{start}} + v_{\text{end}})\,h$.

Heun's method uses that formula. It first takes an Euler step to estimate where the ball will be at the end of the step and how fast it will be moving there. It then works out the forces at that estimate and goes back to the start of the step. The real step moves the ball at the average of the velocities at the start and the end, and changes its velocity by the average of the two accelerations. That is two evaluations a step. On the first frame the vertical velocity is 2.75 m/s at the start and about 2.35 m/s at the end, so Heun moves the ball up at about 2.55 m/s:

$$z = 4.202 + 0.04 \times \tfrac{1}{2}(2.75 + 2.35) = 4.202 + 0.04 \times 2.55 = 4.304\ \text{m}$$

Euler's step reaches 4.202 + 0.04 × 2.75 = 4.312 m, so Heun lands 8.1 mm lower. To see which is closer, the table below uses a run with steps a thousand times shorter (0.04 ms) as the path, and Heun lands within 0.014 mm of it. RK4 (short for the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method) looks at the forces four times a step. The first look is at the start. The second moves half a step with the rates from the first look and works out the forces there, at the middle of the step. The third goes back to the start, moves half a step with the rates from the second look, and works out the forces at the middle again. The fourth moves a full step with the rates from the third look and works out the forces at the end. RK4 then steps with a weighted average of the four, with the two middle looks counting twice as much as the two at the ends. On the first frame its average vertical velocity is also about 2.55 m/s, and it lands on the path to well under a thousandth of a millimeter.

In code each stepper has the same shape as the euler function on page 24, with its one update line replaced by the lines that take the looks. In both functions dt is the step. Each of k1 to k4 holds the six rates found at one look (three velocities and three accelerations), and none of them is the drag constant $k$.

def heun(f, s0, t0, dt, n):
    s = np.array(s0, dtype=float)
    out = np.empty((n + 1, s.size))
    out[0] = s
    for i in range(n):
        t = t0 + i*dt
        k1 = f(t, s)
        k2 = f(t + dt, s + dt*k1)
        s = s + dt/2 * (k1 + k2)
        out[i + 1] = s
    return out

def rk4(f, s0, t0, dt, n):
    s = np.array(s0, dtype=float)
    out = np.empty((n + 1, s.size))
    out[0] = s
    for i in range(n):
        t = t0 + i*dt
        k1 = f(t, s)
        k2 = f(t + dt/2, s + dt/2*k1)
        k3 = f(t + dt/2, s + dt/2*k2)
        k4 = f(t + dt, s + dt*k3)
        s = s + dt/6 * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)
        out[i + 1] = s
    return out
function out = heun(f, s0, t0, dt, n)
    s = s0(:);
    out = zeros(n+1, numel(s));
    out(1,:) = s.';
    for i = 0:n-1
        t = t0 + i*dt;
        k1 = f(t, s);
        k2 = f(t + dt, s + dt*k1);
        s = s + dt/2 * (k1 + k2);
        out(i+2,:) = s.';
    end
end

Checking the Stepper Without Drag

With drag switched off ($k$ = 0) the acceleration is fixed at $g$, and the exact path is the parabola from physics. A drag-free run is a check on the stepper. Heun and RK4 both give back the parabola to far less than a millionth of a millimeter, at every step tried. The reason is the formula above. Under a fixed acceleration the average of the starting and ending velocities is the exact average velocity, and both steppers move the ball with that average. Euler lands above the parabola by 141.3 mm at one step a frame, as page 24 works out. A drag-free run that misses these numbers probably has a mistake in the stepper.

This check says nothing about the drag term. The convergence study does not check it either, since a wrong drag term settles under halving just as well as a right one. Comparing the run with the tracked frames, as page 25 does, is the check on the drag term.

Check
  1. Euler's run of 576 steps on the shot moved 4.22 mm from the run before. You need the position to 1 mm. How many more halvings does that take, and how many steps is the finer run?
    Answer

    Three halvings, 4 608 steps. Each halving halves Euler's move, so the next three are 2.11, 1.05 and 0.53 mm. The second is still just over 1 mm, so it takes the third. The step is then 0.156 ms, and 18 frames at 256 steps a frame is 4 608 steps. Every run from one step a frame on adds up to 9 198 evaluations.

  2. Heun also averages the vertical acceleration. At frame 5 it is −10.11 m/s², and at the end of the Euler step it is −10.06 m/s². Starting from 2.751 m/s, what vertical velocity do Euler and Heun give after one 40 ms step? The path has 2.348 m/s. Why are both so much closer to it than Euler's height was?
    Answer

    Euler 2.347 m/s, Heun 2.348 m/s. Euler uses the starting acceleration, 2.751 + 0.04 × (−10.11) = 2.347. Heun uses the average, −10.08 m/s², which gives 2.348. The acceleration changes by only about 0.05 m/s² over the step, since gravity is fixed and only the drag changes, so holding it fixed costs about 1 mm/s. The velocity changes by about 0.4 m/s over the same step, so holding it fixed costs the 8 mm in height.

  3. You need the position to 0.1 mm. How many evaluations does Euler's study take, and how many does Heun's take?
    Answer

    Euler 73 710, Heun 252. Euler's moves keep halving from 1.05 mm at 2 304 steps (0.53, 0.26, 0.13 and then 0.066 mm), so it first agrees within 0.1 mm at 36 864 steps, eleven halvings in all. Every run from 18 steps on adds up to 73 710 evaluations. Heun's moves are 0.17 and then 0.042 mm, so it takes two halvings, runs of 18, 36 and 72 steps at 2 evaluations each, which is 252. At this precision Euler's study takes almost 300 times the work.

Order of Accuracy, Rounding and the Steppers' Formulas

This section is optional. It names the factors above, explains where RK4's moves stop shrinking, and writes the two steppers as formulas. Nothing earlier on the page depends on it.

Each halving divides Euler's move by 2, Heun's by 4 and RK4's by 16. These factors are $2^p$, where $p$ is the stepper's order of accuracy. Euler's order is 1, Heun's is 2 and RK4's is 4, and a method of order $p$ is called first, second or fourth order. The error at a fixed time is roughly proportional to $h^p$. The order is read from the runs alone, with no exact answer needed. A factor well away from 2 for Euler, 4 for Heun or 16 for RK4 most likely points at a mistake in the stepper.

RK4's moves keep falling by 16 until they reach about 2×10⁻¹³ m, at a step of 1.25 ms. Past that the factor drops under 10 and then to about 2, and the moves level off near 10⁻¹⁴ m. At that size the moves are most likely rounding error, the error from storing each number to about 16 significant figures, which does not shrink with the step. (In those last rows the Python and MATLAB versions differ in the second figure.) The position itself shows no sign of this, so check the move from run to run when the moves get this small.

As formulas, with the state written $s$ and the rate function $f(t, s)$ from page 24, Heun takes two rates and steps with their average:

$$k_1 = f(t,\ s), \qquad k_2 = f(t + h,\ s + h k_1), \qquad s_{\text{next}} = s + \tfrac{h}{2}\,(k_1 + k_2)$$

This is the trapezoid rule from week 3 applied to the rate, with the far end estimated by an Euler step because it is not yet known. RK4 takes four:

$$\begin{aligned} k_1 &= f(t,\ s) \\ k_2 &= f(t + \tfrac{h}{2},\ s + \tfrac{h}{2}k_1) \\ k_3 &= f(t + \tfrac{h}{2},\ s + \tfrac{h}{2}k_2) \\ k_4 &= f(t + h,\ s + h k_3) \end{aligned} \qquad\qquad s_{\text{next}} \;=\; s + \tfrac{h}{6}\,(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)$$
