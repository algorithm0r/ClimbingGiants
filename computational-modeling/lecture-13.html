Fitting a line to measured points

One shot was tracked 25 times a second for 1.2 s.

It is a missed three by Stephen Curry from November 2015, shown as its distance from the hoop and its height. These frames are its free flight only, picked by a rule shown later.

Fitting a line means finding the line that comes closest to the points.

Three lines through the same distances, with each point’s gap to the line drawn in red.

We choose the shape, y = a + bt, and the computer finds the a and b that come closest.

A model is an equation we expect the data to follow. Its shape is the form, a and b are its parameters, and finding them is the fit.

$$y \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{a} + \textcolor{#8A6508}{b}\,t$$

The distance sits on a straight line, within 7 cm.

The right panel shows each point’s gap from the line, its residual, in centimeters.

The RMS gives one typical size for all 31 gaps, 2.9 cm.

RMS stands for root mean square. The plain mean of the gaps is 0 because the positive and negative ones cancel, so each gap is squared first.

$$\text{RMS} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{6.73^2 + 3.57^2 + 3.28^2 + \cdots}{31}} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{257.6}{31}} \;=\; \sqrt{8.31} \;=\; 2.9\ \text{cm}$$

Both fitted numbers check out against the ball.

In y = a + bt, a is the value at t = 0 and b is the change per second.

intercepta = 7.84 m The ball was 7.91 m from the hoop at the first frame.
slopeb = −6.22 m/s It closed 7.49 m in 1.2 s, about 6.24 m/s.

The same fit on the height gives the best line through a curve.

Red sticks join each point to the line. The largest is 1.14 m.

On the height, neither number describes the ball.

intercepta = 4.41 m The ball was at 3.27 m at the first frame.
slopeb = 0.05 m/s Its vertical velocity, shown a few slides on, went from almost 6 m/s up to almost 6 m/s down.

The fitting was the same in both panels, and the straight line suited only the distance.

The form we choose largely decides whether the fitted numbers mean anything.

Computational Physical Modeling

A straight line for the vertical velocity

Gravity makes the vertical velocity a straight line.

The height curved, so we look at its rate of change. The 29 velocities come from central differences of the heights.

$$v \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{v_0} \;-\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{g}\,t \qquad \text{intercept } v_0,\ \text{slope } {-g}$$

The fitted slope gives g = 9.60 m/s².

This is within 2.1% of 9.81. The velocities’ typical gap from the line, their RMS residual, is 0.513 m/s.

A number we already know does not have to be fitted.

The fit found g from the data, though we already knew it. Holding g leaves every frame for v0 alone.

from the data29 velocities They scatter by about half a meter per second, and gave g = 9.60.
already knowng = 9.81 m/s² It is known to more digits than these frames could give.

Holding g at 9.81 moves −gt to the data side.

v + 9.81t should be the same number at every frame, and the fit takes its average as v0.

$$\begin{aligned} v &= v_0 - g\,t && \text{the model} \\[8pt] v + g\,t &= v_0 && \text{add } g\,t \text{ to both sides} \\[8pt] \underbrace{v + 9.81\,t}_{\text{data}} &= \textcolor{#8A6508}{v_0} && \text{one parameter left to fit} \end{aligned}$$

With g held, the RMS residual moves from 0.513 to 0.517 m/s.

The dashed line is the fit with g free. Holding g costs 0.004 m/s of residual for a g we trust.

Finding the free flight in a tracked shot

A straight velocity line holds only while the ball is in free flight.

This is shot 1092, a different shot. The next slides give the rules for its window.

Free flight starts where the vertical velocity stops growing.

After the release it falls 0.39 m/s a frame, and 0.39 × 25 frames a second is 9.75 m/s², close to g.

The rule steps back from the top while an earlier velocity was higher.

def release(t, z):
t is time and z is height
    vz = np.full(len(t), np.nan)
nan where there is no velocity
    vz[2:-2] = (z[4:] - z[:-4]) / (t[4:] - t[:-4])
two frames each side, to calm the noise
    i = int(np.argmax(z))
start at the top of the flight
    while i > 3 and (vz[i - 1] > vz[i] or vz[i - 2] > vz[i]):
two back, so one noisy frame cannot stop it
        i -= 1
step back a frame
    return i

Where a model’s form comes from

We choose the form, and the fit then finds its parameters.

x is the input, and θ (theta) marks a parameter, p of them in all. f is the form.

$$y \;=\; f(x;\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots, \theta_p})$$

A form usually comes from one of three sources.

They are listed in order of how much the fitted numbers mean.

firstThe physics A projectile’s parabola has a height, a speed and an acceleration as its parameters.
secondWhat y does far out Decay to zero suggests ae−kx, and leveling off suggests ax/(b + x).
thirdConvenience A polynomial fits, though its coefficients mean nothing one at a time.

Adding parameters to a fit

Each extra degree adds one more number for the fit to adjust.

Ten points made from a known parabola plus noise, fitted with polynomials of degree 1, 2 and 3.

Degree 9 passes through every point and misses the curve between them.

The ten points come from a parabola (dashed) with noise taken from a real sensor.

The residual falls to zero while the error climbs back.

The error is the RMS gap to the true curve along its whole length, known only because we built the data. Degree 0 is a constant.

Ten parameters on ten points give zero residual, up to rounding, whatever the data are.
