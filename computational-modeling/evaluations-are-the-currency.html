Week 7 · Root Finding and Search

Evaluations are the currency

Claim

Methods are compared by the number of evaluations of $f$ they consume to reach a tolerance, not by the number of iterations they take. An iteration is free. An evaluation costs whatever computing $f$ costs, and the methods in this week differ in how many evaluations each iteration needs.

Count the expensive thing. Iterations are bookkeeping, and evaluations are the bill.

Why Iteration Counts Mislead

Iteration counts are what textbooks tabulate, and they are the wrong unit as soon as an iteration costs something. In this week alone:

MethodEvaluations per IterationEvaluated Quantity
bisection1$f$ at the midpoint
secant1$f$ at the new estimate
Newton2$f$ and $f'$

Newton in five iterations and the secant method in five iterations are not the same amount of work. Comparing them by iteration counts flatters Newton by a factor of two, and that factor is roughly the size of the difference being argued about, so the unit decides the argument.

The Comparison in Evaluations

The beam-deflection root from the previous pages, from a starting point of 1.20 m and a bracket of [0.5, 3.0]:

Methodevaluationserror reached measured order
bisection421.0×10⁻¹²1 (factor 2)
Newton85.7×10⁻¹³2.00
secant72.6×10⁻¹³1.63

The secant method reaches a comparable answer in fewer evaluations than Newton, despite converging more slowly per iteration. Its order of 1.63 is the golden ratio, 1.618, and the arithmetic that makes it competitive is that $1.618$ per evaluation beats $2$ per two evaluations, since $1.618 > \sqrt{2}$. The measured orders in that table are computed from successive errors and not quoted from theory, and they are what the demo on the previous page reports as you step it.

An Expensive Derivative

The table above assumes $f'$ costs about what $f$ costs. Often it costs much more, or nothing at all:

  • An analytic derivative is usually cheaper than $f$, sometimes much cheaper if it shares subexpressions. Newton looks better than the table suggests.
  • A numerical derivative costs one or two more evaluations of $f$, so Newton becomes a 3-evaluation step that converges at order 2, which is worse per evaluation than the secant method, which is getting a derivative estimate for free from values it already has.
  • No derivative at all, where $f$ is a black box, a simulation or an experiment. Then Newton is unavailable and the choice is between bracketing and the secant method.

The last case is the one where the currency argument bites hardest, because that is where an evaluation costs minutes or money and not microseconds.

Genuinely Expensive $f$

Everything above treats evaluations as merely countable. When each one is a finite-element run, a CFD solve, or a physical test, the count becomes the whole design of the analysis:

  1. Budget first. Decide how many evaluations are available before choosing a method, and choose one that can produce something useful within that number.
  2. Coarse then fine. A cheap scan to locate the region, then an expensive method inside it. Twenty coarse evaluations plus five refined ones usually beats twenty-five spent uniformly.
  3. Reuse. Every evaluation is a data point. A method that discards the values it computed, as bisection does with all but the sign, is wasting information it paid for. The secant method's advantage is exactly that it reuses the previous value.
  4. Report the count. An answer costing 6 evaluations and an answer costing 600 are different results, and the second may not be repeatable within the project.
What bisection throws away

Bisection evaluates $f$ at the midpoint and uses one bit of the answer, which is the sign. The magnitude, which would say whether the root is near this end of the bracket or the far one, is discarded entirely. Methods that use it, the secant method among them, converge faster for that reason and not because of any deeper cleverness. It is also why bisection's rate is independent of the function, since a method that ignores the values cannot be helped or hindered by them.

Check
  1. Newton takes 5 iterations and the secant method takes 7 on the same problem. Which consumed fewer evaluations, and by how many?
    Answer

    The secant method, by two. Newton needs f and f′ at every step, so 5 × 2 = 10 evaluations, while the secant method reuses the previous point and needs one new value of f per step, plus one to start, so 7 + 1 = 8. So the method that took more iterations did less work. Counting iterations would have reported Newton the winner by two, and the ranking reverses on the measure that corresponds to time actually spent.

    The exception is worth naming. If f′ comes free, from an analytic derivative evaluated in the same pass or an adjoint that costs a fraction of the forward solve, then Newton's 5 steps cost about 5 and it wins. That is why the useful question is always what an evaluation costs here, and not which method is faster in general.

  2. Each evaluation of your f is a 20-minute simulation and you have one working day. State your evaluation budget and pick a strategy.
    Answer

    24 evaluations. Eight hours is 480 minutes, at 20 minutes each. If the runs must be serial, spend them as a coarse scan then bisection, with about 8 points across the plausible range to confirm a sign change and isolate it, leaving 16 halvings, which reduces the surviving bracket by a factor of 65 536. A scan looks like overhead, though it is what tells you there is one root and not none or three, and with 24 evaluations you cannot afford to discover that late. Avoid Newton unless the derivative is free. At two evaluations a step it buys 12 steps, and it can spend all of them leaving the region.

    If the runs can go in parallel, the answer changes completely. Bisection is strictly serial, each step needs the previous result, so 24 serial runs is 8 hours. A 24-point scan is 24 independent runs and finishes in 20 minutes on 24 cores. With a wall-clock budget in place of an evaluation budget, the method with the worse convergence rate can be an order of magnitude faster.

  3. Why is bisection's convergence rate independent of the function, when Newton's is not?
    Answer

    Because bisection reads only the sign of f, and Newton reads its value and slope.

    A sign is one bit. Whatever f does, whether steep, flat, wiggly or discontinuous in its derivative, each evaluation returns one bit, and one bit halves the interval. The rate is a property of the bookkeeping, not of the function, which is why the count can be promised in advance.

    Newton uses the local shape to extrapolate to the axis, so how well it does depends on how well a straight line describes f near the root. Near a simple root with moderate curvature it is spectacular. Near a double root it degrades to linear. Near an inflection it can step past. On a flat stretch the tangent sends it far away.

    The trade is the recurring one. A method that assumes less is slower and cannot be surprised. A method that assumes more is faster when the assumption holds and fails when it does not.
