Claude's report for Assignment 2: first draft · annotated · edited

Claude Opus 5.5 · first draft

Data phrase: lantern meadow quartz otter (crash test 10642)

Assignment 2 · Sample Rate and Peak Loss

To: Alice Re: The 1 kHz logger from purchasing

Answer

I recommend 6,250 samples a second (6.25 kHz) as the lowest rate the lab accepts. At that rate, on our test, no peak reads more than 2.4% low wherever the logger's samples happen to fall: the crossmember on the car's structure reads 153.2 g instead of 157.0 g, and every dummy measurement stays within 0.6% of its present value.

The proposed 1 kHz logger (1,000 samples a second) gives up much more. On the same test it could read the crossmember peak 47.5% low (157.0 g read as 82.5 g, that is 1,540 m/s² read as 809 m/s²), and the dummy peaks up to 7.0% low (the driver's head, vertical, 22.6 g read as 21.1 g). Which of those readings you get depends only on where the logger's first sample happens to fall, and nothing in a 1 kHz record tells you which one you got.

Our present logger records at 12,500 samples a second (one sample every 0.08 ms). That is the spacing stored in the test file, and it is 12.5 times the proposed rate.

The evidence

The evidence is one test from our own records: NHTSA test 10642, a 2019 BMW X5 driven into a barrier. The file holds eight channels (seven on the crash dummies, one on the car), each with 4,501 samples from 50 ms before impact to 310 ms after it, all at 12,500 samples a second and all passed through a 2,000 Hz filter before they were sampled (the header's "prefilter").

The cheaper logger is not here, so I built the record it would have made out of the one we have, and read the peaks again. A logger at a lower rate keeps only some of our samples. Two things are not known and both are handled in the program (a2_peak_loss.py):

  • Where its first sample falls. It could line up with any of our samples, so I tried every possible start and kept the lowest peak (the worst start) as well as the highest. The lab cannot choose where a logger's samples fall in a crash, so I judge each rate by its worst start.
  • Rates that do not divide 12,500. At 6,250 Hz the logger keeps every 2nd sample, which is exact. At 1,000 Hz one of its intervals is 12.5 of ours, so its samples fall between ours. I gave each of its samples the value of our sample taken at or just before that instant (at most 0.08 ms early), so its gaps alternate between 12 and 13 of our samples. This gives 25 different possible starts at 1 kHz. I checked this choice against a second model (a straight line between our two neighbouring samples) and the worst-start percentages moved by at most 1.7 points, with no change to any pass or fail (Assumptions, below).

My standard: a rate is acceptable if every channel keeps at least 95% of its present peak at the worst start. I included the crossmember as well as the dummy, because the lab records both and I do not know which ones its reports use. The program removes each channel's small pre-impact offset before reading peaks and converts to g (1 g = 9.81 m/s²).

Figure 2 shows why the crossmember is so sensitive. Its peak is a single sharp swing about 11 ms after impact, from −297 to −1,540 and back to −407 m/s² over 8 of our sample intervals (0.64 ms), which is shorter than the 1 ms between two samples of the proposed logger. One start lands on the bottom of the swing. The worst start steps over it entirely, and its largest reading anywhere in the record is 82.5 g.

Figure 2. Left rear crossmember acceleration (g) from 0 to 30 ms after impact, test 10642. The grey line is our 12,500 Hz record. Blue circles are the samples a 1,000 Hz logger keeps from its best start, orange squares those from its worst start.

At 6,250 Hz (every 2nd sample) the worst a logger can do is skip the single peak sample. It then reads the sample next to it, −1,503 m/s², which is the 2.4% loss in the answer. Below 6,250 Hz it can skip two samples in a row, and the next one down is −1,333 m/s² (86.5%).

Figure 3. Worst-start peak (% of the 12,500 Hz peak) at every rate from 500 to 12,500 samples a second in steps of 250, for the crossmember and for whichever dummy channel is lowest at that rate, test 10642.

Figure 3 checks the gap between the rates in Table 1. Every rate from 6,250 Hz up keeps 97.6% of the crossmember peak, and every rate from 6,000 Hz down fails. So the boundary on this test is between 6,000 and 6,250 Hz, and a 10 kHz logger would do no better and no worse than a 6.25 kHz one.

What would change the answer

  • If the lab does not report structure peaks. On the dummy channels alone, the lowest passing rate I tried is 1,250 Hz (and 2,000 Hz on the usual 1, 2, 5 steps). 1 kHz still fails, narrowly, on the driver's head (vertical 93.0%, sideways 93.8%). With a 10% standard instead of 5% and the dummy alone, 1 kHz would pass on this test. That choice is the lab's, and it is the one thing in this report that could make the proposed logger acceptable.
  • If the crossmember swing is not real (Errors, below), the same dummy-only answer applies.

Where the evidence runs out

  • One test, one car, one structure sensor. A car with a sharper swing would lose more at the same rate, and one with a broader one less. I have no basis for saying how typical this test is.
  • A thinned record is a model of the cheaper logger. No record from it exists. A real 1 kHz logger can only represent content up to 500 Hz, so it would need its own filter, set well below that, ahead of its converter. Our record holds content up to its 2,000 Hz prefilter. A real logger's filter would probably lower the sharpest peaks further, so the 1 kHz numbers here are probably not the worst it would do. I have not checked this.
  • Our own record is a sampled record. Every peak in it is a lower bound on the true peak, and the crossmember swing is only a few of our samples wide (−949, −1,333, −1,540, −1,503, −1,262 m/s²), so its true peak could be higher than 1,540 m/s². Every percentage here is measured against a reference that may already be low, and our 12,500 Hz logger had its own unknown start.
  • Rates between our samples. Every rate from 6,250 to 12,250 Hz gives the same answer on this record, because each can skip at most one of our samples in a row. How a 7 or 10 kHz logger would fare between our 0.08 ms samples is not in the record. The step from 86.5% to 97.6% at 6,250 Hz is a property of our spacing; a real logger's loss would change more gradually.
  • Peak only. I looked at the largest value on each channel. I have not checked what the lower rate does to anything computed from the whole record (later quantities such as crush distance or injury criteria may lose more or less).

Errors and anomalies

  • Chest deflection units. The header gives metres, but the peak value is −32,798, which would be 32.8 m of chest deflection. The values are micrometres (32.8 mm), and the program reports millimetres. The percentages do not depend on the units.
  • The crossmember swing. Its consecutive samples differ by up to 579 m/s², against at most 32 m/s² on any dummy channel, and it peaks at 10.8 ms, well before any dummy peak (57 to 84 ms). A 0.6 ms swing is within what a 2,000 Hz filter passes, so I have treated it as real motion of the structure. It is the one feature that sets my rate, so if it turns out to be a sensor artefact, the dummy-only answer above applies.
  • The driver's head Y peak comes late. Its largest value, 6.23 g, is at 278.6 ms, near the end of the record (310 ms) and long after every other dummy peak. The largest value before 150 ms is 5.58 g, at 88.7 ms. I have not checked whether the late peak is real head motion or noise. It is small, and it is one of the two dummy channels that fail at 1 kHz.
  • A slower rate can read higher than a faster one. At the worst start, 1,000 Hz keeps more of the crossmember (52.5%) and the chest (97.6%) than 1,250 Hz does (51.5% and 97.3%), and in Figure 3 2,500 Hz (76.4%) beats 3,000 Hz (66.9%). This is expected, not a fault: the samples kept at one rate are not a subset of those kept at a faster one unless one rate divides the other.
  • Small offsets before impact. Every acceleration channel has a non-zero mean before impact (−0.67 to +0.87 m/s²), which I removed. Leaving it in moves no worst-start percentage at 1 kHz by more than 0.1 point.
  • Check on the program. For the crossmember, the program's start-0, worst and best peaks at 3,125, 1,250, 500 and 250 Hz (for example 793.2 m/s² worst at 1,250 Hz and 421.0 m/s² worst at 250 Hz) match the table for this same channel in the course notes on discrete evaluation.

Assumptions

  • Apart from its rate, the cheaper logger records the same values ours does at the instants it samples (same sensors, no filter of its own). See "Where the evidence runs out".
  • Every starting position is equally possible, so the worst start is a case the lab could get.
  • At rates that do not divide 12,500 (10,000, 5,000, 2,000 and 1,000 Hz and the fine sweep), the logger's sample takes the value of our sample at or just before its instant. With a straight line between our samples instead, the worst-start percentages change by at most 1.7 points (crossmember at 5,000 Hz, 86.5% against 88.2%; at 1,000 Hz, 52.5% against 51.1%), and no pass or fail changes.
  • The 95% standard, applied to every channel equally, is my choice. Alice did not give one.
  • The rates tried are the usual 1, 2, 5 steps plus every rate that divides 12,500 exactly, and a sweep every 250 Hz from 500 to 12,500 Hz.
  • Time zero is impact, as the file declares (records start at −50 ms). The offset removed is the mean of each channel over those 50 ms. One g is 9.81 m/s². The chest deflection is in micrometres.

Program

a2_peak_loss.py (submitted with this report) reads pack/crash/10642.json, prints every number above (its output is saved in run_output.txt) and draws the three figures. It runs in about a minute with Python 3, numpy and matplotlib.
