Computational Physical Modeling

Models that are linear in their parameters

In y = a + bt, the parameters appear in a linear combination.

Each parameter multiplies a known function of t, a the function 1 and b the function t, and the terms are added.

$$y \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{a} \cdot 1 \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{b} \cdot t$$

A model is linear in its parameters when it is a linear combination of known functions of x.

Each gk is a function of x with no unknowns in it, and θk (theta) is the parameter that multiplies it. For the line, the functions are 1 and x, and the parameters are a and b.

$$y \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_1}\, g_1(x) \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_2}\, g_2(x) \;+\; \dots \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_p}\, g_p(x)$$

A curved model can still be linear in its parameters.

lineara + bx + cx² This is a parabola, and c multiplies the function x².
lineara sin x + b cos 3x The functions can be anything with no unknowns in them.
lineara e−2x It has one unknown, a, and a multiplies the function e−2x.

A parameter inside a function makes the model nonlinear in its parameters.

not lineara e−kx This is the same family of curves as before, now with k fitted.
not lineara / (b + x) b is in a denominator.
not linearln(a + bx) Both parameters are inside the logarithm.

With norm 2, a model linear in its parameters has its best fit at the solution of linear equations.

A library solves those equations in one call, with a fixed amount of arithmetic and no starting guess.

linearSolve the equations The best values come from linear equations, the way 2x + 3 = 7 gives x = 2. This is called a closed form.
not linearSearch The computer guesses, improves the guess and repeats. It may stop in a dip of the sum of squares that is not the lowest one.

Fitting a parabola to the height of the shot

The height of a ball in flight is linear in all three parameters.

c0 is the height and c1 the vertical velocity at t = 0, and c2 = −½g.

$$z(t) \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0} \cdot 1 \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_1} \cdot t \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \cdot t^2 \qquad z^{\prime\prime} = 2c_2 = -g$$

Writing the model at every frame gives one equation per frame.

With measured heights, no parabola satisfies all 31 equations. Least squares finds the values that come closest.

$$\begin{aligned} 3.27 &\approx \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0} \cdot 1 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_1} \cdot 0 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \cdot 0 && \text{frame 1, } t = 0 \\[4pt] 3.50 &\approx \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0} \cdot 1 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_1} \cdot 0.04 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \cdot 0.0016 && \text{frame 2, } t = 0.04 \\[4pt] 3.74 &\approx \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0} \cdot 1 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_1} \cdot 0.08 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \cdot 0.0064 && \text{frame 3, } t = 0.08 \\[2pt] &\;\;\vdots \\[2pt] 3.43 &\approx \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0} \cdot 1 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_1} \cdot 1.2 + \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \cdot 1.44 && \text{frame 31, } t = 1.2 \end{aligned}$$

The known numbers form a table, the matrix A.

Each row is one frame’s equation, and each column is one function, 1, t or t², at each frame’s time. z is the column of heights.

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0.04 & 0.0016 \\ 1 & 0.08 & 0.0064 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & 1.20 & 1.44 \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0} \\ \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_1} \\ \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \end{bmatrix}}_{c} \;\approx\; \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3.27 \\ 3.50 \\ 3.74 \\ \vdots \\ 3.43 \end{bmatrix}}_{z}$$

The fit chooses how much of each column to add up.

In numpy, np.linalg.lstsq(A, z) returns the weight on each column of A.

$$z(t) \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{3.30} \cdot 1 \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{5.82} \cdot t \;-\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{4.81} \cdot t^2$$

The fitted parabola gives g = 9.61 m/s².

The velocity line in lecture 13 gave 9.60. The standard value is 9.81, about 2% higher.

$$g \;=\; -2\,\textcolor{#8A6508}{c_2} \;=\; -2 \times (-4.806) \;=\; 9.61\ \text{m/s}^2$$
