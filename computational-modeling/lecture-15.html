Models that least squares solves in one step

In y = a + bt, each parameter only multiplies something we know.

a multiplies 1 and b multiplies t, and both 1 and t come from the data.

$$y \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{a} \cdot 1 \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{b} \cdot t$$

A model is linear in its parameters when each parameter only multiplies.

Each gk is any function of x with no unknowns in it. The θs are a, b, … renamed, and on the last slide g1 = 1 and g2 = t.

$$y \;=\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_1}\, g_1(x) \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_2}\, g_2(x) \;+\; \dots \;+\; \textcolor{#8A6508}{\theta_p}\, g_p(x)$$

A curved model can still be linear in its parameters.

lineara + bx + cx² This is a parabola, and c multiplies x².
lineara sin x + b cos 3x The functions can be anything with no unknowns.
lineara e−2x It has one unknown, a, and a multiplies.

A parameter inside a function makes the model nonlinear in its parameters.

not lineara e−kx This is the same curve as before, now with k fitted.
not lineara / (b + x) b is in a denominator.
not linearln(a + bx) Both parameters are inside the logarithm.

Linear in its parameters means the fit can be solved in one step.

There are two ways a computer can find the best parameters. Lecture 16 shows the one-step way.

linearSolve once The best values come from equations, the way 2x + 3 = 7 gives x = 2. This is called a closed form.
not linearSearch The computer guesses, improves the guess and repeats. It may stop in a dip of the sum of squares that is not the lowest one.

Holding a known parameter fixed in a fit

The height of a ball in flight is linear in all three parameters.

c0 is the height and c1 the upward speed at t = 0, and c2 = −½g. On the shot from lecture 13, c2 = −4.806, so g = 9.61 m/s².

$$z(t) \;=\; c_0 + c_1 t + c_2 t^2 \qquad c_2 = -\tfrac{1}{2}g$$

A known term moves out of the model and into the data.

$$\begin{aligned} z &= c_0 + c_1 t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 && \text{the model, with } g \text{ known} \\[8pt] z + \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 &= c_0 + c_1 t && \text{add } \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2 \text{ to both sides} \\[8pt] \underbrace{z + 4.905\, t^2}_{\text{data}} &= \textcolor{#8A6508}{c_0 + c_1 t} && \text{two parameters left} \end{aligned}$$

Adding ½gt² to the heights turns the arc into a line.

The line gives the height and upward speed at t = 0. The RMS residual is 3.02 cm with g fitted and 3.23 cm with g held.

The matrix A has one row per frame and one column per function.

For the height, the columns are 1, t and t², evaluated at each frame’s time.

$$A \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0.04 & 0.0016 \\ 1 & 0.08 & 0.0064 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & 1.20 & 1.44 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{frame 1} \\ \leftarrow \text{frame 2} \\ \leftarrow \text{frame 3} \\ \\ \leftarrow \text{frame 31} \end{matrix}$$

In code the known term is added to the data before the solve.

Each column of A is one function a parameter multiplies, and lstsq does the least-squares fit.

# all three free
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t, t**2])
three columns, three parameters
c = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z, rcond=None)[0]
[0] keeps the coefficients
# g known: it moves into the data
A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(t), t])
the t² column is gone
c = np.linalg.lstsq(A, z + 0.5*G*t**2, rcond=None)[0]
the known term joins the data

Holding a parameter never lowers the residual.

That alone does not make the held fit worse.

fewer parametersA residual no smaller The free fit could always choose 9.81 itself.
a fixed coefficientA fixed g It cannot bend to follow noise, so a slightly larger residual buys a value we trust.

Log transforms and the weighting of a fit

Taking logs makes an exponential linear in its parameters.

This is the decay shape from lecture 13. After the fit, a is e raised to the intercept.

$$\begin{aligned} y &= a\, e^{-kx} && \text{not linear in } k \\[8pt] \ln y &= \textcolor{#8A6508}{\ln a} - \textcolor{#8A6508}{k}\, x && \text{a straight line in } x \end{aligned}$$

On y itself, the ±0.05 noise is the same size everywhere.

Eight points from y = 5e−0.4x, so the true k is 0.400.

On ln y, the same ±0.05 noise grows from 0.02 wide to 0.33.

ln y moves by about 0.05/y, so the band widens where y is small. The fit on ln y still counts those points fully.

Fit on the scale where the noise is the same size everywhere.

fixed-size noiseFit the original This needs a search and a starting guess.
noise in proportionFit on ln y The log weighting is then likely the right one.
in practiceBoth, in order The log fit gives the starting guess for the search.

Linearity depends on how the unknowns enter the model.

the testEach parameter multiplies Then least squares solves it in one step.
a known valueMove it to the data This leaves one fewer parameter and the same solver.
a transformCheck the weighting It changes which points count most.
