Writing the Labor Journal Essay

The purpose of this page is to show you what past students have done with the Labor Journal Essay, so you can see the range of experiences that make good material and the places where reflection most often falls short. It draws on 363 final essays written in my courses between 2022 and 2026 (Discrete Structures, Data Structures, Algorithms, Formal Models, Artificial Intelligence, Game Design and Computer Ethics). The page describes patterns that showed up across many of them, and it ends with an example essay written from those patterns. Please see the "Labor Journal Essay" page for detailed labor instructions.

What Past Students Wrote About

Most past essays were built from ordinary weeks. These were the most common experiences.

  • Learning group meetings. Dreading the first meeting, speaking up for the first time, arriving unprepared, or having the group explain something you thought you understood. This was the most common event in past essays (about 1 in 10 sub-themes).
  • Changing your schedule. A planner, calendar blocks or a Sunday planning session, usually after a hard first few weeks.
  • The deadline crunch. The 11:59 submission, the evening that got away from you, the assignment you put off all week.
  • Work and family. A job shift that ran long, a sibling who needed help with homework, a commute. These often make strong sub-themes because they force real choices.
  • Your learning log. Finding out where your hours actually went, or rereading an old reflection and seeing a plan you had dropped.
  • A specific assignment. A proof that would not come together, a bug that took hours, a concept that finally made sense.
  • Team projects. Dividing work, a conflict over a design, handing off unfinished code (most common in Game Design).

Other topics include an office hours visit, helping a classmate, an exam, a mistake with version control, and a time you used an AI tool and learned something about how you learn.

Experiences from outside the course (another class, a job, a past quarter) also make good sub-themes when they connect to your theme.

Start With a Moment

In past essays the strongest predictor of good reflection was how specific the experience was. Sub-themes built on one clear moment (a particular evening, a particular assignment, something a teammate said) went further at every later step of Gibbs' cycle than sub-themes built on a general habit. This is likely because a specific moment gives you something to have feelings about, something to evaluate and something to change. For instance, "I opened the week 3 assignment at 7:40 on Sunday night" gives you a time, a task and a choice to reflect on.

Make sure each sub-theme names when it happened, what you were working on, and what you did. If you kept a learning log you likely have the times and details already written down.

Feelings

Feelings were the step most often left thin in past essays. About 1 in 6 sub-themes named no feeling at all, and many others named only one general word ("stressed", "frustrated", "overwhelmed"). The purpose of this step is to notice what was actually going on for you, since that is often where the explanation for your behavior is.

It is a good idea to name more than one feeling, and to say when each one happened. Feelings are often mixed (glad you saw a friend and frustrated about the lost evening) or they change over the course of the moment (relief at submitting, followed by something closer to embarrassment). The feeling you would rather leave out often explains the most about what you did.

Action Plans

The action plan was the weakest step in past essays. About a third of sub-themes had no action plan at all, and many others ended with something like "I will manage my time better" or "I will start earlier." These plans are hard to act on because they do not say what you will do, or when.

A strong action plan is specific enough that you could check later whether you followed it. That is, it names a situation and what you will do when that situation comes up. It also follows from your analysis. If you concluded that you put off assignments because the first hour feels uncomfortable, your plan should say what you will do in that first hour.

Some past students tested their plan during the quarter and reported what happened (for instance, comparing a week before the change to a week after). If you have time to do this it makes a strong sub-theme.

Using Your Learning Log

Several of the strongest past essays used the student's own learning logs and weekly reflections as evidence. A log records what you did, which may be different from what you remember doing. Students found that their hours were not where they thought, that a "distraction" always happened right before a hard step, or that a plan they wrote in week 4 had quietly disappeared by week 6.

It is a good idea to read back through your logs before you write your outline. Look for a week that went badly and a week that went well, and compare them. What changed between those two weeks may make a strong sub-theme.

Questions to Ask Yourself

These questions point at the experiences that gave past students their strongest sub-themes. It is a good idea to answer them for yourself (your learning log will help) before you write your outline.

  1. When did you start each assignment, and what were you doing just before you started?
  2. What did you do the first time you got stuck on an assignment?
  3. Which task did you put off the longest, and how did you feel when you finally started it?
  4. Where did you spend more time than a task needed?
  5. Which week went best, and what was different about it?
  6. When did you ask for help, and what exactly did you ask?
  7. What happened in a learning group or team meeting that you still think about?
  8. Which habits from this quarter would you keep in a course with no learning group or learning log?

Common Problems

  • A theme that is only a topic. A theme names a topic (time management, for instance) and says what your experiences taught you about it.
  • Advice instead of experience. The essay describes what happened to you and what you made of it, so paragraphs written as "you should" or "students need to" tend to drift off track.
  • A story with no reflection. A well told story that ends without feelings, evaluation, analysis and a plan stops at the first step of Gibbs' cycle.
  • Gibbs as a checklist. Some essays give each step its own heading or its own sentence. The steps often read best woven into your paragraphs, with each one following from the last.
  • A vague action plan. See "Action Plans" above.

Example Essay

The essay below was written for this page from the patterns described above. As you read it, notice where each step of Gibbs' cycle shows up in each sub-theme, and that none of the steps are labeled.

Labor Journal Essay

This quarter in Algorithms was the first time I had to keep track of exactly how I spent my time on a class, and it showed me some things about myself that I did not expect. I work about twenty hours a week at a grocery store and I was also taking two other classes. At the start of the quarter I figured that any problems I had would probably be because I did not have enough time. Looking back at my learning logs and weekly reflections, I think the bigger problem was that I put off the parts of the work that I did not understand yet. Through keeping a time log, working with my learning group, and noticing my perfectionism, I learned that I make the most progress when I start the hard parts early and let other people see when I do not understand something.

The first thing I noticed came from my time log in week 3. That week's assignment was on solving recurrences, and I had watched the lecture videos early in the week on my breaks at work, so I felt pretty good about it. However I didn't actually open the assignment until Sunday evening, since Sunday is usually my only day without a shift. I ended up working on it until right before midnight. I was stuck on the first problem for a long time and felt more and more stressed as it got later. By the end I was frustrated because I knew my answer to the last problem was mostly a guess. I submitted it on time, but I did not feel good about it at all.

When I first thought about that night I told myself it was fine because I work better under pressure, which is something I have been saying for years. When I looked at my log for the first three weeks though, I saw that I never started an assignment until two days before it was due, even in weeks where I had free evenings. So time was probably not the main problem. I think what I was actually avoiding was the beginning of the assignment, when I read the problem and have no idea where to start. That part makes me uncomfortable, and if I wait until the deadline the pressure kind of forces me through it. The downside is that when I finally found out what I did not understand (proving a bound using substitution) it was 11 pm on Sunday and there was nobody I could ask.

In week 5 I tried opening the assignment on Tuesday and working on it for about 25 minutes before work. I didn't solve anything that first time and honestly it felt kind of pointless to stop with nothing done. But I wrote down where I was stuck, and when I came back on Thursday I already knew what I needed to figure out. I finished on Saturday, and even though my log showed about the same number of hours as week 3, I was a lot less stressed. I haven't kept this up every week though. In week 8 I picked up extra shifts, skipped the Tuesday session, and was right back to Sunday night. Moving forward I want to start each assignment within a day or two after it comes out, even if it is only for a short time.

Another thing that changed this quarter was how I used my learning group. For the first few weeks I mostly listened during our meetings and agreed with what other people said. I mainly used the meetings to check if my answers matched everyone else's. In week 4 someone asked if anyone could explain the recurrence tree example from the lecture, and since I had watched that part twice I volunteered. I got about halfway through and realized I could not explain how many levels the tree had or why the cost at each level was getting smaller. When someone asked me where the log base 4 came from I had no idea. I was really embarrassed and wanted to just say never mind and let someone else do it.

Instead I told the group I didn't actually understand that part. Two other people said they were confused about the same thing, so we worked through it together. It took about ten minutes to see that each level splits into three problems that are a quarter of the size, so it takes log base 4 of n levels to get down to size 1, and the total cost at each level is 3/4 of the level above it. I think this was one of the most useful meetings we had all quarter. I thought I understood the example because I followed along with the video, but I could not explain it on my own, so I probably did not understand it as well as I thought. After that, one of the other people in my group asked about the next example too, which they told me later they had been too nervous to do before. Going forward I plan to try explaining at least one example out loud before each meeting, and bring whatever part I get stuck on to the group.

The last thing I noticed was during the week 7 assignment, which asked us to prove that a greedy scheduling algorithm was optimal. I got the main proof working on Wednesday, which was early for me, and I was proud of it. However I then spent a lot of Thursday and Friday rewriting it to make it cleaner. My log shows around three hours just on cleaning up that one proof. During that time I did not touch the second problem, which was the one I did not understand, and I ended up rushing it on Saturday night anyway.

At the time it felt like I was being productive and I enjoyed making the proof look good. When I looked at my log later I was pretty annoyed with myself, because I realized the polishing was mostly an excuse to avoid the problem I was stuck on. I have always thought of myself as a perfectionist, and I thought that was a separate issue from putting things off. I think in this case the perfectionism was really just another way of putting off the hard problem. In the future I am going to try to get a first attempt on every problem before I go back and polish anything.

In conclusion, this quarter taught me that my problems with deadlines were less about time than I thought. Most of the time when I was behind, it was because I was avoiding the part I didn't understand yet. Starting early, explaining things out loud before meetings and getting a first attempt on everything before polishing are the changes I want to keep, although week 8 showed me that this is easy to slip on when work gets busy. Next quarter I am going to keep a time log in my classes even if it is not required, since that is what helped me see all of this in the first place.

Note: The example essay is about 1,200 words, near the top of the 900 to 1200 word range for the draft.
