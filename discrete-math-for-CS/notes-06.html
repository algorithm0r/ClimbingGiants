Why Asymptotic Notation?

In Week 4 we derived exact runtime functions for three algorithms by counting every operation. The minimum algorithm cost $4n + 1$ operations in the worst case; the sequential search cost $4n + 5$; the string-equality check cost $4n + 7$. These are precise, complete answers — but their precision is part of the problem.

The dominance of the leading term

Consider what those three functions actually say about the relative cost of the algorithms. For small inputs the constant terms matter a great deal: at $n = 0$, the ratio of $4n + 7$ to $4n + 1$ is $7$, which seems large. But as $n$ grows, something changes. Look at the table below:

$n$ $4n+1$ $4n+5$ $4n+7$ Max difference
$0$ $1$ $5$ $7$ $7\times$ larger
$10$ $41$ $45$ $47$ $\approx 15\%$ larger
$100$ $401$ $405$ $407$ $\approx 1.5\%$ larger
$1000$ $4001$ $4005$ $4007$ $\approx 0.15\%$ larger

The absolute difference between any two of these functions is always at most 6 — a fixed constant, independent of $n$. Yet as $n$ increases the relative significance of that difference shrinks toward zero. At $n = 1{,}000{,}000$, the constant offset of 6 is completely negligible compared to the $4{,}000{,}000$ contributed by the linear term. The leading term $4n$ dominates everything else in the long run.

This is a general principle: for a polynomial like $4n + 7$, the highest-degree term is the one that matters for large $n$; the lower-order terms and constant multipliers are noise. Asymptotic notation is the formal machinery for expressing that principle precisely.

The goals of asymptotic notation

There are two related things we want to do:

  1. Classify functions by their growth rate, ignoring constant multipliers and lower-order terms. We want to say "$4n + 1$, $4n + 5$, and $4n + 7$ are all essentially the same" because they all grow linearly.
  2. Compare functions from different growth classes. We want to be able to say definitively that a quadratic function grows faster than a linear one as $n \to \infty$, even if for small $n$ the linear function happens to be larger (due to a very large constant coefficient).

Both goals require talking about the behavior of functions eventually — for large enough $n$ — rather than for all $n$. The asymptotic notations $O$, $\Omega$, $\Theta$, $o$, and $\omega$ each capture a different aspect of this eventual behavior.

Big-Oh: Upper Bounds

Formal definition

Let $f$ and $g$ be functions from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{R}^+$. We say $f(n)$ is in big-Oh of $g(n)$, written

$$f(n) \in O(g(n)), $$

if there exist positive constants $b$ and $n_0$ such that

$$f(n) \leq b \cdot g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. $$

The definition has three moving parts worth understanding individually.

The constants $b$ and $n_0$. We are not required to find the smallest $b$ or the smallest $n_0$ — only to show that some pair exists. This is an existential claim: to prove that $f \in O(g)$ you just need to exhibit any $b$ and $n_0$ that make the inequality hold. You do not need to find the optimal ones, and you do not need to check that the inequality holds for all possible constants.

The threshold $n_0$. The inequality $f(n) \leq b \cdot g(n)$ only has to hold for $n \geq n_0$. For $n < n_0$ the two functions may behave however they like. This is what "asymptotic" means: we care about eventual behavior, not behavior near the origin. Choosing a larger $n_0$ gives you more room to work with; if the inequality is messy to establish for small $n$, you can simply push $n_0$ out far enough that the messy part lies below the threshold.

The multiplier $b$. The factor $b$ accounts for constant multipliers. It says: "$f$ might be larger than $g$ pointwise, but once we scale $g$ up by a factor of $b$, it dominates $f$ for all large $n$." This is what "up to a constant factor" means. Because $b$ is free to be any positive constant, the constant multiplier $4$ in $4n + 1$ is irrelevant — we can absorb it into $b$.

Intuition

The intuitive reading of $f(n) \in O(g(n))$ is:

$f$ grows no faster than $g$, up to a constant factor and for large enough $n$.

Equivalently, $g$ is an asymptotic upper bound for $f$. The key word is upper: Big-Oh says $g$ is at least as large as $f$ in the long run (after scaling). It does not say anything about $f$ being as large as $g$.

Worked example: $4n + 1 \in O(n)$

We want to show that $f(n) = 4n + 1$ is in $O(n)$, meaning $g(n) = n$.

We need to find constants $b > 0$ and $n_0 > 0$ such that $4n + 1 \leq b \cdot n$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Strategy: choose $b$ generously, then find $n_0$ that makes the inequality true.

Try $b = 5$. We need $4n + 1 \leq 5n$, which simplifies to $1 \leq n$. So the inequality holds for all $n \geq 1$.

Setting $n_0 = 1$, we have: for all $n \geq 1$,

$$4n + 1 \leq 4n + n = 5n, $$

where the first inequality used $1 \leq n$. The constants $b = 5$ and $n_0 = 1$ witness $4n + 1 \in O(n)$. $\square$

The same argument works for $4n + 5$ and $4n + 7$: both are $O(n)$ with constants that are easy to find. All three Week 4 runtime functions live in $O(n)$.

Worked example: $n^2 \notin O(n)$

It is equally important to know how to show that a bound does not hold. Suppose for contradiction that $n^2 \in O(n)$: then there would exist constants $b$ and $n_0$ with $n^2 \leq b \cdot n$ for all $n \geq n_0$. Dividing both sides by $n$ (valid for $n > 0$), this would require $n \leq b$ for all $n \geq n_0$ — but no fixed constant $b$ can bound all sufficiently large $n$. Contradiction. Therefore $n^2 \notin O(n)$. $\square$

Big-Omega: Lower Bounds

Formal definition

We say $f(n)$ is in big-Omega of $g(n)$, written

$$f(n) \in \Omega(g(n)), $$

if there exist positive constants $a$ and $n_0$ such that

$$f(n) \geq a \cdot g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. $$

The only change from the Big-Oh definition is the direction of the inequality: $\leq$ becomes $\geq$. Everything else — the existential quantification over $a$ and $n_0$, the threshold $n_0$, the freedom to choose any positive $a$ — is identical. (The constant is renamed from $b$ to $a$ to signal that when both bounds are present they may take different values, though the same $n_0$ can often serve both.)

Intuition

The intuitive reading of $f(n) \in \Omega(g(n))$ is:

$f$ grows at least as fast as $g$, up to a constant factor and for large enough $n$.

$g$ is an asymptotic lower bound for $f$. Where $O$ is an upper bound ("at most this fast"), $\Omega$ is a lower bound ("at least this fast").

Worked example: $4n + 1 \in \Omega(n)$

We need constants $a > 0$ and $n_0 > 0$ such that $4n + 1 \geq a \cdot n$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Since $4n + 1 > 4n$ for all $n \geq 0$, we immediately have $4n + 1 \geq 4n$ — so $a = 4$ and $n_0 = 0$ witness $4n + 1 \in \Omega(n)$. $\square$

Big-Theta: Tight Bounds

Formal definition

We say $f(n)$ is in big-Theta of $g(n)$, written

$$f(n) \in \Theta(g(n)), $$

if $f(n) \in O(g(n))$ and $f(n) \in \Omega(g(n))$.

Unrolling both definitions, this means there exist positive constants $a$, $b$, and $n_0$ such that

$$a \cdot g(n) \leq f(n) \leq b \cdot g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. $$

$f$ is sandwiched between two scaled copies of $g$. Note that a single $n_0$ can serve as the cutoff for both inequalities — just take the larger of the two $n_0$ values that would work for $O$ and $\Omega$ separately.

Intuition

$f \in \Theta(g)$ means $f$ and $g$ have the same growth rate up to constant factors. The analogy with the number line is direct: if $f \leq g$ (up to constants) and $f \geq g$ (up to constants), then $f$ and $g$ must be essentially equal. Big-Theta is the asymptotic equality relation.

When $f \in \Theta(g)$ we often say:

  • "$f$ and $g$ are asymptotically equivalent";
  • "$g$ is a tight bound for $f$";
  • "$f$ and $g$ have the same order of growth."

Worked example: $4n + 1 \in \Theta(n)$

We have already shown $4n + 1 \in O(n)$ (with $b = 5$, $n_0 = 1$) and $4n + 1 \in \Omega(n)$ (with $a = 4$, $n_0 = 0$). Setting $n_0 = 1$ for both,

$$4n \leq 4n + 1 \leq 5n \quad \text{for all } n \geq 1, $$

which establishes $4n + 1 \in \Theta(n)$ with constants $a = 4$, $b = 5$, $n_0 = 1$. $\square$

By the same reasoning, $4n + 5 \in \Theta(n)$ and $4n + 7 \in \Theta(n)$. All three Week 4 runtime functions have the same asymptotic growth rate — linear — even though their exact operation counts differ by constants.

Proving Bounds from the Definition

The general technique

To prove $f(n) \in O(g(n))$ from the definition:

  1. Write down the goal: find constants $b > 0$ and $n_0 > 0$ such that $f(n) \leq b \cdot g(n)$ for all $n \geq n_0$.
  2. Choose a generous $b$: pick a value that absorbs all the constant coefficients of $f$. It does not have to be tight.
  3. Establish $n_0$: rearrange $f(n) \leq b \cdot g(n)$ to find the smallest $n$ for which it holds; set $n_0$ to that value (or any larger value).
  4. Write the proof: state your $b$ and $n_0$, then show the inequality holds for all $n \geq n_0$.

The key algebraic moves are:

  • Drop lower-order terms: $4n + 1 \leq 4n + n = 5n$ when $n \geq 1$ (because $1 \leq n$).
  • Replace a smaller term with a larger one: $\log n \leq n$ for all $n \geq 1$, so $n \log n \leq n \cdot n = n^2$.
  • Factor and simplify: after bounding each term individually, combine the results.

For $\Omega$, the moves are reversed: you bound $f$ from below by dropping positive terms.

Example: a polynomial is $O$ of its leading term

Claim: $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in O(n^2)$.

Proof: For $n \geq 1$, the constant $5 \leq 5n^2$ (since $n^2 \geq 1$). For $n \geq 6$, the term $6n \leq 6n^2$. However, we can give a cleaner argument using the triangle inequality: for $n \geq 1$,

$$n^2 - 6n + 5 \leq n^2 + 5 \leq n^2 + 5n^2 = 6n^2, $$

where the second step uses $5 \leq 5n^2 \leq 5n^2$ once $n \geq 1$.

Setting $b = 6$ and $n_0 = 1$ witnesses $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in O(n^2)$. $\square$

Claim: $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in \Omega(n^2)$.

Proof: We need to bound $n^2 - 6n + 5$ from below by $a \cdot n^2$ for large $n$. For $n \geq 12$, note that $6n \leq \tfrac{1}{2} n^2$ (equivalent to $12 \leq n$), so:

$$n^2 - 6n + 5 \geq n^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} n^2 + 5 \geq \tfrac{1}{2} n^2. $$

Setting $a = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $n_0 = 12$ witnesses $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in \Omega(n^2)$. $\square$

Combining, $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in \Theta(n^2)$: the function is asymptotically quadratic, as expected from its highest-degree term.

Why constant multipliers do not matter

The freedom to choose $b$ in the Big-Oh definition is precisely what makes constant multipliers irrelevant. Given $f(n) = c \cdot n$ for any positive constant $c$, we immediately have $f(n) \leq c \cdot n \cdot 1 = c \cdot n$, so $f \in O(n)$ with $b = c$ and $n_0 = 1$. A function with a bigger constant coefficient is $O(n)$ just as much as one with a small coefficient — both are linear.

This is what allows us to say $4n + 1$, $4n + 5$, and $4n + 7$ are all "the same" asymptotically: the constant offsets and the coefficient 4 are all absorbed by the freedom in choosing $b$.

Little-o and Little-omega

Filling the gap in our intuition

Big-Oh, Big-Omega, and Big-Theta correspond to $\leq$, $\geq$, and $=$ respectively in the asymptotic sense. But numbers also have a strict less-than $<$ and a strict greater-than $>$. The notations little-o and little-omega fill those gaps.

Little-o: strict upper bound

We say $f(n)$ is in little-o of $g(n)$, written

$$f(n) \in o(g(n)), $$

if for every positive constant $b$ there exists a positive constant $n_0$ such that

$$f(n) < b \cdot g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. $$

The crucial difference from Big-Oh: the existential quantifier and universal quantifier have swapped roles. Big-Oh says: there exists a $b$ that works. Little-o says: every $b$ works, no matter how small — as long as we push $n_0$ out far enough.

Intuitively, $f \in o(g)$ means $g$ grows strictly faster than $f$: no matter how small a constant you multiply $g$ by, $g$ will eventually dominate $f$.

Little-omega: strict lower bound

We say $f(n)$ is in little-omega of $g(n)$, written

$$f(n) \in \omega(g(n)), $$

if for every positive constant $a$ there exists a positive constant $n_0$ such that

$$f(n) > a \cdot g(n) \quad \text{for all } n \geq n_0. $$

Little-omega is the strict lower-bound counterpart: $f$ grows strictly faster than $g$.

The complete picture

Notation Meaning Analogy
$f \in O(g)$ $f$ grows no faster than $g$ $f \leq g$
$f \in \Omega(g)$ $f$ grows no slower than $g$ $f \geq g$
$f \in \Theta(g)$ $f$ and $g$ grow at the same rate $f = g$
$f \in o(g)$ $f$ grows strictly slower than $g$ $f < g$
$f \in \omega(g)$ $f$ grows strictly faster than $g$ $f > g$

Note that the analogy is imperfect in one important respect: two functions can both be in $O(g)$ without having the same growth rate as each other. The table captures the relationship of each function to $g$, not a total order on all functions.

The Limit Test

Motivation

Proving bounds from the definition requires algebraic manipulation — finding explicit constants $b$ and $n_0$. For complex functions (products, compositions, exponentials), that algebra can become unwieldy. The limit test offers an alternative route that leverages calculus.

Statement of the limit test

Let $f$ and $g$ be eventually-positive functions, and suppose the limit

$$L = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} $$

exists (possibly as $0$ or $\infty$). Then:

$$\begin{cases} L = 0 & \Rightarrow f \in o(g) \text{ and } f \in O(g) \\ 0 < L < \infty & \Rightarrow f \in \Theta(g) \\ L = \infty & \Rightarrow f \in \omega(g) \text{ and } f \in \Omega(g) \end{cases}$$

The intuition is direct: the ratio $f(n)/g(n)$ tells us how large $f$ is relative to $g$ for large $n$. If the ratio goes to zero, $f$ is eventually negligible compared to $g$. If the ratio goes to infinity, $f$ eventually dominates $g$. If the ratio stabilizes at a positive constant $L$, the two functions grow at the same rate (they are within a constant factor of each other), placing them in $\Theta$ of each other.

Example: $n \in o(n^2)$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{n^2} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0. $$

Since $L = 0$, we conclude $n \in o(n^2)$: linear functions grow strictly slower than quadratic ones.

Example: $4n + 1 \in \Theta(n)$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{4n + 1}{n} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(4 + \frac{1}{n}\right) = 4. $$

Since $L = 4$, a positive finite constant, we conclude $4n + 1 \in \Theta(n)$. This confirms what we proved from the definition earlier.

Example: $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in \Theta(n^2)$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n^2 - 6n + 5}{n^2} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 - \frac{6}{n} + \frac{5}{n^2}\right) = 1. $$

Distributing the limit across the three terms: $\lim 1 = 1$, $\lim 6/n = 0$, $\lim 5/n^2 = 0$, so $L = 1 > 0$. Therefore $n^2 - 6n + 5 \in \Theta(n^2)$.

Using L'Hôpital's rule

When the limit produces the indeterminate form $\infty/\infty$ or $0/0$, L'Hôpital's rule applies: if both $f(n) \to \infty$ and $g(n) \to \infty$, then

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f(n)}{g(n)} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{f'(n)}{g'(n)}, $$

provided the latter limit exists. This is particularly useful for comparing polynomials against exponentials, or logarithms against polynomials.

Example: $\log_2 n \in o(n)$.

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\log_2 n}{n} \overset{\text{L'H}}{=} \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1/(n \ln 2)}{1} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n \ln 2} = 0. $$

Therefore $\log_2 n \in o(n)$: logarithms grow strictly slower than any linear function.

When the limit test applies vs. the definition

The limit test is convenient for most elementary functions. However, it requires that the limit exist. Functions that oscillate or are not eventually-monotone may not have a limit of the ratio, and in those cases the definition must be used directly. In practice, the limit test handles virtually all runtime functions that arise in algorithm analysis.

Common Growth Classes

The following hierarchy of growth classes arises repeatedly in algorithm analysis. The notation $A \subset B$ means "every function in class $A$ is also in $O(g)$ for $g \in B$, but not vice versa" — i.e., $A$ grows strictly slower than $B$:

$$O(1) \subset O(\log n) \subset O(n) \subset O(n \log n) \subset O(n^2) \subset O(n^3) \subset O(2^n) $$

Each inclusion is strict, meaning functions in the smaller class are in little-o of any function in the larger class.

Class Common name Typical algorithm
$O(1)$ Constant Array index lookup
$O(\log n)$ Logarithmic Binary search
$O(n)$ Linear Sequential search, MinAlg
$O(n \log n)$ Linearithmic Mergesort, Heapsort
$O(n^2)$ Quadratic Bubble sort, insertion sort
$O(n^3)$ Cubic Naive matrix multiplication
$O(2^n)$ Exponential Brute-force subset enumeration

Why the hierarchy matters

Going from $O(n)$ to $O(n^2)$ might seem like a modest step — two different exponents. But the practical consequences are enormous. An $O(n)$ algorithm running on $n = 10^6$ inputs performs on the order of $10^6$ operations — fast. An $O(n^2)$ algorithm performs on the order of $10^{12}$ operations on the same input — likely too slow to be useful without a much faster computer.

The jump from polynomial to exponential is even more dramatic. No polynomial algorithm grows as fast as $2^n$: even $n^{100}$ is in $o(2^n)$. An exponential algorithm with $n = 100$ inputs requires more operations than there are atoms in the observable universe.

Key relationships to remember

  • Every polynomial of degree $k$ is in $\Theta(n^k)$ — only the leading term matters.
  • $n^k \in o(2^n)$ for any fixed $k$ — exponentials dominate all polynomials.
  • $(\log n)^k \in o(n)$ for any fixed $k$ — any power of a logarithm is eventually dominated by $n$.
  • $n^k \in o(n^{k+\epsilon})$ for any $\epsilon > 0$ — polynomial growth classes are dense.

Exercise: Ordering Functions by Growth Rate

This section walks through the type of exercise in Video 4: given a collection of functions, determine the $\Theta$ class of each and then order them from slowest-growing to fastest-growing.

The five functions

Suppose we are given:

  1. $f_1(n) = n^2 - 6n + 5$
  2. $f_2(n) = (2n + 5)^8$
  3. $f_3(n) = 2^n$
  4. $f_4(n) = n \log_2 n$
  5. $f_5(n) = 100$

Our task: find the $\Theta$ class of each, then sort them.

Step 1: Find the $\Theta$ class of each function

$f_1(n) = n^2 - 6n + 5 \in \Theta(n^2)$.

The limit test gives $\lim_{n\to\infty} (n^2 - 6n + 5)/n^2 = 1$, a positive constant. So $f_1 \in \Theta(n^2)$.

$f_2(n) = (2n + 5)^8 \in \Theta(n^8)$.

Here the function is a polynomial of degree 8 in disguise. One approach is to use the limit test directly:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{(2n + 5)^8}{n^8} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(\frac{2n + 5}{n}\right)^8 = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(2 + \frac{5}{n}\right)^8 = 2^8 = 256. $$

Since $L = 256 > 0$ is a finite positive constant, $(2n+5)^8 \in \Theta(n^8)$.

An alternative: expand $(2n + 5)^8$ by the binomial theorem to obtain $256n^8 + \text{lower-order terms}$. The leading coefficient $256$ does not affect the $\Theta$ class, and all lower-order terms are in $o(n^8)$. Either approach confirms the result.

Note that attempting to prove this from the definition directly involves either expanding an eighth power — tedious — or choosing $n_0$ large enough to handle the expansion cleanly. The limit test is considerably more efficient here.

$f_3(n) = 2^n \in \Theta(2^n)$.

This is trivially true: $2^n \leq 1 \cdot 2^n \leq 1 \cdot 2^n$ for all $n \geq 0$.

$f_4(n) = n \log_2 n \in \Theta(n \log n)$.

The limit test gives:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n \log_2 n}{n \log_2 n} = 1. $$

This is immediate (the function equals its own target), so $f_4 \in \Theta(n \log n)$.

$f_5(n) = 100 \in \Theta(1)$.

A constant function: $100 = 100 \cdot 1$ for all $n$, so $f_5 \in O(1)$; and $100 \geq 100 \cdot 1$, so $f_5 \in \Omega(1)$. Therefore $f_5 \in \Theta(1)$.

Step 2: Order the growth classes

We now have:

Function $\Theta$ class
$f_5(n) = 100$ $\Theta(1)$
$f_4(n) = n \log_2 n$ $\Theta(n \log n)$
$f_1(n) = n^2 - 6n + 5$ $\Theta(n^2)$
$f_2(n) = (2n+5)^8$ $\Theta(n^8)$
$f_3(n) = 2^n$ $\Theta(2^n)$

Placing them in increasing order of asymptotic growth rate, from slowest to fastest:

$$100 \prec n \log n \prec n^2 \prec n^8 \prec 2^n $$

where $f \prec g$ means $f \in o(g)$ (strict asymptotic domination). Each relationship can be confirmed with the limit test:

  • $\lim_{n\to\infty} 100 / (n \log n) = 0$: constant is $o(n \log n)$. $\checkmark$
  • $\lim_{n\to\infty} (n \log n) / n^2 = \lim_{n\to\infty} (\log n)/n = 0$ (by L'Hôpital or known hierarchy): $n \log n$ is $o(n^2)$. $\checkmark$
  • $\lim_{n\to\infty} n^2 / n^8 = \lim_{n\to\infty} n^{-6} = 0$: quadratic is $o(n^8)$. $\checkmark$
  • $\lim_{n\to\infty} n^8 / 2^n = 0$ (exponentials dominate polynomials; can be shown by eight applications of L'Hôpital): $n^8$ is $o(2^n)$. $\checkmark$

Lessons from the exercise

Three practical takeaways:

  1. Simplify first. Functions like $(2n + 5)^8$ are intimidating until you recognize them as polynomials in disguise. Looking for the dominant term — whether by expanding, by the limit test, or by the binomial theorem — quickly determines the $\Theta$ class.

  2. Choose your tool. The definition is direct and rigorous; the limit test is often faster for smooth functions. Knowing both gives you flexibility. For the $(2n+5)^8$ example, the limit test turns a potentially messy binomial expansion into a one-line calculation.

  3. The hierarchy is absolute. An exponential function will always eventually dominate a polynomial, regardless of constant coefficients. Even $0.0001 \cdot 2^n$ eventually exceeds $10^{100} \cdot n^{1000}$.

Connections

Backward — Week 4 Notes. The runtime functions $4n + 1$, $4n + 5$, and $4n + 7$ derived by exact operation counting in Week 4 are the direct motivation for Big-Oh. Week 4 shows how to get an exact count; Week 6 shows how to express the result concisely by discarding the constant offsets and coefficients that are negligible for large $n$. The progression from exact counts to asymptotic bounds is the natural arc of algorithm analysis.

Forward — Week 7 Notes. Sorting algorithms are the canonical application of asymptotic analysis. Insertion sort runs in $\Theta(n^2)$ in the worst case; mergesort runs in $\Theta(n \log n)$. The Big-Oh and Big-Theta machinery developed this week is the language in which those claims are stated and proved.

Forward — Week 8 Notes. Recursive algorithms do not admit straightforward operation-count arguments — their runtime satisfies a recurrence relation rather than a closed-form expression. Techniques like the Master Theorem resolve those recurrences into closed-form asymptotic bounds, which are then interpreted using exactly the $O$, $\Omega$, and $\Theta$ notation established this week.
