Strong Induction

Why weak induction is sometimes not enough

The induction introduced in Week 4 Notes — call it weak induction — has the following structure: to prove $P(n)$ for all $n \geq n_0$, assume $P(n)$ holds and derive $P(n+1)$. The inductive hypothesis covers exactly one prior case.

This works perfectly when the proof for case $n+1$ only needs to lean on case $n$. But many recursive algorithms do not have this shape. The Fibonacci sequence, for example, is defined by

$$F(n) = F(n-1) + F(n-2), $$

which requires two prior cases. Binary search reduces a problem of size $n$ to one of size $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$, which for large $n$ is far below $n-1$. Weak induction's single-step hypothesis is not wide enough to cover either situation.

The formal statement

Theorem (Strong Induction). Let $P(n)$ be a predicate over the integers. Suppose:

  1. Base case(s): $P(n_0), P(n_0 + 1), \ldots, P(n_1)$ are all true for some $n_1 \geq n_0$.
  2. Inductive step: For all $n > n_1$, if $P(k)$ holds for every integer $k$ with $n_0 \leq k < n$, then $P(n)$ holds.

Then $P(n)$ is true for all $n \geq n_0$.

The critical difference from weak induction is in the hypothesis of the inductive step: instead of assuming $P(n)$ to prove $P(n+1)$, we assume $P(k)$ for all $k < n$ and prove $P(n)$.

Rewriting the inductive hypothesis

When carrying out a strong induction proof it is common to write the inductive hypothesis in one of two equivalent forms:

  • General form: Assume $T(k) \leq B \cdot f(k)$ for all integers $k$ with $n_0 \leq k < n$.
  • Targeted form: Assume $T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) \leq B \cdot f(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor)$.

The general form is more versatile; the targeted form is more readable when only one sub-problem size appears in the recurrence. Either is valid: the targeted form is just the general form applied to the specific $k = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ that will appear in the next step.

Why more base cases may be needed

A subtle issue arises with strong induction that rarely surfaces in weak induction: the base case(s) must cover every value that the inductive step cannot reach by unwinding the recurrence. Consider a recurrence $T(n) = T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) + c$ starting from $n = 1$. For even $n$, $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ eventually reaches 1, so the base case $n = 1$ is sufficient. But if $n = 1$ makes the thing we are trying to prove undefined (for example, because it requires $\log_2 1 = 0$ in a denominator), we must move $n_0$ upward and verify all small cases individually.

Structural comparison: weak vs. strong induction

In weak induction the dependency chain is linear:

$$n \;\longleftarrow\; n-1 \;\longleftarrow\; n-2 \;\longleftarrow\; \cdots \;\longleftarrow\; n_0. $$

In strong induction the dependency graph can skip levels:

$$n \;\longleftarrow\; \lfloor n/2 \rfloor \;\longleftarrow\; \lfloor n/4 \rfloor \;\longleftarrow\; \cdots \;\longleftarrow\; n_0. $$

All weak induction proofs can be recast as strong induction proofs; the converse is false. Whenever a recurrence's sub-problem is anything other than $n - 1$, strong induction is the appropriate tool.

Recursive Insertion Sort

The algorithm

Insertion sort can be expressed recursively. The key observation: to sort a list of $n$ elements, sort the first $n - 1$ elements recursively, then insert the $n$-th element into its correct position among the already-sorted prefix.

InsertionSort(A, n):
  if n <= 1:
    return A
  A' = InsertionSort(A, n - 1)
  A'.add(A[n])          // append A[n] to the end of the sorted prefix
  j = n
  while j > 1 and A[j] < A[j-1]:
    swap A[j] and A[j-1]
    j = j - 1
  return A'

The recursive call sorts $A[1..n-1]$. The while loop then shuffles $A[n]$ leftward until it is in its correct position. In the best case $A[n]$ is already the maximum and the loop does not execute. In the worst case $A[n]$ is the minimum and it must travel all the way to position 1, performing $n - 1$ swaps.

Setting up the recurrence

Counting operations at mid-level resolution in the worst case:

  • The base case ($n \leq 1$) costs a fixed constant: $T(1) = 3$ (one method call, one comparison, one return).
  • For $n > 1$: one recursive call of size $n - 1$, plus the cost of the while loop and surrounding overhead.

The while loop executes with $j$ ranging from $n$ down to $2$ (worst case), giving $n - 1$ iterations. Counting the operations per iteration and the surrounding statements yields:

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + 8n + 3, \qquad n > 1. $$

In simplified form, absorbing constants:

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + 8n + 3. $$

Recursive Insertion Sort Analysis

Unrolling the recurrence

To guess a closed form, apply repeated substitution (algebraic unwinding). Replace $T(n-1)$ with its own definition, then $T(n-2)$, and so on:

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + 8n + 3 $$ $$= T(n-2) + 8(n-1) + 3 + 8n + 3 $$ $$= T(n-3) + 8(n-2) + 3 + 8(n-1) + 3 + 8n + 3 $$ $$= \cdots $$

After $n - 1$ substitutions the recursion bottoms out at $T(1) = 3$. Collecting terms:

$$T(n) = 3 + \sum_{j=0}^{n-2} \bigl(8(n - j) + 3\bigr). $$

Distributing and applying Gauss's sum gives the closed form

$$T(n) = 4n^2 + 7n - 8. $$

The dominant term is $4n^2$, so $T(n) \in \Theta(n^2)$.

Proving the upper bound by strong induction

Rather than proving the exact formula, it is often easier to prove a bound. We prove $T(n) \leq Bn^2$ for a suitable constant $B$.

Theorem. There exists a constant $B > 0$ and an integer $n_0 \geq 1$ such that $T(n) \leq Bn^2$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Proof.

Base case ($n = 1$): $T(1) = 3$ and $B \cdot 1^2 = B$. The inequality $3 \leq B$ holds provided $B \geq 3$. This is a condition we will carry forward.

Inductive hypothesis: Assume $T(k) \leq Bk^2$ for all $k < n$ (specifically for $k = n-1$).

Inductive step ($n > 1$):

Starting from the recurrence definition:

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + 8n + 3. $$

Apply the inductive hypothesis to $T(n-1)$:

$$T(n) \leq B(n-1)^2 + 8n + 3. $$

Expand $(n-1)^2$:

$$= Bn^2 - 2Bn + B + 8n + 3. $$

We wish to show this is at most $Bn^2$. The leap of faith is:

$$Bn^2 - 2Bn + B + 8n + 3 \leq Bn^2. $$

This simplifies to

$$-2Bn + B + 8n + 3 \leq 0. $$

Promote the constants: since $n \geq 1$, we have $B \leq Bn$ and $3 \leq 3n$, so

$$-2Bn + B + 8n + 3 \leq -2Bn + Bn + 8n + 3n = n(-B + 11). $$

For this to be $\leq 0$ we need $B \geq 11$.

Choosing constants: We have two conditions: $B \geq 3$ (from the base case) and $B \geq 11$ (from the inductive step). Setting $B = 11$ satisfies both. Take $n_0 = 1$.

Conclusion: By induction, $T(n) \leq 11n^2$ for all $n \geq 1$. Hence $T(n) \in O(n^2)$. $\blacksquare$

Proving the lower bound by strong induction

Theorem. There exists a constant $a > 0$ such that $T(n) \geq an^2$ for all $n \geq 1$.

Proof.

Base case ($n = 1$): $T(1) = 3 \geq a \cdot 1$. This holds for any $a \leq 3$.

Inductive hypothesis: Assume $T(n-1) \geq a(n-1)^2$.

Inductive step ($n > 1$):

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + 8n + 3 \geq a(n-1)^2 + 8n + 3. $$

Expand:

$$= an^2 - 2an + a + 8n + 3. $$

We wish to show this is at least $an^2$:

$$an^2 - 2an + a + 8n + 3 \geq an^2. $$

This simplifies to $-2an + a + 8n + 3 \geq 0$. Since $a + 3 > 0$, we can discard that positive term and ask when $n(8 - 2a) \geq 0$, which holds for all $n \geq 1$ provided $a \leq 4$.

Choosing constants: Two conditions: $a \leq 3$ and $a \leq 4$. Setting $a = 3$ satisfies both. Take $n_0 = 1$.

Conclusion: By induction, $T(n) \geq 3n^2$ for all $n \geq 1$. Hence $T(n) \in \Omega(n^2)$. $\blacksquare$

Result

Combining both bounds:

$$T(n) \in \Theta(n^2). $$

Recursive insertion sort has the same asymptotic cost as its iterative counterpart. The recursion adds no asymptotic savings.

Using Strong Induction to Prove Recurrence Bounds

The insertion sort analysis above illustrates a general technique that can be applied to any recurrence relation when an exact closed form is difficult to find or unnecessary.

The technique in four steps

  1. State the bound. Formulate the claim as $T(n) \leq B \cdot f(n)$ (for an upper bound) or $T(n) \geq a \cdot f(n)$ (for a lower bound), where $f$ is your guessed asymptotic class and $B$, $a$ are unknown positive constants.

  2. Verify the base case(s). Plug in small values. If $f(n_0) = 0$ makes the inequality trivially false (as happens with $\log_2 1 = 0$ on the upper side), move to the next value or augment the bound with an additive constant.

  3. Apply the inductive hypothesis. Replace the recursive term $T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor)$ (or $T(n-1)$, etc.) with the hypothesized bound. Use $\leq$ when proving upper bounds, $\geq$ for lower bounds.

  4. Make the leap of faith and solve for the constant. After algebra, the inequality reduces to a condition on $B$ (or $a$). Confirm that such a constant exists; any positive real number satisfying the condition is a valid choice.

Upper vs. lower bound asymmetry

A key asymmetry appears when floors and ceilings are involved.

  • Upper bound: Replace $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ with $n/2$. The floor is never larger than $n/2$, so this replacement makes the argument of $T$ larger, which increases the right-hand side of the $\leq$ and keeps the inequality valid.
  • Lower bound: Replace $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ with $n/4$. Since the floor of $n/2$ is at least $n/4$ for $n \geq 2$, this makes the argument smaller, which decreases the right-hand side of the $\geq$ and keeps that inequality valid.

The choice of $n/4$ (a power of 2 in the denominator) is deliberate: it makes logarithmic algebra clean.

The "plus $a$" trick for base cases

When $f(1) = 0$ causes the upper-bound base case to fail, augment the guessed bound to $T(n) \leq Bn \log_2 n + a$. The additive constant $a$ belongs to the same asymptotic class as $Bn \log_2 n$ (it only changes the implicit constant), so the theorem is unchanged in strength. The payoff: at $n = 1$ the right-hand side becomes $0 + a = a$, and choosing $a \geq T(1)$ makes the base case valid without moving $n_0$ upward.

Merge Sort

The divide-and-conquer idea

Merge sort is a recursive sorting algorithm based on the divide-and-conquer paradigm: split the input in half, sort each half independently, then combine the two sorted halves. Unlike insertion sort's sequential structure, merge sort performs two recursive sub-calls, which leads to a fundamentally different recurrence.

Pseudocode

MergeSort(A, n):
  if n <= 1:
    return A
  B = MergeSort(A[1 .. floor(n/2)],  floor(n/2))
  C = MergeSort(A[floor(n/2)+1 .. n], ceil(n/2))
  return Merge(B, C)

The Merge subroutine combines two already-sorted lists $B$ and $C$ of sizes $|B|$ and $|C|$ into a single sorted list. It does so in a single linear pass — comparing the front elements of each list and appending the smaller — requiring $\Theta(|B| + |C|)$ operations.

The merge subroutine

Although the internals of Merge are not spelled out here, the key fact is:

$$\text{cost of Merge}(B, C) = \Theta(|B| + |C|). $$

For merge sort, $|B| = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ and $|C| = \lceil n/2 \rceil$, so $|B| + |C| = n$. The merge step costs $\Theta(n)$.

Setting up the recurrence

With the base case costing a fixed constant (3 operations as before) and assuming $n$ is a power of 2 for simplicity (which removes floors and ceilings):

$$T(1) = 3, $$ $$T(n) = 2T\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + Dn, \qquad n > 1, $$

where $D$ is the constant guaranteed by the $\Theta(n)$ bound on the merge step. (The small fixed overhead from the recursive calls and list setup is absorbed into $D$ by adjusting the constant.)

This recurrence is fundamentally different from insertion sort's: the recursive term $2T(n/2)$ involves a sub-problem of size $n/2$, not $n-1$. Strong induction — not weak induction — is required to handle it.

Merge Sort Analysis

Guessing the bound

The recurrence $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Dn$ is one of the most important recurrences in computer science. The intuition: at each level of recursion the total work across all sub-problems is $Dn$; there are $\log_2 n$ levels; so the total is $Dn \log_2 n$. The formal goal is to prove

$$T(n) \in \Theta(n \log_2 n). $$

Upper bound

Theorem. There exist constants $B, a > 0$ and $n_0 \geq 1$ such that $T(n) \leq Bn\log_2 n + a$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

The additive constant $a$ is included to handle the base case cleanly (the "plus $a$" trick described above).

Proof.

Base case ($n = 1$): $T(1) = 3$. The bound gives $B \cdot 1 \cdot \log_2 1 + a = 0 + a = a$. We need $3 \leq a$, so take $a \geq 3$.

Inductive hypothesis: Assume $T(k) \leq Bk\log_2 k + a$ for all $k < n$. In particular, since $n/2 < n$:

$$T\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) \leq B\cdot\frac{n}{2}\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + a. $$

Inductive step ($n > 1$):

$$T(n) = 2T\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + Dn $$ $$\leq 2\left(B\cdot\frac{n}{2}\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + a\right) + Dn $$ $$= Bn\log_2\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + 2a + Dn. $$

Apply the logarithm rule $\log_2(n/2) = \log_2 n - \log_2 2 = \log_2 n - 1$:

$$= Bn(\log_2 n - 1) + 2a + Dn $$ $$= Bn\log_2 n - Bn + 2a + Dn $$ $$= Bn\log_2 n + a + \underbrace{(-Bn + Dn + a)}_{\text{must be} \leq 0}. $$

The leap of faith requires

$$-Bn + Dn + a \leq 0. $$

Promote $a$ to $an$ (valid since $n \geq 1$ makes $an \geq a$), giving $n(-B + D + a) \leq 0$, so $B \geq D + a$.

Choosing constants: Set $a = 3$ (from the base case condition). Then $B \geq D + 3$; take $B = D + 3$. Set $n_0 = 1$.

Conclusion: $T(n) \leq Bn\log_2 n + a$ for all $n \geq 1$, so $T(n) \in O(n\log n)$. $\blacksquare$

Lower bound

Theorem. There exist constants $a > 0$ and $n_0 \geq 1$ such that $T(n) \geq an\log_2 n$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Note: the additive constant is not needed here because the lower-bound base case is easy.

Proof.

Base case ($n = 1$): $T(1) = 3 \geq a \cdot 1 \cdot \log_2 1 = 0$. True for any $a > 0$. No condition on $a$ required.

Inductive hypothesis: Assume $T(n/2) \geq a \cdot (n/2) \cdot \log_2(n/2)$.

Inductive step ($n > 1$):

$$T(n) = 2T\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + Dn \geq 2 \cdot a\cdot\frac{n}{2}\cdot\log_2\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + Dn $$ $$= an(\log_2 n - 1) + Dn $$ $$= an\log_2 n + n(D - a). $$

We want this to be $\geq an\log_2 n$, so we need $n(D - a) \geq 0$, which holds when $a \leq D$.

Choosing constants: Take $a = D$ (or any $a \leq D$). Set $n_0 = 1$.

Conclusion: $T(n) \geq Dn\log_2 n$ for all $n \geq 1$, so $T(n) \in \Omega(n\log n)$. $\blacksquare$

Result

$$T(n) \in \Theta(n\log n). $$

This is the tight asymptotic bound for merge sort. The proof demonstrates exactly how the divide-and-conquer structure — two sub-calls of size $n/2$ combined with $\Theta(n)$ merge work — collapses to $\Theta(n\log n)$ via strong induction.

Comparing Sorting Algorithms

The landscape

Four sorting algorithms now appear in this course. Their worst-case complexities are:

Algorithm Recurrence Complexity
Bubble sort $T(n) = T(n-1) + cn$ $\Theta(n^2)$
Selection sort $T(n) = T(n-1) + cn$ $\Theta(n^2)$
Insertion sort (iterative or recursive) $T(n) = T(n-1) + cn$ $\Theta(n^2)$
Merge sort $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + cn$ $\Theta(n\log n)$

The $\Theta(n^2)$ algorithms share a common recurrence structure: one sub-problem of size $n-1$, plus linear work. The essential difference in merge sort is the two sub-calls of size $n/2$ in place of one sub-call of size $n-1$.

The practical gap

The ratio of $n^2$ to $n\log n$ grows without bound:

$$\frac{n^2}{n\log_2 n} = \frac{n}{\log_2 n} \to \infty \quad \text{as } n \to \infty. $$

At $n = 1{,}000$: $n^2 = 10^6$, while $n\log_2 n \approx 10{,}000$. Merge sort is roughly 100 times faster at this size. At $n = 10^6$: $n^2 = 10^{12}$, while $n\log_2 n \approx 2 \times 10^7$ — a factor of roughly 50,000. For large inputs, the difference between $\Theta(n^2)$ and $\Theta(n\log n)$ is not academic; it is the difference between a computation that finishes in seconds and one that would take years.

Why merge sort is not always preferred

Despite its asymptotic advantage, merge sort has practical costs that the $\Theta$-notation hides:

  • Space: merge sort requires auxiliary space proportional to $n$ for the merge step. Bubble and insertion sort can be implemented in place.
  • Constant factors: for small $n$ (say $n \leq 20$), the constant overhead of merge sort's bookkeeping can make insertion sort faster in practice. Many production sort implementations use merge sort for large sub-arrays and switch to insertion sort once the sub-array is small.
  • Cache behavior: insertion sort accesses memory sequentially, which is cache-friendly. Merge sort's access pattern is less predictable.

These considerations are why asymptotic analysis always comes with the caveat that constants matter at small scales — and why knowing both the asymptotic class and the constant factors is valuable.

Connections

Week 4 Notes — Simple induction. The weak induction introduced in Week 4 (assume $P(n)$, prove $P(n+1)$) is the foundation for everything in this week. Strong induction is the natural extension: the hypothesis is wider, covering all prior cases rather than just the immediately preceding one. Every structural move — base case, inductive hypothesis, inductive step, identifying conditions on constants — is identical; only the strength of the assumption changes.

This week's recursive insertion sort confirms the same bound via a different analysis path (recurrence + strong induction rather than direct loop counting), and merge sort finally breaks out of that $\Theta(n^2)$ ceiling.

Week 9 then pairs that technique with strong induction, shifting the goal from finding the exact expression to proving a bound — a technique that is both more robust and more widely applicable when exact forms are hard to find.
