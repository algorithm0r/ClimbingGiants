What Is a Proof?

A proof is a valid and sound argument that establishes the truth of a proposition.

Unpack that carefully:

  • A proposition is a statement that can be true or false.
  • An argument is a sequence of statements (premises) leading to a conclusion.
  • An argument is valid if the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises — that is, if the premises are all true, the conclusion must be true. Validity is a structural property; it says nothing about whether the premises are actually true.
  • An argument is sound if it is valid and all of its premises are in fact true.

A proof requires both. Validity without soundness gives you a correct chain of reasoning built on false foundations. A proof must start from true things and reason correctly to establish something new.

In mathematics and computer science, proofs are the mechanism by which we move from what we assume to what we know. Without proof, a claim — however plausible — remains a conjecture.

Axioms, Theorems, and Definitions

Mathematical reasoning is built on a foundation of named, categorized statements:

Term Meaning
Axiom (or postulate) A statement accepted as true without proof; it is a starting assumption.
Theorem A statement that has been proved true.
Lemma A helper theorem — proved true primarily to assist in proving a larger theorem.
Corollary A theorem that follows easily and directly from a theorem already proved.
Conjecture A statement believed to be true but not yet proved.

A definition assigns a precise meaning to a term. In a proof, definitions are crucial: to show that a number is odd, you must use the definition of odd. You cannot rely on intuition or examples alone.

Formal vs. informal proofs. A formal proof spells out every inference step using named rules of inference (as we did in propositional logic). An informal proof uses ordinary mathematical language, citing steps of algebra or well-known facts without justifying each one individually. Most proofs you will encounter and write are informal — clear, precise prose with enough detail that a careful reader could fill in any gaps.

A Menagerie of Proof Types

Before looking at specific techniques in depth, it helps to name the major varieties:

Direct proof. Start from the premises and derive the conclusion step by step. Most proofs are direct.

Vacuous proof. When the hypothesis of a conditional is false, the conditional $p \rightarrow q$ is vacuously true regardless of $q$. There is nothing to prove; the statement holds trivially.

Exhaustive proof. Enumerate every possible case and verify the conclusion holds in each one. Practical only when the number of cases is finite and manageable (or can be reduced to a finite number via careful partitioning).

Proof by contrapositive. Prove $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$ instead of $p \rightarrow q$, using the logical equivalence $$(p \rightarrow q) \equiv (\neg q \rightarrow \neg p).$$

Proof by contradiction. Assume $\neg p$ (the negation of what you want to prove), then derive a contradiction — a statement of the form $r \wedge \neg r$. Since contradictions cannot be true, the assumption $\neg p$ must be false, so $p$ must be true.

Proof by induction. Prove a base case, then prove that if the statement holds for some $k$ it also holds for $k+1$. This technique gets its own extended treatment in later weeks.

Diagonalization. A powerful technique for comparing infinite sets — used, for instance, to show that the real numbers are strictly more numerous than the integers.

Direct Proof

In a direct proof of $p \rightarrow q$:

  1. Assume $p$ (the hypothesis).
  2. Derive $q$ (the conclusion) through a sequence of valid inference steps.

Each step must follow from the previous ones by a rule of inference, an algebraic identity, or a previously established theorem.

Worked example. Prove: If $n$ is odd, then $3n + 2$ is odd.

Proof. Assume $n$ is odd. By definition of odd, there exists a natural number $k$ such that

$$n = 2k + 1. $$

Substituting into $3n + 2$:

$$3n + 2 = 3(2k + 1) + 2 = 6k + 3 + 2 = 6k + 5. $$

Rewrite $6k + 5 = 6k + 4 + 1 = 2(3k + 2) + 1$.

Since $k$ is a natural number, $3k + 2$ is also a natural number (natural numbers are closed under multiplication and addition). Therefore $3n + 2 = 2(3k + 2) + 1$ is of the form $2m + 1$ with $m = 3k + 2 \in \mathbb{N}$, so $3n + 2$ is odd. $\square$

Proof by Contrapositive

The contrapositive of $p \rightarrow q$ is $\neg q \rightarrow \neg p$. These two conditionals are logically equivalent:

$$(p \rightarrow q) \equiv (\neg q \rightarrow \neg p). $$

When to use it. Choose contrapositive when the negation of the conclusion ($\neg q$) gives you something more concrete to work with than the hypothesis ($p$) itself. Going "backwards" from $p$ to $q$ is sometimes much harder than going forwards from $\neg q$ to $\neg p$.

Structure. Assume $\neg q$. Derive $\neg p$.

Worked example. Prove: If $3n + 2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd.

The contrapositive is: If $n$ is even, then $3n + 2$ is even.

Proof. Assume $n$ is even. By definition, $n = 2k$ for some $k \in \mathbb{N}$. Then:

$$3n + 2 = 3(2k) + 2 = 6k + 2 = 2(3k + 1). $$

Since $3k + 1 \in \mathbb{N}$, we have $3n + 2 = 2(3k + 1)$, which is even.

We have shown: $n$ even $\Rightarrow$ $3n + 2$ even. By contrapositive, $3n + 2$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $n$ odd. $\square$

Proof by Contradiction

In a proof by contradiction of proposition $p$:

  1. Assume $\neg p$.
  2. Derive a contradiction — a statement $r \wedge \neg r$ that is always false.
  3. Conclude that $\neg p$ must be false, so $p$ is true.

The justification rests on a bedrock principle of classical logic: a contradiction cannot hold. If our assumption $\neg p$ leads inevitably to $r \wedge \neg r$, then $\neg p$ cannot be true.

Relationship to contrapositive. Proof by contrapositive is a special case of proof by contradiction. When proving $p \rightarrow q$ by contrapositive, we assume $\neg q$, derive $\neg p$, and note that $p \wedge \neg p$ would be the contradiction if we had also kept $p$ in scope. The two techniques are closely related; in practice, "proof by contradiction" often names the more general strategy where we manufacture an explicit absurdity, while "proof by contrapositive" names the specific flip of a conditional's direction.

Biconditional Proofs

To prove $p \leftrightarrow q$, use the logical equivalence:

$$(p \leftrightarrow q) \equiv (p \rightarrow q) \wedge (q \rightarrow p). $$

A biconditional proof always requires proving both directions separately:

  • Forward direction ($p \rightarrow q$): Assume $p$, derive $q$.
  • Reverse direction ($q \rightarrow q$): Assume $q$, derive $p$.

Either direction can itself be proved directly, by contrapositive, or by contradiction, depending on which is easier.

This structure parallels how subset equality is proved in set theory ($A = B$ iff $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$) and how equality is established by double inequality in real analysis ($a = b$ iff $a \leq b$ and $b \leq a$). Whenever you want to establish a two-way relationship, the standard strategy is to divide and conquer: split into two one-way obligations and handle each separately.

Worked example. Prove: $n$ is odd if and only if $3n + 2$ is odd.

This requires both directions. The forward direction ($n$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $3n + 2$ odd) was proved directly above. The reverse direction ($3n + 2$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $n$ odd) was proved by contrapositive above. Together, both proofs establish the biconditional. $\square$

NAND is Universal

Background: Universal Sets of Operators

The standard propositional connectives $\{\neg, \wedge, \vee, \rightarrow, \leftrightarrow, \oplus\}$ are not all independent. A set of operators is called universal (or functionally complete) if every possible boolean function — equivalently, every possible truth table — can be expressed using only operators from that set.

Theorem. The set $\{\neg, \wedge, \vee\}$ is universal.

Proof sketch. Any boolean function $O(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ can be represented by its truth table. For each row of the truth table where $O$ evaluates to true, write down the conjunction of its inputs: for each $x_i$, write $x_i$ if that row has $x_i = \text{true}$, and $\neg x_i$ if $x_i = \text{false}$. Then take the disjunction of all such conjunctions (one per true-row). The result is a formula using only $\neg$, $\wedge$, and $\vee$ that is logically equivalent to $O$.

The resulting form is called disjunctive normal form (DNF): a disjunction of conjunctions, where each conjunction may contain negated or unnegated variables. Every boolean formula can be written in DNF. (Its dual — conjunctive normal form (CNF) — is a conjunction of disjunctions, obtainable by applying De Morgan's laws.) $\square$

The NAND Operator

The NAND operator, written $p \uparrow q$, is defined as:

$$p \uparrow q \equiv \neg(p \wedge q). $$

Its truth table:

$p$ $q$ $p \uparrow q$
T T F
T F T
F T T
F F T

Theorem. $\{\uparrow\}$ is universal.

Proof. Since $\{\neg, \wedge, \vee\}$ is universal, it suffices to show that each of $\neg$, $\wedge$, and $\vee$ can be expressed using $\uparrow$ alone.

NOT from NAND:

$$\neg p \equiv p \uparrow p. $$

Proof. When $p = \text{T}$: $p \uparrow p = \neg(\text{T} \wedge \text{T}) = \neg\text{T} = \text{F}$. When $p = \text{F}$: $p \uparrow p = \neg(\text{F} \wedge \text{F}) = \neg\text{F} = \text{T}$. This matches the truth table for $\neg p$. $\square$

OR from NAND:

$$p \vee q \equiv (p \uparrow p) \uparrow (q \uparrow q). $$

Proof by boolean algebra:

$$p \vee q \equiv \neg\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg(\neg p \wedge \neg q) \equiv \neg p \uparrow \neg q \equiv (p \uparrow p) \uparrow (q \uparrow q). \qquad\square $$

The steps used: double negation, then De Morgan's law ($\neg(A \wedge B) \equiv \neg A \vee \neg B$ applied in reverse gives $\neg(\neg p \wedge \neg q) \equiv \neg p \uparrow \neg q$), then substituting $\neg p = p \uparrow p$ and $\neg q = q \uparrow q$.

AND from NAND:

$$p \wedge q \equiv (p \uparrow q) \uparrow (p \uparrow q). $$

Proof by boolean algebra:

$$p \wedge q \equiv \neg\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg(p \uparrow q) \equiv (p \uparrow q) \uparrow (p \uparrow q). \qquad\square $$

The steps: double negation; recognize $\neg(p \wedge q) = p \uparrow q$ by definition; apply $\neg X = X \uparrow X$.

Since $\neg$, $\wedge$, and $\vee$ are all expressible using $\uparrow$, and $\{\neg, \wedge, \vee\}$ is universal, it follows that $\{\uparrow\}$ is universal. $\square$

Engineering significance. In digital circuit design, every logic gate can be implemented from NAND gates alone. This means a chip manufacturer needs to produce only one type of gate. NOR ($\neg(p \vee q)$) is likewise universal, and similar proofs apply.

Counterexamples and Practical Reasoning

Disproving Universal Statements

A universal statement has the form "for all $x$, $P(x)$." To prove such a statement requires showing $P(x)$ holds for every $x$ in the domain — potentially infinitely many. To disprove it requires only a single counterexample: one specific $x_0$ such that $P(x_0)$ is false.

This asymmetry is fundamental: proofs of universal statements are hard (you must cover all cases); disproofs are potentially easy (one bad case suffices).

Equivalence Partitioning and Exhaustive Testing

A practical application of these ideas appears in software testing and code verification. When verifying that an implementation matches a specification, the goal is to find the minimal set of test inputs that covers all logically distinct cases.

Key insight. Most variables with large or infinite domains can be partitioned into a small number of equivalence classes — groups of inputs that the program treats identically. You only need to test one representative from each class.

Example. For a canGetDiscount function with the following specification:

  • Student must have valid ID and be currently enrolled.
  • Show must be a matinée (before 4:00 p.m.) or on a Tuesday.
  • If the film is rated R, the customer must be at least 17 or accompanied by an adult.

The variable showHour ranges over 0–23, but the logic only cares whether it is less than 16 (matinée). So the domain splits into two equivalence classes: $\{\text{showHour} < 16\}$ and $\{\text{showHour} \geq 16\}$. Similarly, customerAge splits on 17. Once every continuous variable is reduced to a boolean partition, the full input space becomes a finite truth table.

Finding bugs. Consider the showtime logic as originally coded:

timeValid = (showHour < 16 AND NOT isTuesday) OR (showHour >= 16 AND isTuesday)

Testing the four cases (matinée/not × Tuesday/not-Tuesday) reveals a bug: a Tuesday matinée (showHour = 14, isTuesday = true) evaluates to false, but the spec says it should be true. The corrected logic is:

timeValid = (showHour < 16) OR isTuesday

Similarly, the original age-check used AND where the spec requires OR:

// Buggy:
ageValid = (customerAge >= 17) AND isWithAdult
// Correct:
ageValid = (customerAge >= 17) OR isWithAdult

The counterexample that exposes the bug: age = 25, no adult. The customer is old enough but the buggy condition rejects them.

Proof by exhaustive case analysis. After fixing the code, proving it correct means verifying it on every equivalence class. For this function there are three logically independent groups of inputs (student credentials: 4 cases; showtime: 4 cases; age/rating: 8 cases), giving 16 test cases total. If the implementation passes all 16, it is provably correct for all possible inputs — because every input belongs to exactly one case that has been tested.

Introduction to Abstract Algebra

Abstract algebra studies mathematical structures defined by a set together with one or more binary operations that satisfy specified properties. The goal is to reason about whole classes of structures at once rather than one number system at a time.

Closure

A set $S$ is closed under a binary operation $\star$ if for every $a, b \in S$, the result $a \star b$ is also in $S$.

Examples.

  • The set of even integers $E = \{\ldots, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, \ldots\}$ is closed under multiplication: an even times an even is even. It is also closed under addition.
  • The set of odd integers is not closed under multiplication when restricted to natural numbers in the usual sense, since odd $\times$ odd = odd (closed), but the key point is that closure depends on both the set and the operation.

Closure is the minimal property needed for a binary operation to make sense within a set — without it, applying the operation might take you outside the set entirely.

Semigroups and Monoids

A semigroup is a set $S$ with a binary operation $\star$ that is:

  1. Closed: $\forall a, b \in S,\ a \star b \in S$.
  2. Associative: $\forall a, b, c \in S,\ (a \star b) \star c = a \star (b \star c)$.

A monoid is a semigroup that also has:

  1. Identity element: $\exists e \in S$ such that $\forall a \in S,\ e \star a = a \star e = a$.

The integers under addition form a monoid with identity $0$. The natural numbers under multiplication form a monoid with identity $1$.

Groups

A group is a monoid that additionally satisfies:

  1. Inverses: $\forall a \in S,\ \exists a^{-1} \in S$ such that $a \star a^{-1} = a^{-1} \star a = e$.

The integers under addition form a group: the inverse of $n$ is $-n$. The natural numbers under addition do not form a group (there are no additive inverses within $\mathbb{N}$).

Using Definitions in Proofs

Abstract algebra illustrates an important proof technique: proof from definition. To show a set is closed under an operation, you take two arbitrary elements, apply the operation, and verify the result is in the set — exactly as in the odd/even proofs above. To show something is a group, you verify each of the four properties in turn.

Example. Prove that the set of even integers is closed under addition.

Proof. Let $a, b$ be even integers. By definition, $a = 2j$ and $b = 2k$ for some $j, k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $a + b = 2j + 2k = 2(j + k)$. Since $j + k \in \mathbb{Z}$, we have $a + b = 2(j+k)$, which is even. $\square$

This is a direct proof that follows the same structure as the odd-number examples: invoke the definition, do algebra, conclude by applying the definition again.

Connections

The proof techniques introduced this week are the primary tools used throughout the course and throughout mathematics.

Looking back. Everything here depends on Week 1 Notes: propositional logic supplies the connectives ($\wedge$, $\vee$, $\neg$, $\rightarrow$, $\leftrightarrow$), De Morgan's laws, logical equivalence, and the structure of valid argument. Without that foundation, the contrapositive would have no meaning and the NAND universality proof would have no tools.

Looking ahead. Week 3 Notes introduce loop invariants — a technique for proving that iterative algorithms are correct. A loop invariant proof has the same skeleton as a direct proof: state a proposition, show it holds at initialization, show it is preserved at each step, conclude it holds at termination. The same discipline of "assume the hypothesis, derive the conclusion" applies.

Week 9 Notes develop strong induction. Strong induction extends direct proof to infinite families of statements: the base case is a direct proof, and the inductive step is itself a conditional proof (if the statement holds for all values up to $k$, prove it for $k+1$). The biconditional structure of the odd-number proof — two separate conditional proofs combined — foreshadows how inductive arguments are organized.
