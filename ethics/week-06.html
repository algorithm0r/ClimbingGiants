Week 6 · Misinformation & Epistemic Harm

This week examines misinformation and what researchers call epistemic harm, the erosion of our collective ability to know what is true. Deepfakes, confident false outputs from language models, and the psychology of how people update beliefs are all part of the picture. The readings look at where AI-generated misinformation has appeared in elections and public discourse, and what responses have been tried.

This Week's Writing Goal

Supporting Claims with Sources

This week you will learn to find and evaluate sources and to attach evidence to every claim, which means telling credible evidence apart from mere assertion. You will also learn why some sources are stronger than others and how to build an argument that a reader can check. The evidence you add this week is what your paper's claims will depend on.

Content

For your required weekly reflection, engage with one source from Primary Content. Any source (primary or additional) can be used for an extra reflection for extra credit.

Primary Content

Social Media Accounts Use AI-Generated Audio to Push Election Misinformation — NPR · 2023 · ~10 min listen · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

NPR reports on deepfakes and synthetic media being deployed at scale in elections. It provides concrete examples of what AI-generated misinformation actually looks like in the wild. Note: this is audio with a transcript available, so you can listen or read, depending on your preference.

Reflection prompts:

  • The NPR report describes a right-wing influencer who created a fake Biden video announcing a military draft, and labeled it AI-generated, framing it as "a preview of something that hadn't happened yet." Does the label change your reaction? Is disclosing a deepfake while still spreading it a meaningful ethical distinction, or does it not matter once the clip is out there?
  • A Wharton professor demonstrated creating a convincing deepfake of himself in eight minutes for $11. When you heard that, what was your immediate gut reaction? Does the accessibility of the tool change who you think is responsible when it gets misused?
  • The NPR piece focuses especially on the risk to down-ballot candidates, local races with no resources to counter synthetic content. Does that shift the stakes for you? What does it mean to build tools that can be cheaply weaponized against people who cannot defend themselves?

The Apocalypse That Wasn't: AI Was Everywhere in 2024's Elections, But Often in Unexpected Ways — Harvard Ash Center · 2024 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Think tank]

A more measured analysis of where AI actually showed up in the 2024 elections, and where the fears did not materialize. It helps separate real harms from hypothetical ones and is useful for judging how large the risk actually was.

Reflection prompts:

  • The Harvard Ash Center piece reports that "nearly eight times as many respondents expected AI to be used for mostly bad purposes" compared to good, but the catastrophic misuse mostly did not materialize. Does that reassure you, or does it make you more anxious about the next election cycle? What does it mean to be right about risk when the harm has not happened yet?
  • The report describes AI being used for legitimate campaign work (language translation, voter chatbots, ad generation) alongside documented deepfakes and robocalls. Where does the line fall for you between AI as a helpful campaign tool and AI as manipulation? Is that line clear, or does it depend on who is using it?
  • The authors suggest competitive advantage will drive AI adoption in politics regardless of whether it works. If everyone believes AI gives them an edge and deploys it defensively, what kind of environment does that create? Is there an analogy to arms races that lands for you?

Why Facts Don't Change Our Minds — Elizabeth Kolbert, The New Yorker · 2017 · ~12 min read · 🟡 Limited access · [Media]

Kolbert explores the psychology of belief, in particular why people often keep a belief after seeing evidence against it. As a result, false information may persist partly because people tend to resist correcting their beliefs.

Reflection prompts:

  • Kolbert argues that part of the problem of misinformation is that we are built to resist correcting false beliefs, even when shown evidence. Has there been a moment in your own life when you held onto a belief longer than the evidence warranted? What finally shifted you, if anything?
  • The piece treats motivated reasoning as a normal part of human cognition. On this view we may have evolved to defend our beliefs in front of others as much as to track the truth. If that is right, what does it mean for the assumption that better information will solve the misinformation problem?
  • If correction does not reliably work, what obligation does a builder have when creating tools that can spread false information at scale? Does understanding the psychology of belief change your answer?

Additional Content

➕ After Truth: Disinformation and the Cost of Fake News — HBO · 2020 · ~90 min watch · 🔴 Paywalled · [Media]

A documentary about disinformation and its real-world costs. It takes the broader view beyond AI to show how false information cascades through society.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • The film traces the human cost of specific disinformation campaigns, with real people and real consequences. If a disinformation campaign you built or worked on caused harm to a real person, what would your responsibility look like? Would the fact that you did not intend harm change anything?
  • The documentary focuses on the cascade (how false information spreads and amplifies beyond its origin point). At what point in that chain does ethical responsibility transfer from the creator to the platform to the sharer?

➕ "Deepfakes and the Crisis of Knowing" — UNESCO · 2025 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Org]

A substantive essay on deepfakes as a threat to epistemic authority itself (our collective ability to know what is true), a harm that goes beyond individual reputations. It introduces the "liar's dividend", that is, the ability to dismiss authentic recordings as probable fakes. It proposes structural responses centered on "epistemic agency", with content removal as only one part.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • The essay introduces the "liar's dividend," the idea that deepfakes also give bad actors a way to dismiss real evidence as fake. Has that idea shifted how you think about the harm? Is the ability to sow doubt more dangerous than the fake content itself?
  • The author argues that epistemic agency (the ability to form your own reliable beliefs) is a right that institutions, as well as individuals, should protect. Do you think of your ability to know what is true as something society has an obligation to protect? Where does that obligation fall?
  • The essay proposes that solutions need to include rebuilding trust in institutions and processes as well as detecting fakes. Does that feel more or less achievable than a technical detection solution? What would it take for you to trust a source again after a major deepfake incident?

➕ "AI-Driven Disinformation: Policy Recommendations for Democratic Resilience" — Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence · 2025 · ~30 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Peer-reviewed]

Recent peer-reviewed research on AI-driven misinformation and policy solutions. It shows what researchers think the structural answer should be.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • The researchers argue that platform accountability, regulatory harmonization across countries, and digital literacy education are the three structural pillars of a response to AI disinformation. Which of those feels most achievable to you, and which feels like a long shot? What does your answer tell you about where you think power actually sits?
  • The paper is co-authored by researchers at a Ukrainian think tank, written during an ongoing war in which AI disinformation has been weaponized by state actors. Does knowing the context of the authors change how you read their urgency? Should it?
  • If a policy recommendation would slow down AI development to reduce disinformation risk, what is your honest reaction to that tradeoff?

Reflection

Choose one source from this week's Primary Content. Ground your reflection in something specific from that source, such as a claim you agreed or disagreed with, or a moment that surprised you. Then apply the three-step structure:

Gut check: What was your honest, immediate reaction? Trace it: Where does that reaction come from in your values or experience? Challenge it: What would someone who sees it differently say? Does their case have merit?

~150 words. The source-specific prompts above are starting points. You can use one of those or follow your own reaction, but your reflection must be anchored to something specific from the content you chose.

See Reflection Instructions for the full guide.

Meeting

Use these prompts as starting points. The goal is to build a shared understanding of how we know what is true and what we are responsible for in a world of synthetic media.

This week's discussion directions:

  • Is a platform responsible for AI-generated misinformation it hosts? What about misinformation created with tools it provided?
  • What is the difference between a tool that can be misused and a tool designed to deceive? How do you tell the difference?
  • How do you think about your own epistemic responsibility as a consumer and creator of AI content?

Essay share: Share the sources you integrated this week and the claims they support. Which source was hardest to find or evaluate? What questions came up about credibility? The group responds with reactions and suggestions.
