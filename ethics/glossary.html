Glossary

This page defines the terms used across the course, including every term on the weekly quiz vocabulary lists. Each entry names the week or page that introduces the term. Elsewhere on the site these terms have a dotted underline, and hovering over one shows its definition (a click brings you here). Skills from the Writing Toolkit have a solid green underline, and a click on one opens its section of the toolkit.

A

abstract
A single paragraph (about 150 words in this course) that compresses a whole paper: the problem, the position it takes, and what it proposes. Week 10
accountability
Being answerable for a decision or outcome. That means having to explain it, and facing consequences if it caused harm. Week 8
active voice
A sentence form in which the subject performs the action, as in "the team shipped the model". Week 9
AI companion
A chatbot built to act as a friend, partner, or confidant over a long period of conversations. Week 7
AI hallucination
False or made-up content that an AI system produces in the same fluent, confident way as accurate content, such as an invented court case or citation. Week 9
algorithm
A set of step-by-step instructions a computer follows to turn inputs into an output, such as a score, a ranking, or a decision. Week 1
algorithmic audit
A review, often by an outside party, that tests an algorithm's outputs (and where possible the data and code behind them) for errors, bias, and harm. Week 8
algorithmic bias
Bias in the outputs of an algorithm. It usually comes from the training data, from proxy variables, or from choices about what the system is built to optimize. Week 3
antithesis
In a dialectic, a second position that answers or opposes the first one (the thesis). Meeting Instructions
argument
A claim together with the reasons and evidence offered to support it. Week 2
assertion
A claim stated without support. It may be true, though you have only the writer's word for it. Week 6
auditability
How easily outside reviewers can check what a system does. It depends on their access to the system's data, code, and decisions. Week 8
automation
Using machines or software to do work that people used to do. Week 5
autonomy
A person's ability to make their own choices, based on their own reasoning and values. Week 7

B

bias
A consistent tilt in a person's or a system's judgments that favors some people or outcomes over others. In AI it usually means outputs that are less accurate or less favorable for a particular group. Week 1
bibliography
The list of full citations for every source a paper uses, placed at the end of the paper. Toolkit
black box
A system whose inputs and outputs can be seen while the process connecting them is hidden, either kept secret by the owner or too complex to trace. Week 8
brainstorming
Writing down as many ideas as you can in a set time, without judging them, so you have raw material to choose from later. It is the Week 1 writing goal. Week 1

C

challenge it
The third step of a reflection. You state the strongest case against your position and test your position against it. Reflection Instructions
chatbot
A program that holds a conversation in text or speech. Most current chatbots are built on large language models. Week 7
civil disobedience
Breaking a law openly and on purpose to protest a law or policy you believe is unjust, usually without violence and while accepting the legal consequences. Week 4
clarity
The quality of writing that can be understood on the first reading. Week 9
class action
A lawsuit in which one or a few people sue on behalf of a whole group with the same claim, such as all the authors whose books were used to train a model. Week 4
code of ethics
A profession's written statement of the principles its members agree to follow, such as the ACM Code of Ethics for computing. Week 10
coded gaze
Joy Buolamwini's name for the assumptions and blind spots of a system's builders that become built into the system. Week 3
coercion
Getting someone to do something by threat or force, so that they have no acceptable alternative. Week 7
collaborative paragraph
The paragraph of 150 to 200 words your group writes together at the end of each meeting, recording what you explored and where you agreed or disagreed. Meeting Instructions
consent
Permission a person gives for something to be done to them or with their information. Week 2
consequence
A result that follows from an action or decision, including results nobody intended. Week 1
consequentialism
A family of ethical theories that judge actions by their results. The toolkit question "Who is harmed?" draws on it. Toolkit
copyright
A legal right that gives the creator of an original work (writing, art, music, code) control over copying, distributing, adapting, and publicly performing or displaying it for a limited time. It covers a work's specific expression, and copyright law leaves ideas and general styles open to others. Week 4
copyright infringement
Copying, distributing, or adapting a copyrighted work without the holder's permission and outside a legal exception such as fair use. Week 4

D

dark pattern
A design trick in an app or website that steers users into something they did not mean to do, such as signing up, sharing data, or staying longer. Week 7
data lifecycle
The full path a piece of data takes, from collection through storage, use (including model training), and sharing, to deletion. Week 2
data ownership
The question of who has rights over a piece of data: the person it describes, the organization that collected it, or the company that stores it or trains a model on it. Week 2
deepfake
Synthetic media that shows a real person saying or doing something they never said or did. Week 2
demographic parity
One definition of fairness. A system meets it when each group receives the positive outcome (a loan, an interview) at the same rate. Week 3
deontology
A family of ethical theories that judge actions by duties and rules, so that some actions are right or wrong whatever their results. The toolkit question "What are our obligations?" draws on it. Toolkit
derivative work
A new work based on an existing one, such as a translation, a sequel, or an adaptation. Making one usually requires the copyright holder's permission. Week 4
dialectic
A discussion in which people start from different positions and work together toward a fuller understanding of the question. Meeting Instructions
diffuse accountability
Accountability spread so thinly across people and organizations that each one can point to another. Week 9
disinformation
False information created or spread on purpose to deceive. Week 6
disparate impact
A pattern where a rule or system applied to everyone produces noticeably worse outcomes for a group defined by a protected characteristic such as race or sex. In US employment and housing law it can count as discrimination without any intent, unless the organization shows the rule serves a legitimate need. Week 3
DMCA
The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (1998), a US copyright law. Among other things it makes removing copyright information from a work illegal in some cases, and it sets the notice-and-takedown process for websites. Week 4
duty of care
A legal obligation to take reasonable care to avoid harming others who could be affected by what you do. Week 9

E

emotional AI
AI systems built to infer people's emotions from facial expressions, voice, or text, and to respond to those inferences. Week 7
epistemic agency
Your ability to form your own reliable beliefs by gathering and weighing evidence yourself. Week 6
epistemic harm
Harm to people's ability to know what is true. For instance, a flood of fakes can make it harder to trust any evidence at all. Week 6
essay share
The round in each meeting where everyone briefly says what they did on their essay that week and one thing they are wrestling with. Meeting Instructions
ethical reasoning
Working through a question of right and wrong step by step, with reasons that someone else can follow, check, and challenge. Week 1
EU AI Act
The European Union's law regulating AI systems (adopted in 2024). The stricter rules apply to uses the law classes as high risk, such as hiring and credit scoring. Week 8
evidence
Something concrete a reader can examine and check, such as data, a quotation, a documented case, or the findings of a study. Week 6
explainability
How well people can understand why a system produced a particular output. Week 8
extra labor
Optional work that raises your grade above the base B: +0.1 for each extra reflection and +0.3 for a larger project, up to a 4.0. Extra Labor

F

facial recognition
Software that identifies or verifies a person by comparing an image of their face against stored images. Week 3
facilitator
The group member who hosts a week's meeting by opening it, watching the time, and inviting quieter people in. The role rotates each week. Meeting Instructions
fair use
A US legal exception that allows some uses of copyrighted work without permission. Courts weigh the purpose of the use, the kind of work, how much was used, and the effect on the market for the original. Week 4
fairness
Treating people justly, so that nobody is disadvantaged for reasons that should not matter. Computer scientists have written several precise definitions of fairness, and some of them conflict, so a system usually cannot meet all of them at once. Week 3
feedback loop
A cycle in which a system's outputs feed back into the system as new inputs. A biased decision can produce data that makes the next decision more biased (bias amplification). Week 3
filler language
Words and phrases that add length without adding meaning, such as "it is important to note that" or "in today's society". Week 9

G

GDPR
The General Data Protection Regulation, the European Union's main data protection law (applied since 2018). It covers organizations in the EU, and organizations elsewhere that offer goods or services to people in the EU or track their behavior. Week 2
generative AI
AI systems that produce new content (text, images, audio, video, or code) in response to a prompt. ChatGPT, Claude, and Stable Diffusion are examples. Week 1
Gibbs reflective cycle
A six-step structure for reflective writing: description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan. Extra Labor
gut check
The first step of a reflection. You write your honest, immediate reaction to something specific in a source. Reflection Instructions

H

harm
A setback to someone's interests, such as lost money, a lost opportunity, an injury, distress, or damage to their reputation. Harm can be direct or indirect, and intended or accidental. Week 1
hedge
A word such as "may", "likely", or "often" that tells your reader how strongly your evidence supports a claim. In this course a hedge should match the strength of that evidence. Week 9

I

informed consent
Consent given after the person has been told, in terms they can understand, what will happen, what the risks are, and what other options they have. The idea comes from medical research ethics. Week 7
integrity
Acting consistently with your values, and being willing to explain and stand behind your decisions to the people they affect. Week 1
intellectual property
Creations of the mind that the law treats as property, such as inventions (patents), brand names (trademarks), and creative works (copyright). Week 4

J

journalism
Reporting on current events, usually by news organizations, where editors (and at some outlets fact-checkers) check stories before quick publication. Journalism is usually not peer-reviewed. Week 6

L

labor displacement
Workers losing their jobs or tasks because machines or software now do that work. Week 5
labor-based grading
A grading system based on how much of the assigned work you complete with a genuine attempt. In this course completing all required work (with up to 8 late) earns a B (3.1), and extra labor raises it. Syllabus
large language model (LLM)
A neural network trained on very large amounts of text to predict the next word, and usually trained further to follow instructions and hold a conversation. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are built on large language models. Week 3
liability
Legal responsibility for a harm, which can require paying damages or facing other penalties. Week 9
liar's dividend
The benefit a liar gets once deepfakes are common. A real recording can be dismissed as probably fake. Week 6
licensing
Permission from a rights holder to use a work under agreed terms, often for payment. Week 4
living essay
The single Google Doc you revise every week, which grows into your final paper by Week 10. Essay Instructions

M

manipulation
Influencing someone's beliefs or choices by going around their reasoning, for instance by exploiting their emotions, habits, or blind spots. Week 7
many-hands problem
The difficulty of holding anyone responsible when many people each made a small contribution to a harmful outcome. Week 9
meaningful consent
Consent given by someone who understands what they are agreeing to, has a real option to say no, and can withdraw it later. Week 2
misinformation
False or misleading information shared without an intent to deceive. Week 6
moral outsourcing
Rumman Chowdhury's name for handing the blame for a decision to a system or to someone else in the chain, as in "the algorithm decided". Week 9
moral responsibility
Being the right person to praise or blame for an outcome, because of what you did, decided, or failed to do. Week 9
motivated reasoning
Weighing evidence in a way that favors the conclusion you already want. Week 6

N

negligence
In law, failing to take the care a reasonable person would take, so that someone is harmed. Week 9
nudging
Arranging how choices are presented (the default option, the order, the wording) so that people tend toward one choice while every option stays open to them. Week 7

O

obligation
Something you owe to others (or to yourself) because of a duty, a promise, a role, or a principle. An obligation can hold even when meeting it costs you something. Week 1
opacity
The quality of a system whose workings the affected people cannot see or follow. Week 8
open access
Making research and other knowledge free for anyone to read, with no paywall or subscription. Week 4

P

passive voice
A sentence form in which the subject receives the action, as in "the model was shipped". The sentence can leave the actor out entirely. Week 9
peer review
The checking of a research paper by other experts in the field before a journal or conference publishes it. In computing, many of the most important papers appear at peer-reviewed conferences. Week 6
personal data
Any information that identifies a person or could be linked back to them, such as a name, a face, a location, a voice, or a browsing history. Week 2
persuasion
Influencing someone's beliefs or choices through communication, such as arguments, evidence, or emotional appeals. Week 7
policy proposal
A specific recommendation that names what should change, who carries it out, who pays, and what tradeoffs it creates. It is Section 3 of the course essay. Essay Instructions
position paper
An essay that takes a position on a problem, argues for it with evidence, and proposes what should be done. The course essay is a 2000-word position paper. Essay Instructions
precision
Saying exactly what you mean, with specific nouns and claims in place of vague ones. Week 9
preprint
A research paper shared publicly before peer review, often on a site such as arXiv. Week 6
primary source
Original evidence, such as the study, dataset, court ruling, interview, or recording itself. Week 6
privacy
A person's ability to keep information about themselves, and parts of their life, from others. This includes control over who collects and uses that information. Week 2
protected class
A group that anti-discrimination law protects, defined by a characteristic such as race, sex, religion, national origin, age, or disability. Week 3
proxy variable
A piece of data that stands in for something a system is not given directly. For instance, a zip code can act as a proxy for race, so a model can learn racial patterns from it. Week 3
pseudo-intimacy
A sense of a close personal relationship with an AI companion, built up by features such as remembering details about the user and expressing affection. Week 7

R

recidivism
Returning to crime after a conviction, usually measured as a new arrest or conviction within a set period (often two years). Risk assessment tools such as COMPAS try to predict it. Week 3
right to be forgotten
A right under the EU's GDPR to have an organization delete personal data about you in certain cases (for instance when the data is no longer needed, or you withdraw your consent), with some exceptions. Week 2
right to explanation
The idea that a person affected by an automated decision can get a meaningful explanation of how it was made. EU law includes a version of it. Week 8
risk assessment algorithm
A tool that scores how likely a person is to do something in the future, such as reoffend or miss a court date. Courts in many US states use these scores in bail and sentencing decisions. Week 3

S

safety-critical system
A system whose failure can injure or kill people, such as a medical device, an aircraft control system, or a self-driving car. Week 9
scale
The number of people or decisions a system reaches. A system working at scale repeats the same choice many times, so the system's errors and benefits both multiply. Week 1
secondary source
A source that reports on, summarizes, or interprets primary sources, such as a news story about a study. Week 6
section coherence
Each section of a paper doing one clear job, in an order where each part sets up the next. Week 8
source credibility
How far a source has earned a reader's trust, judged by who wrote it, how it was checked, who funded it, and how clearly it shows its evidence. Week 6
stakeholder
Any person or group affected by a system or a decision. This includes the people who build it, use it, profit from it, and are judged by it. Week 1
stakeholder analysis
A closer look at the most important groups on a stakeholder map: what each stands to gain or lose, and where their interests conflict. It is the Week 4 writing goal. Week 4
stakeholder map
A broad list of every group affected by a system, with a few words on how each is affected. It is the Week 3 writing goal. Week 3
strawman
A weakened or distorted version of an opposing view, set up so it is easy to knock down. Toolkit
surveillance
Systematic watching or recording of people's behavior by a government, a company, or another person, often to predict or influence what they do. Week 2
surveillance capitalism
Shoshana Zuboff's name for a business model that collects data about people's behavior and sells predictions about that behavior, often to advertisers. Week 2
synthesis
In a dialectic, the fuller view a group reaches by examining two or more starting positions together. It can keep a disagreement while making clearer what is at stake. Meeting Instructions
synthetic media
Images, audio, video, or text generated or altered by AI, often made to look or sound real. Week 2

T

technological unemployment
Unemployment that results when technology replaces workers faster than new jobs appear for them. Week 5
theme statement
The central claim of a Labor Journal Essay, stated in one sentence: what changed or developed for you over the course. Extra Labor
thesis
The central claim a piece of writing argues for, specific enough that a reasonable person could disagree with it. In a dialectic, the thesis is the first position put forward. Week 2
think tank
An organization that researches policy questions and publishes reports and recommendations. Many are funded by groups with a stake in the issues they study, or hold a political viewpoint, so it is a good idea to check who backs one. Week 6
topic sentence
A sentence, usually the first in a paragraph, that states the paragraph's claim. Week 8
trace it
The second step of a reflection. You work out where your reaction comes from in your values, your experience, or the communities you belong to. Reflection Instructions
training data
The collection of examples (text, images, code, and so on) a model learns its patterns from. A model's outputs reflect what is in the training data and what is missing from the training data. Week 2
transformative use
A use of a work for a purpose or character different from the original's, such as parody, commentary, or a search index. The more a use differs in purpose, the more it counts toward fair use. Week 4
transparency
Openness about how systems work, what data they use, and how they are used, so that outsiders can understand and question them. Week 8

U

underrepresented data
Training data with few examples of some groups. A model trained on it tends to be less accurate for those groups. Week 3

V

virtue ethics
A family of ethical theories that focus on character, asking what a good person would do and what kind of person an action makes you. The toolkit question "What would a person of integrity do?" draws on it. Toolkit

W

weapons of math destruction
Cathy O'Neil's name for algorithms that are important (they shape many people's lives), secret (people cannot see how they work), and harmful to the people they score. Week 8
web scraping
Collecting large amounts of content from websites automatically with a program. Much of the training data for generative AI was gathered this way. Week 2
whistleblower
A person inside an organization who reports wrongdoing they have seen there, to people inside the organization who can act on it, or to regulators, lawmakers, or the public. Week 10

X

XAI (explainable AI)
Methods for making a model's outputs understandable to people, for instance by showing which inputs mattered most to a decision. Week 8

Toolkit Skills

Each skill is explained in full, with an example, in the Writing Toolkit.

The three toolkit questions
Who is harmed? What are our obligations? What would a person of integrity do? Each question draws on a different family of ethical theory.
Who is harmed?
This toolkit question asks who is affected by a system or decision and what specific harms they could face.
What are our obligations?
This toolkit question asks what duties, rules, or principles should guide a decision, whatever its consequences.
What would a person of integrity do?
This toolkit question asks what the situation asks of you as a person with values, and whether you could explain your decision to the people it affects.
Free Ideation
Free ideation means writing down everything that comes to mind in a set time, without judging it, to build a large pile of raw ideas.
So What
The so what skill means asking "so what?" of an observation, again and again, until you reach a claim with real stakes.
Steelmanning
Steelmanning means stating the strongest version of the case against your own position before you respond to it.
Role Reversal
Role reversal means imagining a system from one stakeholder's position to see what they need and what the system costs them.
Finding and Evaluating Sources
This skill means telling credible evidence apart from assertion, and choosing the right kind of source (journal, think tank, journalism, blog) for each claim.
Citing Sources
Citing sources means giving every source in one consistent citation style, so a reader can find and check it.
Paragraph as Unit of Argument
This skill means building each paragraph as one unit of argument, with a topic sentence, evidence, analysis, and a transition.
Structural Editing
Structural editing means revising a whole paper at the level of sections and paragraphs by cutting, reordering, combining, and splitting them.
Sentence-Level Editing
Sentence-level editing means revising each sentence for clarity, precision, and efficiency, with hedges that match your evidence.
Final Framing
Final framing means writing the conclusion, introduction, abstract, and title last, once you know what your paper argues.
