Meeting Instructions

Each week you meet with your group to discuss that week's topic. This page explains how meetings work and what happens when your group gets together. Read it once at the start, then refer back if you need to reorient.

What Meetings Are

Meetings are small-group discussions among the same fixed group all quarter. The goal is synthesis and dialectic, that is, building an understanding of the question together.

Debate and dialectic are different activities. In debate each side tries to win, presenting its strongest case and attacking the other side's in order to prove itself right. In dialectic the group starts from different positions and works toward a more complete view of the question. The group may not agree, but by the end each of you should understand the question better than you did at the start.

Your meetings use the same sources and topics as the weekly reflections. Reflections are written alone (your values, your tracing, your challenge), while meetings bring those individual perspectives into conversation. You hear how the prompt lands for someone else, and you notice where your thinking overlaps with theirs and where it branches.

Fixed Groups

Your group is fixed all quarter deliberately, so you will meet with the same 5 people 9 times. This is because sustained familiarity often changes how a group thinks together. By Week 7 your group will likely know your positions and tendencies, and that shared history lets them challenge you in ways a new group could not. This also means you are thinking with people who know your work and your values, so you can think out loud without re-establishing context every week.

The Facilitator Role

Every meeting has one person who serves as facilitator for that week. Rotate this role each meeting so everyone leads at least twice across the quarter. The facilitator hosts the meeting and does not need to supply answers. Their job is to create conditions for good discussion.

What the facilitator does:

  • Opens the meeting with a brief framing (a sentence or two), for instance "This week we're talking about manipulation and autonomy. Let's start with the gut-check prompt."
  • Introduces the prompts and invites the group in
  • Watches the time so there is space for both discussion and essay share
  • Notices when one person is dominating and gently invites others in ("I want to hear from you on this")
  • Notices when discussion stalls and returns to a prompt to restart it
  • Opens the collaborative paragraph at the close ("Let's try to capture what we were actually thinking. What's one idea from tonight that would go in the paragraph?")

Purpose: The purpose of the rotation is to practice facilitation, a skill you will likely use in meetings and on teams after graduation. The meeting is low-stakes and its goal is mutual understanding, so it is a good place to practice.

How to Use the Weekly Prompts

Each week has the same prompts as that week's reflection. You bring your personal thinking (from your reflection) into the group as your material, and the conversation will usually move on from there. Here is how it typically works. Someone shares what their gut check was, and someone else's lands differently. You notice the difference and ask, "Why did that land different for you?" The aim is to understand why each of you lands where you do.

The prompts anchor the conversation, so if discussion stalls go back to a prompt. If it goes somewhere unexpected and interesting, follow it (the prompts are a starting point, and the group can leave them when something better comes up).

The Essay Share

Each meeting includes a round where everyone briefly shares what they did on their essay this week and one thing they are wrestling with. Keep it short (three minutes per person at most) and focus on this week, for instance "This week I mapped my stakeholders and I realized I was missing labor, so I'm wrestling with how deep to go there." Or "I narrowed to AI and content creation, and now I'm wrestling with whether to focus on music or visual art. They raise different ethics issues."

After each person shares, the group responds with reactions and questions (not corrections or edits). For instance, "That labor dimension is interesting. Have you thought about unpaid content moderation?" is a reaction, while "You should include content moderation." is an edit. The purpose is to help each person think more clearly about their own argument. Your group is helping you ask better questions about your essay, and reviewing the draft is not part of the share.

The Collaborative Paragraph

Every meeting closes with one paragraph the group writes together, which captures the shared thinking from the discussion.

What it captures: The paragraph records the questions you explored together, the tensions you noticed, and the insights that emerged, including disagreements. It does not need to reach consensus or a "correct" answer. If the group landed in different places, the paragraph says so, for instance "We noticed tension between transparency and privacy. Some of us prioritize radical disclosure, others see privacy risks to training data contributors."

How to write it: Rotate who types. One person starts writing what the group is saying, and the others add, adjust, and refine. You are aiming for one paragraph of 150 to 200 words.

Purpose: The paragraph is your meeting record, and you submit it on Canvas as evidence that the group met and thought together.

No Instructor Present

There is no instructor in your meetings, and meetings are graded on completion, so there is no reason to perform. The structured prompts and the collaborative paragraph are designed to make productive discussion possible without me in the room. The group has the prompts, a goal (shared understanding), and a deliverable (the paragraph), and that structure is usually enough.

If discussion stalls: Return to the prompts and restart the conversation ("Let me ask the prompt again. Does anyone want to tackle that?"). A stalled discussion often means the group needs to reorient.

If discussion goes somewhere unexpected: Follow it. If someone raises a good, real question the prompts did not ask, spend time there.

If someone dominates: It is the group's job to notice and gently redirect ("I want to hear from others on this."). You can manage the space together.

If You Miss a Meeting

If you cannot attend your group's meeting in a given week, that meeting is graded Complete (Late) instead of Incomplete. It counts toward your late budget, not as a missing labor.

Let your group know in advance if you cannot make it. After the meeting, read the collaborative paragraph and the discussion notes, and let your group know you received them. This keeps you current with what your group discussed.

If your entire group cannot find a common time that week, the same policy applies. Reach out via Discord or email if scheduling is a persistent issue.

Understanding Synthesis and Dialectic

Debate is trying to win. You each present your position, attack the other side, and hope to convince listeners you are right. Dialectic is trying to understand. You start with multiple positions and think together about what each one sees, what it misses, and what is at stake. This may not end in agreement, though it should end with a clearer view of the question.

The usual pattern is thesis, antithesis, synthesis. Someone starts with a position (thesis), and someone else holds a different one (antithesis). Through examination of both, you arrive somewhere neither of you started, something richer and more honest than either position alone (synthesis). The synthesis may keep the disagreement and still be clearer about what is at stake.

A concrete example from this course:

Thesis: "AI hiring tools are more fair than human recruiters because they're consistent. They don't have bad days, don't make snap judgments based on how someone looks, and apply the same criteria every time."

Antithesis: "AI hiring tools encode historical biases at scale and are much harder to challenge than a human decision. At least you can argue with a recruiter. The algorithm just says no."

Synthesis: "AI tools and human recruiters are both likely to be biased, so the more useful question is whether either system can be audited, challenged, and made accountable. Fair hiring requires designing for accountability, since neither humans nor algorithms are neutral."

Each starting position names a real concern, and the synthesis keeps both. Your meetings work the same way. Bring a position you actually hold and see what changes when it meets someone else's.

Meeting Values

These are the values that make a meeting worth showing up to.

Come prepared. You have done your reflection, so you have something to contribute.

Listen actively. When someone else is thinking out loud, actually listen. Try to hear what they are saying before planning your reply.

Ask genuine questions. Do not ask leading questions designed to change their mind. Ask out of real curiosity, for instance "I hear you prioritizing X. What made that matter to you?"

Push back thoughtfully. If you disagree, say so. However, steelman the opposing view first (for instance, "I hear your concern, and that matters. I also think...").

Stay humble about what you know. You are all learning. You might be wrong, and the person you disagree with might see something you are missing.

Lead with empathy. Before you respond to what someone said, check whether you actually understand where they are coming from. If not, ask. You do not have to agree with someone's position to understand what makes it feel true to them. Synthesis requires that understanding, though not agreement.

Extend compassion. People in this group will say things that feel vulnerable. They will admit uncertainty and take positions that surprise you, so treat those moments with care. Meetings typically go better when people feel safe taking intellectual risks.

Write the paragraph together. Everyone contributes, so do not let one person drive it.

AI Use and Meetings

AI use in this course is optional. See the AI Use page for the course's full philosophy.

Your meetings are live collaborative discussions. AI is not a participant in your group. The value of the meeting comes from thinking together in real time, with people who know you and your work. AI use before or after a meeting can either support or shortcut that.

When and how you use AI What you get
AI generates talking points you read from in the meeting Your group ends up responding to AI output, and your own position never enters the discussion.
You use AI to draft the collaborative paragraph after the meeting AI's summary of what you told it, which may miss what the group actually thought.
You use AI to summarize the week's content before the meeting Useful context, as long as you also engaged with the actual source.
You use AI to help articulate your position before the meeting Good preparation. You arrive with a clearer starting point for the discussion.
You use AI to steelman a position before the meeting so you can challenge it more precisely Strong preparation. You know the best version of the argument before you encounter it.
After the meeting, you use AI to help you understand something a peer said that you did not fully grasp Good. You are working through the discussion after the fact.

The test is whether you took part in the conversation yourself. Your group needs your own positions and reactions, including your confusion. Working through ideas live (not knowing what you think yet, being surprised by someone else's perspective, having to respond in the moment) is the purpose of the meeting.

Getting Started

Your first meeting will likely feel awkward, since you do not know each other yet and the group is still settling. This is normal, and most groups settle into a rhythm by Week 3.

Here is what to do in Week 1:

  • Introduce yourselves (name, year, why you are in this course)
  • Read the prompts together
  • Pick one and start: "What's your gut reaction to this?"
  • Listen to what people say
  • Ask follow-up questions
  • Near the end, draft the collaborative paragraph together
