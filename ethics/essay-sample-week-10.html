Why AI Training Data Should Require Authorization, Not Opt-Out

Claude · Computer Ethics

Abstract

Generative AI image tools like Stable Diffusion were trained on datasets containing billions of images scraped from the public web. This paper argues that the opt-out systems AI companies offer do not satisfy their ethical obligations to the people whose content ended up in those datasets. Meaningful consent requires being asked before your work is used, not being given a form after the model is already deployed. The scale of these datasets makes individual opt-in unworkable, but collective licensing provides a framework that already exists. The music industry has handled rights licensing at scale for over a century through organizations like ASCAP and BMI. This paper proposes that federal legislation should require a similar framework for AI training data and replace the current opt-out default.

Introduction

Generative AI image tools let users create images from text descriptions. A user types a prompt and the system generates an image based on patterns it learned during training. These tools are used for commercial illustration, product mockups, and other creative work. Stable Diffusion, one of the most widely used open-source image models, was trained on LAION-5B, a dataset of 5.85 billion image-text pairs assembled by crawling the public web (Schuhmann et al., 2022). The dataset includes images from artist portfolio sites, photography archives, personal photo albums, and online communities built around artists sharing their work.

These tools launched publicly in 2022 and 2023 and adoption grew quickly. Midjourney, DALL-E, and Stable Diffusion all reached public users around the same time. Commercial uses now include advertising, book cover design, game concept art, and product illustration. Professional illustrators and photographers are competing for clients in markets where AI-generated images have become an option.

In early 2023, Getty Images sued Stability AI, claiming that more than 12 million photographs had been scraped from its catalog without a license (Brittain & Kahn, 2023). The lawsuit is one of several that have followed the release of major image generation models. This paper argues that generative AI companies have not met their ethical obligations to the people whose content ended up in training data, and that a collective licensing framework backed by federal legislation should replace the current opt-out default.

Stakeholder Analysis and Ethical Argument

Independent artists and illustrators

Independent artists are the most directly harmed group. Their work was scraped without consent and used to train models that now compete with them in the same markets. The financial harm is real. The harder harm to describe is the style problem. Style is not copyrightable, which means there is no legal protection for an artist whose visual identity was absorbed by a system they never agreed to contribute to. Some image generation tools let users prompt for images in the style of a specific living artist by name. That artist had no say in any of it. I sell digital illustration on Etsy. My work is probably in at least one of these datasets. I want to name that upfront because it affects how I came to this argument.

The obligation AI companies had seems clear. If you are building a product trained on someone's work, you should ask first. A person of integrity would have stopped to weigh that before proceeding.

Ordinary people who posted online

The scale of LAION-5B makes the consent problem much broader than a dispute between AI companies and artists. Stable Diffusion was trained on LAION-5B, a dataset of 5.85 billion image-text pairs scraped from the public web (Schuhmann et al., 2022). LAION-5B is not a curated archive of professional creative work. It is basically everything that was publicly accessible online.

Most of the people whose content is in that dataset are not professional artists or photographers. They are ordinary people who posted personal photos, wrote in forums, or shared things in small online communities. They had no idea any of this was happening.

The consent problem reaches far beyond professional creators. It affects anyone who ever posted anything publicly online.

Writers and other text creators

Writers faced the same structure as visual artists in a different medium. Large language models are trained on text scraped from the web, including copyrighted books, articles, and other creative writing. The Authors Guild filed a class action against OpenAI in September 2023, alleging that OpenAI trained its models on copyrighted books without permission or payment (Authors Guild, 2023). The financial harm is real. Their work was used in a commercial product without compensation. The consent problem is the same. Nobody asked. Writers face competition from tools trained on their own writing, the same dynamic artists face with image generators.

AI companies and engineers

AI companies knew they were creating legal liability when they proceeded with scraping at this scale. The lawsuits that followed were not a surprise. The engineers who built the data collection pipelines made real choices about what to scrape and whether to build any consent mechanism before training. I am going to be a software engineer. Those were ethics decisions whether or not anyone in the room called them that.

People who benefit, including me

The people who benefit most from generative AI tools are the companies that built them and their investors. But the benefit is wider than that, and I need to be honest about my own position. I use Claude for coding help. I used it to debug an assignment earlier this quarter. I benefit from AI tools that probably have similar data sourcing issues in their pipelines. I cannot argue that AI companies caused harm while pretending I am not part of the system. I do not think that cancels the argument. I think the honest version of this paper has to say so.

The central conflict

AI companies want to train on as much data as possible at the lowest cost. Creators and ordinary users want some say before their work is used commercially. These interests do not align. If anything publicly accessible can be used for training by default, creators have no real choice.

The ethical argument

Publicly accessible is not the same as authorized for every use. AI companies point to the fact that their training content was publicly available online, which is true. But accessibility and authorization are two different things.

The comparison AI companies make is that training on public content is like a human artist learning from it. When a human artist is influenced by my work, though, they are not building a product that uses my illustrations as an ingredient. I keep my work. The human artist answers for what they make. In AI training my work enters the pipeline and a commercial product comes out. I had no say in it.

A library makes its books accessible to anyone. That does not give publishers the right to scan and reprint everything in it.

Opt-out arrives too late. The model is already built. Opting out of future training runs does not change what is already inside the model that exists right now.

The opt-out form says it will not use your work in future training runs. It says nothing about the model already trained on your work. The mechanisms don't work anyway. Robots.txt is the standard tool for telling web crawlers not to scrape a site, and The Washington Post found that AI company crawlers were regularly ignoring these files, meaning creators who tried to block their work had no way to confirm it worked (Verma, 2023).

The only point where a creator's choice matters is before the work is used.

Real harm has been documented in legal filings. Getty Images sued Stability AI in early 2023, alleging that Stability AI scraped over 12 million photographs from Getty's catalog without authorization. AI-generated outputs sometimes bore distorted versions of the Getty watermark, visible evidence that the model absorbed images with the watermark still in them (Brittain & Kahn, 2023).

The Authors Guild filed a class action against OpenAI in 2023, alleging that OpenAI trained its language models on copyrighted books without the authors' permission (Authors Guild, 2023).

Two industries, same structure. Real people experienced real harm, and no adequate remedy exists under the current system.

Steelman and response

The strongest argument against my position is scale. You cannot get individual consent from every person whose content ended up in LAION-5B. There are billions of items from millions of people. Individual opt-in is not workable at this scale.

But that does not eliminate the obligation. It means the mechanism has to be something other than individual agreements. The music industry faced a similar problem and built collective licensing to deal with it. Hard logistics do not cancel the obligation.

There is also the human learning comparison. AI companies argue that training on publicly available content is the same as a human artist learning from publicly available work. When a human artist is influenced by my work, they are not building a product that uses my illustrations as a direct input. I keep my work. The human artist answers for what they make. The comparison does not hold.

Policy Proposal

Collective licensing is a solution that already exists and works at scale. ASCAP and BMI have administered licensing for hundreds of thousands of composers for over a century, handling millions of uses annually without individual negotiation (U.S. Copyright Office, 2023). Radio stations don't call every songwriter. Rights are aggregated and compensation flows through the system.

Requiring pre-consent for AI training is not practical at the scale of these datasets. Billions of works from millions of people cannot be individually negotiated for. But that means individual consent is unworkable, not that consent itself is impossible.

The Copyright Office has identified collective licensing as a potential framework for AI training data. Making this work requires legislation to flip the default. Authorization must come before training, not after. The structure already exists in the music industry.

Congress would need to pass the legislation. The Copyright Office already handles some collective licensing functions and is the most logical agency to oversee a new framework, possibly with a dedicated licensing board established by the legislation.

AI companies would pay licensing fees into the collective fund. Those costs would be passed on to users through subscription prices or API pricing. Radio stations pay ASCAP and BMI fees as part of their operating costs. The same principle applies here.

This proposal would make AI development more expensive and slower. Smaller companies and open-source projects would face real challenges with collective licensing fees that large companies could absorb more easily. That is a real cost.

There are also international complications. LAION-5B contains content from creators around the world. A U.S. collective licensing law would not automatically cover international content. International agreements would need to follow.

How to calculate distributions is not a solved problem. In music, plays can be tracked to figure out what each rights holder is owed. For training data it is not clear how to measure how much any individual piece of content influenced a model. Smaller creators might end up with very small distributions. This needs to be designed carefully, not treated as a reason to abandon the framework.

Conclusion

Generative AI companies trained their models on content scraped from the public web without the permission of the people who created or posted it. The opt-out systems they offer do not fix this because they come after the model is already built, and because the evidence shows those mechanisms often do not function reliably. The problem is not limited to professional artists. It reaches anyone who has ever posted anything publicly online.

Collective licensing already exists as a tested framework. The music industry has used it for over a century. The Copyright Office has identified it as a potential approach for AI training data. What is needed is legislation to make it mandatory and flip the default from opt-out to authorization before use.

I came into this paper as someone with a stake in it. I sell digital illustration and I use Claude to help with coursework. I do not think those positions cancel each other out. I think they both belong in an honest account of the problem. If you end up working in software, the decisions about what data to collect and how to handle it will come up. The people who built these training datasets made those decisions too. They just did not call them ethics decisions. That is the part that needs to change.
