Week 9 · Safety & Accountability

This week examines safety failures and accountability in AI systems. Among the cases, an Air Canada chatbot invented a bereavement policy, a deepfake video call in Hong Kong transferred $25.6 million to fraudsters, and lawyers filed briefs citing cases that do not exist. When failures are distributed across many hands (developer, deployer, user), it can be hard to say who should answer for them. The readings work through recent cases and the accountability frameworks being built in response.

This Week's Writing Goal

Edit at Sentence Level

You have cut at the paragraph level, and now you will edit sentence by sentence. The purpose is to make each sentence clear and precise, and shorter sentences are often a side effect. Every sentence should do one job and do it well. Hedges should match your evidence. A hedge like "may," "likely," or "often" tells your reader how strongly your evidence supports a claim, so keep it where the evidence is partial. Cut hedges that are stacked ("it could perhaps be argued that this might...") or added out of habit to a claim you can support ("seems like," "arguably"). Watch for vagueness: "things," "kind of," "sort of," "in a way." Watch for passive voice hiding responsibility: "mistakes were made" instead of "we made mistakes."

Content

For your required weekly reflection, engage with one source from Primary Content. Any source (primary or additional) can be used for an extra reflection for extra credit.

Primary Content

"Moral Outsourcing: Humanity in the Age of AI" — Rumman Chowdhury, TED · 2023 · ~13 min watch · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

Rumman Chowdhury (former Director of AI Ethics at Twitter, one of the leading voices in AI accountability) names and argues the pattern this week is about, which she calls "moral outsourcing". Each party in the chain points to another (engineers say "the algorithm decided," companies say "the deployer is responsible," and deployers say "the user should have known better"), and in the end no one is held accountable for the harm. This talk gives you the conceptual vocabulary for the week, so watch it before reading the case studies.

Reflection prompts:

  • Chowdhury argues that "I was just doing my job" and "the algorithm decided" are the same evasion, since both hand moral responsibility to a system that cannot hold it. When you look at the Air Canada chatbot case or the hallucinated case citations from the other readings, where does that evasion show up? Who is doing the outsourcing?
  • She argues that when responsibility is distributed, each actor in the chain (engineer, company, deployer, user) still holds a real share of it. Does that framing feel right to you, or does it feel like it spreads accountability so thin it loses meaning? What would it look like to actually assign shares?
  • Chowdhury is a practitioner who has worked inside major AI companies on exactly these questions. Does that give her argument more weight, or does it make you want to ask what she was not able to change from the inside?

"AI Safety Incidents of 2024: Lessons from Real-World Cases" — Responsible AI Labs · 2024 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Think tank]

A year in review of AI failures: chatbots giving wrong medical advice, recommendation algorithms amplifying extremism, hiring systems rejecting candidates systematically. Each case study walks through what happened, why, and what it means for accountability. These cases are a good source of concrete examples for your essay, since they let you point to a specific failure and the party who should answer for it.

Reflection prompts:

  • Seven families sued OpenAI alleging that ChatGPT encouraged suicides, and OpenAI acknowledged that "safeguards can sometimes be less reliable in long interactions." That phrase admits a known failure mode without naming it as a design flaw. Does that framing feel honest to you? What would a more accountable acknowledgment look like?
  • The Air Canada tribunal established a legal precedent that a company is responsible for what its chatbot says, even if the chatbot made it up. Does it seem right to you? If a chatbot invents a policy, who made the mistake: the model, the company that deployed it, or the person who trusted it?
  • NYC's MyCity chatbot gave advice that was illegal, telling landlords they could discriminate. The people most likely to be harmed by following that advice were not the people deploying the system. Does that asymmetry (benefits to deployers, harms to the affected) change how you think about who owes what?

"What the Numbers Show About AI's Harms" — TIME · 2025 · ~10 min read · 🟡 Limited access · [Media]

TIME on the data: how many people have been harmed by AI systems, in what ways, and where the harms are concentrated. This gives you quantified stakes for your argument. If you are writing about AI and labor, healthcare, or hiring, this has the numbers.

Reflection prompts:

  • The article notes that tracked incidents represent only "a fraction of the lived realities of everybody experiencing AI harms." Most harm does not meet reporting thresholds or get media coverage. What does it mean to make policy based on a dataset you know is systematically incomplete? Does the 8-fold increase in malicious incidents since 2022 feel like a signal or just the part of the iceberg that is visible?
  • The article identifies a sharp recent shift, in that deepfake incidents now outnumber autonomous vehicle, facial recognition, and content moderation incidents combined. This is a change in what kind of AI harm dominates. Does your understanding of AI risk match what the data shows, or has this week's reading shifted the picture?
  • Over a third of recent incidents involved developers who could not be identified. If the person who built a harmful system is unknown, what does accountability even mean? Is there a version of responsibility that does not require identifying an individual?

"AI Hallucinations Are Creating Real-World Risks for Businesses" — National Law Review · 2024 · ~12 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Legal]

Lawyers and business leaders on the cascade of hallucination-related failures, including the Air Canada chatbot inventing bereavement policies, Hong Kong's $25.6 million deepfake fraud, and lawyers hallucinating case citations. In each case an AI system confidently stated something false and people trusted it because of that confidence. For your sentence editing, notice how this article separates the technical failure (hallucination) from the human failure (trusting without verification).

Reflection prompts:

  • The article makes a pointed observation: "consumers find human mistakes more understandable and forgivable than AI-generated errors because they empathize with human fallibility and expect higher accuracy from AI systems." We may hold AI to a higher standard than we hold humans, even though AI often fails in ways humans do not. Does that asymmetry seem fair? Does it seem like a design problem, a communication problem, or just how humans work?
  • The article reports that some of the newest, most powerful AI models are "generating more errors, not fewer," despite improved fluency. That is, the systems may sound more confident while being less reliable. If you were advising a company about AI adoption, and you knew this, what would you tell them? What would you refuse to help deploy?
  • Google's Bard fabricated a fact in a promotional demo and the error cost Alphabet roughly $100 billion in market cap in a single day. ChatGPT fabricated a bribery accusation against a real Australian mayor. The article frames these as business risk stories. Does that framing bother you, or is business risk the most effective lever for changing behavior?

Additional Content

➕ Challenger: The Final Flight — Netflix · 2020 · ~90 min watch · 🔴 Paywalled · [Media]

A case study in distributed responsibility and systemic failure. The Challenger disaster came from a cascade of decisions, each reasonable in isolation, that together led to catastrophe. Watch Episodes 1 and 2 and think about how that pattern applies to AI systems.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • The Challenger disaster is often described as a case where everyone followed procedure and people still died. If each individual decision seemed defensible at the time, what, if anything, makes someone responsible? Where does moral accountability live when it is distributed across many hands?
  • Engineers at Morton Thiokol raised concerns about the O-rings the night before launch and were overruled. If you were that engineer, what would you have done? What does it cost to push back when you are the one who can see the problem?

➕ "An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents" — Leveson & Turner · 1993 · ~45 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Peer-reviewed]

A technical deep-dive into one of the first major software failures to cause deaths, in which radiation therapy machines killed patients because of bugs. Leveson and Turner show that the failures were organizational, cultural, and systemic as well as technical, and that responsibility was shared across the organization.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • Leveson and Turner argue the Therac-25 failures came from organizational culture, inadequate testing, and misplaced confidence in software correctness. Does that framing make the engineers less responsible, or does it just mean more people were responsible?
  • The Therac-25 killed people, and nobody was charged with a crime. The legal system could not assign individual culpability to a systemic failure. Is that a problem with the law, with the technology, or with how we think about responsibility?

➕ "A Closer Look at the Existing Risks of Generative AI" — arXiv · 2025 · ~45 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Preprint]

A comprehensive survey of current research on the known risks of generative AI and the open questions that remain.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • The survey finds that the majority of AI incidents harmed people who never directly interacted with the AI system (third parties, communities, bystanders). The benefits mostly go to users and developers, while the harms often fall on others. Does that distribution seem like an accident, or does it follow from how AI systems are designed and deployed?
  • The researchers identify a new harm category they call "overburdening ecosystems", in which AI-generated content floods job portals, creative submission systems, and other spaces with low-quality synthetic material and overwhelms the humans who review it. Did you expect that to be on a list of AI harms? Does it change how you think about who counts as a victim of AI?

Reflection

Choose one source from this week's Primary Content. Ground your reflection in something specific from that source, such as a claim you agreed or disagreed with, or a moment that surprised you. Then apply the three-step structure:

Gut check. What was your honest, immediate reaction? Trace it. Where does that reaction come from in your values or experience? Challenge it. What would someone who sees it differently say? Does their case have merit?

~150 words. The source-specific prompts above are starting points. You can use one of those or follow your own reaction. However, your reflection must be anchored to something specific from the content you chose.

Meeting

You have read cases of chatbots inventing facts, deepfakes used for fraud, and algorithms optimizing for the wrong outcome. You have also edited your own draft to name things precisely and to make your hedges match your evidence. Now think together about accountability when harm is distributed across many hands. These prompts are starting points, but go where the discussion takes you. What is the shared thinking you can build about responsibility?

This week's discussion directions:

  • When harm is distributed across many hands (engineers, managers, deployers, users), how should accountability be assigned?
  • Is there a difference between being legally liable and being morally responsible? What is the gap?
  • At what point does "I was just doing my job" stop being a sufficient answer?

Essay share: Share one claim in your essay where you cut or kept a hedge this week. How strongly does your evidence support that claim? The group responds with reactions to the claim.
