Week 1 · Introduction to Ethical Reasoning

This week introduces the ethical reasoning framework that runs through the course. Three questions give you a structure for analyzing the cases you will study. They are: who is harmed, what are our obligations, and what would a person of integrity do. You will apply these questions to generative AI, since CS professionals build these systems and their decisions affect large numbers of people.

This Week's Writing Goal

Brainstorming

You are starting a living essay that will grow across nine weeks. This week the job is to generate material, and the answer can come later. You will apply the three toolkit questions to a specific case of generative AI and then use AI as a thinking partner to push your brainstorm further. The goal is range and quantity, since exploring more directions now will likely give you more to ground your essay in later.

Content

For your required weekly reflection you will engage with one source from Primary Content. Any source, primary or additional, can be used for an extra reflection for extra credit.

Primary Content

Toby Walsh: AI and Ethics — TED · 2018 · ~10 min watch · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

In this foundational talk, computer scientist Toby Walsh explains why AI systems need ethical reasoning and what happens when they lack it. He covers real cases where AI made decisions that seemed reasonable in code but caused harm in the world. It is a strong introduction to why ethical questions come up in ordinary CS work.

Reflection prompts:

  • Walsh's central claim is that AI creates no new ethical problems, and that it gives us new and more powerful ways to commit old ones. Faking an identity, deceiving people, and manipulating outcomes have always been wrong. Does that framing land for you, or does AI seem different in kind as well as in scale? Where does your instinct come from?
  • Think of a specific AI capability that concerns you, such as deepfakes, impersonation bots, or automated scams. If Walsh is right that the underlying wrong is the same as it has always been, does that make the ethical question simpler or more complicated? What does applying existing ethics to a new tool actually require of the person doing the applying?
  • AI operates at a scale and with a level of autonomy that human actors never could. A con artist can deceive dozens of people, while an AI system can deceive millions at once without any single person making each choice. What does that change about how we apply ethics? Do scale and autonomy require new thinking, or more careful application of what we already have?

"'Godfather of AI' Geoffrey Hinton on the dangers AI poses to society" — PBS NewsHour · 2023 · ~10 min watch · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

Hinton, one of the pioneers of modern AI, discusses the real risks AI systems pose and why the people building them need to think carefully about consequences. Note: this talk focuses on safety risk (ethical reasoning in general gets less attention). If you watch it, pair it with at least one of the readings to ground the three toolkit questions.

Reflection prompts:

  • Hinton compares the relationship between humans and a superintelligent AI to the relationship between an adult and a two-year-old, where the adult can easily manipulate the child into choices that serve the adult's interests. When you heard that analogy, what was your gut reaction? Does it feel alarmist, or does it clarify something real?
  • Hinton argues that an advanced AI pursuing any goal will develop "get more control, get more power" as a useful subgoal. On his account control helps it achieve any other goal, so no evil intent is needed. Does that logic hold for you? What does it mean for how we think about the systems we build?
  • Hinton spent decades building the foundations of modern AI and now says he regrets it. He is warning about fake news, political polarization, and job losses and growing inequality, harms that are likely already happening. What is your honest reaction to hearing this from someone who helped create the technology?

Additional Content

➕ "Top AI ethics and policy issues of 2025 and what to expect in 2026" — AIhub · 2026 · ~10 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Blog]

This is a current overview of the ethical landscape in AI. It covers where the tensions are, what is being debated, and what decisions are being made.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • The article describes a $5.3 billion AI impersonation scam targeting fans of a celebrity, with fake concert tickets and fake VIP experiences run through AI-generated personas. At what point does a technology become a tool for harm? Who bears responsibility there?
  • The article notes that the US rolled back Biden-era AI safety rules while the EU moved toward enforcement. The rules governing AI development may now differ a great deal depending on where you are. Does it concern you that the company building your tools might operate in a regulatory environment with fewer protections than you would want? Why or why not?
  • The article points out that a single Google Gemini prompt emits 0.03g CO₂ and consumes water, and this scales to trillions of queries. This was likely not on most people's ethical radar a few years ago. What other hidden costs of the tools you use might you not be thinking about?

➕ "Amazon Scrapped a Secret AI Recruiting Tool That Showed Bias Against Women" — Jeffrey Dastin, Reuters · 2018 · ~8 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

This is a specific, close case of an AI system built with good intentions that ended up encoding and amplifying bias. Read this after you have brainstormed, since it will help you see how the three toolkit questions apply to a real scenario.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • Amazon's recruiting tool was trained on resumes submitted over a decade, and those resumes reflected a male-dominated field. The tool learned to penalize resumes that included the word "women's", as in "women's chess club". Nobody programmed that rule in. What does it mean to be responsible for an outcome your system produced without your explicit instruction?
  • Amazon eventually scrapped the tool, but it had already been in use. If you were an engineer on that team and learned what it was doing, what would you do? What would make that easy or hard?
  • The system was encoding historical discrimination. That is, it learned to repeat past hiring decisions. What does that tell us about the limits of using data to make decisions about people?

Reflection

Choose one source from this week's Primary Content. Ground your reflection in something specific from that source, such as a claim you agreed or disagreed with, or a moment that surprised you. Then apply the three-step structure:

Gut check. What was your honest, immediate reaction? Trace it. Where does that reaction come from in your values or experience? Challenge it. What would someone who sees it differently say? Does their case have merit?

Write about 150 words. The source-specific prompts above are starting points. You may use one of those or follow your own reaction. Make sure your reflection is anchored to something specific from the content you chose.

Meeting

Use these prompts as starting points and go where the discussion takes you. The goal is to build understanding together, so listen for the values underneath what people are saying.

This week's discussion directions:

  • What concerns you most about gen AI right now?

  • What is your gut reaction to the idea that the tools you build as a CS professional could cause harm you did not intend?

  • Does ethical responsibility end when you hand off your code, or does it follow the system into the world?

Essay share: Each person briefly shares the case of gen AI they chose to brainstorm about and one thing they found themselves wrestling with. The group responds with reactions and questions. Close with one collaborative paragraph capturing what the group is thinking about ethics and AI.
