Week 4 · Intellectual Property & Ownership

This week covers the intellectual property questions that generative AI has opened up. These questions include training on copyrighted work without permission, generating outputs that resemble original creative work, and competing with the artists and writers whose work trained the model. Courts have been ruling on them since 2023, and the law is still unsettled. The readings walk through the major cases and what each one established.

This Week's Writing Goal

Stakeholder Analysis (Deepening)

This week you are moving from a rough stakeholder map to a full analysis. For each group affected by your essay topic, you will apply the three toolkit questions directly: who is harmed, what are we obliged to do, and what would integrity require? You will also develop steelmanning, which means learning to articulate the strongest case against your own position before you argue it down. This analysis will likely become the core of your paper.

Content

For your required weekly reflection you will engage with one source from Primary Content. Any source, primary or additional, can be used for an extra reflection for extra credit.

Primary Content

A.I. Versus The Law — LegalEagle · 2023 · ~20 min watch · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

LegalEagle walks through the first major AI copyright lawsuits: Andersen v. Stability AI and Getty Images v. Stability AI. He covers what the artists and Getty are actually claiming, what fair use means in this context, and why the legal questions around AI image generators are harder to resolve than they look. This is accessible legal analysis from someone who explains law for a general audience, and it is a useful foundation for everything else this week.

Reflection prompts:

  • Getty Images sued Stability AI alleging their copyrighted images were used to train the model without a license, and that outputs sometimes reproduce Getty's watermarks. Artists in Andersen v. Stability AI argued their distinctive styles were captured at scale. Copyright does not protect style, but it does protect specific expression. Where do you think the line is? Does it matter to you whether a company profits from work it did not license?
  • The video explains the fair use defense: courts weigh whether the use is transformative, whether it competes with the original, and other factors. AI companies argued that training is transformative because the model learns from work without reproducing it. Does that argument feel convincing to you, or does it feel like a legal technicality that misses something important about what is actually happening?
  • LegalEagle notes that existing copyright law was not written with AI training in mind. Courts are being asked to apply 20th-century frameworks to 21st-century technology. If you were on a legislative committee tasked with updating copyright law for generative AI, what would you want the new law to do, and whose interests would you prioritize?

AI Lawsuit Developments in 2024: A Year in Review — Copyright Alliance · 2024 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Think tank]

A clear, current summary of major AI copyright lawsuits filed in 2024, including New York Times v. OpenAI and Getty Images v. Stability AI. It lists who is suing in each case and what they allege.

Reflection prompts:

  • In New York Times v. Microsoft and OpenAI, the Times alleges that ChatGPT was trained on journalistic work and can now produce near-verbatim reproductions of articles, potentially letting users bypass the paywall. OpenAI has argued fair use. When you use an AI tool to summarize a news article you did not pay for, who do you think bears responsibility: you, the AI company, or someone else?
  • The article describes how DMCA copyright management information (CMI) removal claims kept getting dismissed because courts imposed a "double scienter" standard, meaning plaintiffs had to prove the company knew it was removing copyright info AND knew that would enable infringement. When legal standards make it nearly impossible to prove harm even when harm is visible, what does that tell you about how well copyright law was designed to protect creators?
  • The Andersen v. Stability AI case involves artists suing over their work being used to train image generators. A 2024 ruling rejected the argument that AI only copies "statistical representations," establishing that past copyright cases might not apply cleanly to AI. Does it matter to you whether a model trained on someone's art is legally infringing, or do you have a separate moral intuition about it?

AI and the Visual Arts: The Case for Copyright Protection — Brookings Institution · 2025 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Think tank]

A policy-focused argument for why copyright protections matter specifically for visual artists in the age of generative AI, presenting one perspective on the rights and harms at stake.

Reflection prompts:

  • The article describes a contradiction: U.S. copyright law says AI-generated images cannot be copyrighted because they lack a human author, yet an AI artwork titled "Machine Hallucinations" sold for over $270,000 at Christie's. Does it bother you that an AI company can profit from a work that no one legally owns, while the human artists whose work trained the model have no claim on that value?
  • Over 6,500 artists signed a letter calling AI companies' use of their work "mass theft." The Brookings authors recommend creating a new copyright category for human-AI collaborative works. Does a new legal category feel like the right response, or does it feel like it is solving the wrong problem?
  • ChatGPT's Studio Ghibli-style image feature reached 15 million weekly active users. The Brookings article frames this as a market harm to visual artists, since their style is now available to anyone on demand and for free. How do you weigh that harm against the value users get from the feature? Who should decide?

Generative AI Lawsuits Timeline — Sustainable Tech Partner · updated 2025 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

A chronological overview of major AI copyright litigation from 2023 to 2025. Use this to ground yourself in what is actually happening in courts right now.

Reflection prompts:

  • In December 2025, Anthropic reached a $1.5 billion class action settlement with authors and publishers over training data. Music publishers then filed a $3.1 billion lawsuit alleging "Anthropic built Claude AI on a foundation of torrented piracy." When you read that the AI tools you may already use were built partly on content taken without permission, what is your reaction? Does the size of the settlement change how you think about the harm?
  • A Munich Regional Court ruled in November 2025 that OpenAI violated German copyright law, a different outcome than what U.S. courts have been trending toward. When different countries reach different legal conclusions about the same technology, who do you think should set the standard? Does it matter to you whether the law is U.S.-centric?
  • The timeline shows lawsuits from individual artists, major news publishers, music companies, and authors, all making the same basic claim: their work was taken without consent to train AI that now competes with them. If you were building a model and had the choice between training on consented data (more expensive, slower) or scraped data (cheaper, faster), what would you do?

Additional Content

➕ The Internet's Own Boy — 2014 · ~105 min watch · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

The documentary about Aaron Swartz and open access to knowledge. It provides historical context for the broader question of what should be openly accessible and who decides.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • Aaron Swartz was prosecuted under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act for downloading millions of academic journal articles from MIT's network, facing up to 35 years in prison. He believed publicly funded research should be publicly accessible. Do you think what he did was wrong? Does the severity of the prosecution change your answer?
  • The documentary raises the question of who controls access to knowledge: corporations, governments, institutions, or individuals. In the context of AI training on web-scraped data, if Swartz is right that information should be free to access, does that extend to using it to train commercial AI products? Where does your intuition about "open access" run out?

➕ Guerrilla Open Access Manifesto — Aaron Swartz · 2008 · ~5 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Media]

Swartz's argument for why information should be free, which represents one end of the spectrum on ownership and access. It is a short, provocative read.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • Swartz writes: "sharing isn't immoral — it's a moral imperative." He rejects the legal/illegal distinction entirely, arguing that "there is no justice in following unjust laws." Does that argument land for you, or does it feel like it is rationalizing something self-serving? What is your gut reaction to someone who frames unauthorized downloading as civil disobedience?
  • Swartz specifically names Reed Elsevier and frames academic publishers as extracting enormous fees from research that scholars produce for free and that is often publicly funded. He calls it "private theft of public culture." If you accept that framing, even partially, what does it do to the AI training data debate? Who is stealing from whom?

➕ AI's Copyright Dilemma: Recent Lawsuits and Implications — Darrow AI · 2025 · ~15 min read · 🟢 Free access · [Legal]

A legal analysis of key AI copyright cases and what each one reveals about the future of AI training and fair use law.

Extra reflection prompts:

  • In Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence (February 2025), the first major U.S. ruling on AI training data, the court found that Ross violated copyright because its use of Westlaw headnotes "directly contributed to a competing product," failing the transformative use test. The court rejected the fair use defense. Does "competing with the original" feel like the right line to you? What if the AI product does not directly compete? Does that change the moral calculus?
  • The Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that "prompts alone do not provide sufficient control over the output" to qualify for copyright protection, meaning if you prompt an AI and it generates something, you do not own the result. Does that surprise you? When you use a generative AI tool to create something for a project, what did you assume about who owned the output?
  • The Thaler v. Perlmutter ruling held that human authorship is mandatory for copyright in the U.S., rejecting protection for work created "entirely by an AI." Contrast this with the case where Kent Keirsey got copyright protection for an AI-inpainting project after documenting 35 iterative edits. If human authorship is what creates copyright, what counts as "enough" human involvement? Who benefits from keeping that standard vague?

Reflection

Choose one source from this week's Primary Content. Ground your reflection in something specific from that source, such as a claim you agreed or disagreed with, or a moment that surprised you. Then apply the three-step structure:

Gut check. What was your honest, immediate reaction? Trace it. Where does that reaction come from in your values or experience? Challenge it. What would someone who sees it differently say? Does their case have merit?

Write about 150 words. The source-specific prompts above are starting points. You may use one of those or follow your own reaction. Make sure your reflection is anchored to something specific from the content you chose.

Meeting

Use these prompts as starting points and go where the discussion takes you. The goal is synthesis and dialectic, that is, building understanding together.

This week's discussion directions:

  • Is training an AI on someone's work without permission different from a human learning from that work? Why or why not?
  • Who benefits from open AI models and who is harmed? Are those the same people?
  • What would a fair compensation model for training data actually look like? Who would manage it, and how would it work?
