Extra Labor

What Is Extra Labor?

Extra labor is optional work that boosts your grade beyond the base B (3.1) you earn by completing all required assignments. Completing required work well is sufficient. You do not need extra labor to succeed in this course. Extra labor is for students who want to go deeper on topics that genuinely interest them.

There are two tiers:

  • +0.1 options: lightweight, submit anytime during the quarter
  • +0.3 options: substantial multi-week projects, three-stage submissions due at weeks 4, 7, and 10

You can combine these. For example, completing one +0.3 project and several +0.1 reflections across the quarter. Grade boosts are additive up to the maximum of 4.0.

+0.1 Options — Additional Reflections

An additional reflection earns +0.1 and can be submitted anytime during the quarter.

An additional reflection uses the same gut-check → trace-it → challenge-it structure as your weekly reflections. You apply it to a piece of additional content you chose to engage with. The reflection must be anchored to a specific source: a specific article, video, documentary, podcast episode, essay, or other piece of content.

The same length and tone norms apply as your weekly reflections: about 150 words, in your own voice, honest. However, for extra credit, the genuine engagement must be clearly present. You may be asked to revisit with more depth.

Gut check: What was your immediate, unfiltered reaction? What jumped out, disturbed you, surprised you, or resonated?

Trace it: Where does that reaction come from? What value or experience of yours does this content press on? Be specific. Do not write "I care about fairness." Instead, say what specifically in your background or history makes this land the way it does.

Challenge it: What is the strongest case against your reaction or against the content's argument? Steelman the opposition. Does your position hold up?

There is no formal cap on the number of additional reflections you can submit, but instructor discretion applies. Quality and genuine engagement matter more than volume.

+0.3 Options — Scaffolded Projects

The following options each add +0.3 to your grade and follow the same three-stage submission schedule:

  • Week 4: Proposal, outline, or article list (varies by option)
  • Week 7: Draft
  • Week 10: Final submission

You must complete all three stages to receive credit. Late submissions are accepted at most one week past the deadline and may receive delayed feedback. Drafts more than one week late will not be accepted, which means the full project cannot receive credit. For this reason, plan accordingly.

Labor Journal Essay

A personal reflective essay on your intellectual and ethical development during the course. This is not a summary of what you learned. It is an essay that examines how your thinking changed: about a specific ethical issue, about your own assumptions, about your sense of professional responsibility.

Learning outcomes: Practice reflective writing. Engage in sustained, structured self-examination of your development as an ethical thinker.

How It Works

  1. Select a theme: The theme is the central claim of your essay: what, specifically, changed or developed for you? A good theme is not chosen in advance. It emerges from looking back at your learning logs, weekly reflections, and the course's progression. Once you have a theme, refine it into a focused theme statement that will anchor your introduction and conclusion.

    Default framing is values-focused: how your thinking on an ethical issue evolved, how the course challenged an assumption you brought in, how your sense of professional responsibility changed. If you have a different genuine angle (and a real reason to pursue it), talk to the instructor. The goal is substantive reflection, and the net is wide for engaged students.

    The examples below are strong, ethics-course-specific theme statements. Do not copy them. Write your own based on your own experiences.

    Example Theme Statements
    Researching the labor displacement debate pushed me to confront assumptions I had made about technological neutrality that I hadn't noticed I held.
    Engaging seriously with algorithmic fairness arguments — both for and against — changed how I think about what "objective" even means in the context of systems I might build.
    The course's emphasis on whose perspective is centered in ethical arguments gave me a new framework for evaluating professional decisions I expect to face.
    Writing my policy paper forced me to choose a position on AI accountability that I initially resisted, and tracking that resistance across the quarter taught me something about my own values.
    Through meeting discussions and weekly reflections I developed a more honest understanding of where my ethical intuitions come from and where they are more fragile than I thought.

  2. Select three sub-themes: Sub-themes are the supporting topics you'll explore to develop your main theme. Three is the right number: enough to develop detail, not so many that the essay becomes a list. Each sub-theme should be grounded in a specific experience or moment from the course: a reading that shifted something, a discussion that challenged you, an assignment that was harder than expected for reasons you later understood.

    Within each sub-theme, follow the Gibbs reflective cycle:

    1. Description: What happened?
    2. Feelings: What were your thoughts and feelings at the time?
    3. Evaluation: What was valuable about the experience? What was difficult?
    4. Analysis: What sense can you make of it now?
    5. Conclusion: What did you learn? What might you have done differently?
    6. Action plan: How will you approach similar situations in the future?

    You don't need to mechanically write one sentence per step, but this cycle should shape what you explore in each body paragraph.

  3. Submit your outline (Week 4): A single-page, point-form plan for your essay. Your outline should include: a draft theme statement, three sub-themes, the supporting experience for each sub-theme, and a note on how the Gibbs cycle will apply to each. It is fine at this early point in the quarter to write with alternative themes or developing hypothetical topics. Not all of your relevant experiences have happened yet.

    Time estimate: 1–2 hours

  4. Submit your draft (Week 7): A 3–4 page (900–1200 word) draft following your outline. Edit for content, spelling, and grammar before submitting. The purpose of the draft is to get feedback you can use. You will get the most out of this process if you put real effort into the draft. Feedback on a rough sketch is less useful than feedback on a serious attempt. Late drafts are placed at the back of the grading queue. Drafts more than one week late will not be accepted.

    Effective reflective paragraphs are neither purely abstract ("reflection is important for growth...") nor purely narrative (a play-by-play of what happened). They balance the concrete (a specific moment, a specific text, a specific feeling) with the analytical: what that moment meant, what it revealed, what it changed.

    Time estimate: 6–12 hours

  5. Submit your final essay (Week 10): Revised based on instructor feedback on your draft. Read all feedback carefully. Edit for content and structure, then again for clarity, conciseness, and organization, then again for spelling and grammar. Your final draft should be the highest quality writing you can produce.

    Time estimate: 4–8 hours

Literature Survey

Research and summarize academic literature on a topic of your choosing. This option develops your ability to find, read, and synthesize scholarly sources, regardless of which discipline they come from.

This is a CS ethics course. The relevant academic literature spans many fields: philosophy, law, policy studies, science and technology studies (STS), sociology, communication, journalism studies, public health, and more, as well as ethics-focused CS research. You are not expected to survey technical CS papers. You are expected to find serious academic work on a topic that matters to you within this course's intellectual terrain.

Possible topic areas include: AI policy and governance, algorithmic fairness and auditing, surveillance studies, digital rights and civil liberties, labor displacement and the future of work, misinformation and platform responsibility, data privacy law, accessibility and inclusive design, facial recognition and policing, environmental costs of computing, or any other topic you can connect to the course.

Learning outcomes: Develop research skills. Practice reading academic literature from unfamiliar fields. Practice writing clear, accurate summaries of complex arguments.

Deliverables

  1. Week 4 — Article list: Submit a list of 8 relevant academic articles found through Google Scholar, with brief descriptions of what each article appears to be about and why it's relevant to your topic. At this stage you don't need to have read all of them in depth. You're building your reading list.

    Time estimate: 2–4 hours

  2. Week 7 — Three summaries: Submit three 1-page summaries (one per article) for three articles selected from your list (the instructor may assign which three). Each summary should cover: the article's central argument or question, the methods or evidence used, the main findings or conclusions, and what you found most significant or surprising. Emphasis is on accurate comprehension, not evaluation.

    Time estimate: 6–10 hours

  3. Week 10 — Revised summaries and reflection: Submit your three revised summaries (improved based on instructor feedback) plus a 1-page reflection on what you learned from the survey as a whole. The reflection should address what you now understand about this topic that you didn't before, what the literature agrees on, where it disagrees, and what questions it leaves open.

    Time estimate: 4–8 hours

Public-Facing Explainer Video

A short researched video on your paper topic aimed at a general audience: people who don't study CS or ethics and who may not care about the topic yet.

This is not a presentation of your paper. It is a translation: you take an argument that requires technical or philosophical background to follow, and you make it accessible and compelling to someone who has none of that background. That is a different and harder task than explaining something to your peers. It requires you to think about who your audience is, what they already believe, why they should care, and what you can strip away without losing the argument's force.

Learning outcomes: Practice communicating across expertise levels. Develop the ability to make abstract ethical arguments legible to general audiences. Build public communication skills relevant to professional and civic life.

Deliverables

  1. Week 4 — Proposal: Submit a 1-page (roughly 300 words) proposal describing your topic, your intended audience, the core argument you plan to make, and why you think a general audience should care about it. Include a brief note on format (screen recording, talking head, animated, documentary-style, etc.).

    Time estimate: 1–2 hours

  2. Week 7 — Script and outline: Submit a complete script or detailed outline for your video. If you are doing a narrated video, submit the full narration text. If you are doing something more visual, submit a scene-by-scene outline with notes on what you'll say and show. The goal is to get feedback on your argument's structure and clarity before you invest time in production.

    Time estimate: 5–10 hours

  3. Week 10 — Final video: Submit a finished video, 4–8 minutes long, posted to YouTube (unlisted is fine) or another accessible link. It should be watchable and comprehensible to someone with no background in the topic. Include a brief note (1 paragraph) on what you changed based on feedback from your script stage.

    Time estimate: 8–15 hours

+0.3 Options — Open-Ended

The following options involve real-world engagement instead of a fixed assignment structure. They require reaching out to the instructor first.

Programming Competition

The instructor occasionally runs a programming competition. Participating earns +0.3. Note: this option is not available every quarter. Check with the instructor during the first week of class.

Community Service, Volunteering, and Activism

Documented engagement with a real-world CS ethics issue. Examples that have been accepted in the past:

  • Attending a public meeting on a technology policy question (city council, school board, regulatory hearing)
  • Submitting a formal public comment on an AI regulation proposal
  • Volunteering with a digital equity or digital literacy organization
  • Writing a piece for a public outlet (a local paper, a student publication, a public blog) on a technology ethics issue
  • Participating in organized advocacy or a public campaign on a tech policy question

The common thread: you engaged with the issue in a real-world context, not just in an academic one, and you can document it.

To pursue this option, reach out to the instructor with your idea before you do it (when possible) or as early as you can. Past examples have been accepted. Your own ideas are welcomed. The instructor is open to proposals.

A Few Notes on All of This

Extra labor is for students who find themselves genuinely pulled toward a topic and want structured support for going deeper.

If you're unsure whether an idea qualifies (for any of these options), ask. The instructor would rather hear an early question than a late explanation.

If you want to propose something not listed here, propose it. The net is wide.
