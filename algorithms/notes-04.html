The Master Theorem

The recursion tree and repeated substitution methods both work for any recurrence but require significant algebraic work each time. Many divide-and-conquer algorithms produce recurrences of a common general form. The Master Theorem gives immediate $\Theta$ bounds for this entire class.

First proven by Cormen, Leiserson, and Rivest in Introduction to Algorithms (1990).

Applicable Form

$$T(n) = aT(n/b) + n^d \quad \text{for } n \geq k; \quad T(n) = c \text{ for } n < k $$
  • $a$: number of recursive subproblems (positive integer)
  • $b$: factor by which problem size is reduced ($b > 1$)
  • $n^d$: polynomial cost of the non-recursive work at each level
  • Floors and ceilings are omitted — they do not affect the $\Theta$ bound

Does not apply to: recurrences with $T(n-1)$ or $T(n-k)$ terms, or non-polynomial non-recursive work.

Statement

Compare $d$ to $\log_b a$ (equivalently, compare $b^d$ to $a$):

Case Condition Bound
Case 1 (leaves dominate) $d < \log_b a$ $\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$
Case 2 (balanced) $d = \log_b a$ $\Theta(n^d \log n)$
Case 3 (root dominates) $d > \log_b a$ $\Theta(n^d)$

Rule of thumb: whichever of $d$ and $\log_b a$ is larger determines the exponent. When they are equal, multiply by $\log n$.

Applying the Master Theorem

Procedure:

  1. Read $a$, $b$, and $d$ from the recurrence.
  2. Compute $\log_b a$.
  3. Compare $d$ to $\log_b a$.
  4. State the result from the appropriate case.

Worked Examples

Recurrence $a$ $b$ $d$ $\log_b a$ Case Result
$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + dn$ (mergesort) 2 2 1 1 2 $\Theta(n \log n)$
$T(n) = T(n/2) + d$ (binary search) 1 2 0 0 2 $\Theta(\log n)$
$T(n) = T(n/2) + dn$ 1 2 1 0 3 $\Theta(n)$
$T(n) = 4T(n/2) + dn$ 4 2 1 2 1 $\Theta(n^2)$
$T(n) = 3T(n/2) + dn$ (Karatsuba) 3 2 1 $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ 1 $\Theta(n^{\log_2 3})$
$T(n) = 25T(n/5) + dn^3$ 25 5 3 2 3 $\Theta(n^3)$

The Master Theorem confirms every result proven by induction in Weeks 2–3 in one step each.

Proof Sketch via Recursion Tree

Build the recursion tree level by level:

Level Size # Nodes Cost/Node Total Cost
0 $n$ 1 $n^d$ $n^d$
1 $n/b$ $a$ $(n/b)^d$ $a(n/b)^d$
$i$ $n/b^i$ $a^i$ $(n/b^i)^d$ $a^i(n/b^i)^d = n^d(a/b^d)^i$
$k$ 1 $a^k$ $c$ $c \cdot a^k$

The depth is $k = \log_b n$. The total is:

$$c \cdot a^k + n^d \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \left(\frac{a}{b^d}\right)^i $$

Let $r = a/b^d$. The geometric series behaves differently depending on $r$:

  • $r < 1$ (Case 3, $d > \log_b a$): Series converges to a constant; total $= \Theta(n^d)$.
  • $r = 1$ (Case 2, $d = \log_b a$): Every term equals 1; sum $= k = \log_b n$; total $= \Theta(n^d \log n)$.
  • $r > 1$ (Case 1, $d < \log_b a$): Series is dominated by its largest term; algebra yields $\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$.

The bottom-level term $c \cdot a^k = c \cdot a^{\log_b n} = c \cdot n^{\log_b a}$ (using the log identity from Week 1). This is the dominant term only in Case 1 (when leaves dominate).

Integer Multiplication

Input: Two $n$-bit integers $N$ and $M$.

Output: Their product $N \cdot M$.

Input size: measured in bits — integer $N$ requires $n = \lfloor \log_2 N \rfloor + 1$ bits. This distinction matters: an algorithm that is $O(N)$ in the numeric value is $O(2^n)$ in the input size — exponential.

Grade-School Algorithm

Multiply each bit of one number by the entire other number ($n$ partial products), shift, and add. Both the multiplications and the additions require $O(n)$ work each across $n$ iterations.

Runtime: $\Theta(n^2)$.

In 1952, Andrey Kolmogorov conjectured that $\Theta(n^2)$ was optimal for integer multiplication. He was wrong.

Splitting Integers

Write an $n$-bit integer $N$ as upper and lower halves, each of $n/2$ bits:

$$N = N_u \cdot 2^{n/2} + N_l $$

Similarly $M = M_u \cdot 2^{n/2} + M_l$. Then:

$$N \cdot M = N_u M_u \cdot 2^n + (N_u M_l + N_l M_u) \cdot 2^{n/2} + N_l M_l $$

This involves four sub-multiplications of size $n/2$, plus linear-time additions and bit shifts (multiplying by powers of 2 is a shift — an $O(n)$ operation).

Naive recursive recurrence: $T(n) = 4T(n/2) + dn$.

By Master Theorem: $a=4$, $b=2$, $d=1$; $\log_2 4 = 2 > 1$; Case 1; $T(n) \in \Theta(n^2)$.

No improvement over grade school — recursion alone does not help.

Karatsuba's Algorithm

Anatoly Karatsuba discovered this algorithm in 1960 at approximately age 23.

Key insight: The middle term $N_u M_l + N_l M_u$ can be computed with only one multiplication instead of two.

Define three products:

  • $P = N_u \cdot M_u$
  • $Q = N_l \cdot M_l$
  • $R = (N_u + N_l) \cdot (M_u + M_l)$

Then:

$$R - P - Q = (N_u + N_l)(M_u + M_l) - N_u M_u - N_l M_l = N_u M_l + N_l M_u $$

So the full product is:

$$N \cdot M = P \cdot 2^n + (R - P - Q) \cdot 2^{n/2} + Q $$

Only three recursive multiplications needed, at the cost of a few extra additions (which are $O(n)$ and don't change the $\Theta$ class of the non-recursive work).

Multiply(N, M):
  If N = 0 or M = 0: return 0.
  If N = 1: return M.
  If M = 1: return N.
  Let P = Multiply(N_u, M_u).
  Let Q = Multiply(N_l, M_l).
  Let R = Multiply(N_u + N_l, M_u + M_l).
  Return P*2^n + (R - P - Q)*2^(n/2) + Q.

Recurrence: $T(n) = 3T(n/2) + dn$.

By Master Theorem: $a=3$, $b=2$, $d=1$; $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585 > 1$; Case 1:

$$T(n) \in \Theta(n^{\log_2 3}) \approx \Theta(n^{1.585}) $$

This is the first irrational, non-integer runtime exponent in the course. It is strictly better than $n^2$ for large $n$.

Worked Example

$N = 20$ (binary: $00010100$), $M = 35$ (binary: $00100011$), both padded to 8 bits.

  • $N_u = 0001 = 1$, $N_l = 0100 = 4$
  • $M_u = 0010 = 2$, $M_l = 0011 = 3$
  • $P = 1 \times 2 = 2$, $Q = 4 \times 3 = 12$, $R = 5 \times 5 = 25$
  • $R - P - Q = 25 - 2 - 12 = 11$
  • Result: $2 \cdot 2^8 + 11 \cdot 2^4 + 12 = 512 + 176 + 12 = 700$ ✓

Historical Context

The technique generalizes: splitting into more parts and reducing the number of multiplications further lowers the exponent. The Schönhage–Strassen algorithm achieves approximately $\Theta(n \log n \log \log n)$. Research toward $\Theta(n \log n)$ is ongoing.

Matrix Multiplication

Input: Two $n \times n$ matrices $A$ and $B$.

Output: $C = AB$, an $n \times n$ matrix with $C_{i,j} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{i,k} b_{k,j}$.

Input size: $n$ (the dimension). Integer arithmetic is assumed constant time. Note: matrix multiplication is not commutative — $AB \neq BA$ in general.

Elementary Algorithm

Three nested loops computing each of $n^2$ entries; each entry requires $n$ multiplications.

Runtime: $\Theta(n^3)$.

Naive Recursive Algorithm

Partition each $n \times n$ matrix into four $(n/2) \times (n/2)$ submatrices:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} B_{11} & B_{12} \\ B_{21} & B_{22} \end{pmatrix} $$

Then $C_{11} = A_{11}B_{11} + A_{12}B_{21}$, and similarly for the other three quadrants. This requires 8 submatrix multiplications of size $n/2$, plus $\Theta(n^2)$ additions.

Recurrence: $T(n) = 8T(n/2) + dn^2$.

By Master Theorem: $a=8$, $b=2$, $d=2$; $\log_2 8 = 3 > 2$; Case 1; $T(n) \in \Theta(n^3)$.

No improvement — naive recursion recovers the cubic bound exactly.

Strassen's Algorithm

Volker Strassen (1969) reduced 8 submatrix multiplications to 7 — the same structural idea as Karatsuba, but for matrices. The derivation is considerably less transparent than Karatsuba's.

Strassen's Seven Products

$$M_1 = (A_{11} + A_{22})(B_{11} + B_{22}) $$ $$M_2 = (A_{21} + A_{22})B_{11} $$ $$M_3 = A_{11}(B_{12} - B_{22}) $$ $$M_4 = A_{22}(B_{21} - B_{11}) $$ $$M_5 = (A_{11} + A_{12})B_{22} $$ $$M_6 = (A_{21} - A_{11})(B_{11} + B_{12}) $$ $$M_7 = (A_{12} - A_{22})(B_{21} + B_{22}) $$

The output quadrants:

$$C_{11} = M_1 + M_4 - M_5 + M_7, \quad C_{12} = M_3 + M_5 $$ $$C_{21} = M_2 + M_4, \quad C_{22} = M_1 + M_3 - M_2 + M_6 $$

Recurrence: $T(n) = 7T(n/2) + dn^2$.

By Master Theorem: $a=7$, $b=2$, $d=2$; $\log_2 7 \approx 2.807 > 2$; Case 1:

$$T(n) \in \Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx \Theta(n^{2.807}) $$

Strictly better than $\Theta(n^3)$.

Worked Example (Verification)

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad C = AB = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 4 \\ 24 & 10 \end{pmatrix} $$

Computing Strassen's seven products and combining: $C_{11} = 25 + (-4) - 3 + (-8) = 10$ ✓, $C_{12} = 1 + 3 = 4$ ✓, $C_{21} = 28 + (-4) = 24$ ✓, $C_{22} = 25 + 1 - 28 + 12 = 10$ ✓.

Practical Notes

  • Strassen's algorithm is asymptotically superior but practically slower for small $n$ (large constant factors).
  • The Coppersmith–Winograd algorithm and its descendants push the exponent below $2.373$.
  • For most real use cases, optimized cubic implementations (e.g., BLAS) are used because Strassen's constants are large.

Connections

The Master Theorem is the culmination of the divide-and-conquer analysis arc spanning Weeks 2–4. Every recurrence solved by recursion tree or induction in Week 2 and Week 3 is now a one-line computation.

Karatsuba (integer, 1960) and Strassen (matrix, 1969) share identical strategy: reduce the number of recursive multiplications by one, paying in extra additions. Both are Case 1 of the Master Theorem.

The log identity $a^{\log_b n} = n^{\log_b a}$ from Week 1 is essential for evaluating the leaf-count term $c \cdot a^k = c \cdot n^{\log_b a}$ in the Master Theorem proof sketch.

In Week 6, the design strategy pivots from divide and conquer to dynamic programming. The key difference is that divide and conquer requires non-overlapping subproblems; when subproblems overlap, DP is preferred.
